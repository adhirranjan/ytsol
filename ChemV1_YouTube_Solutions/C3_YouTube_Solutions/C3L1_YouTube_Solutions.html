1Which one has maximum number of molecules? 16 g O₂ / 16 g NO₂ / 4 g N₂ / 32 g H₂Mole concept / moleculesWhich has maximum molecules? (Mole concept)🟡 Similard32 g of H₂
Concept The number of molecules in a sample is fixed by the number of moles present — not by the mass. Two samples of equal mass can hold very different molecule counts because their molar masses differ. Since molecules = n × NA and NA is the same constant for every substance, ranking samples by molecule count is exactly the same as ranking them by moles n. So the whole problem reduces to converting each mass to moles and comparing.
Formula n = m ÷ M: moles = given mass m (g) ÷ molar mass M (g mol⁻¹). M is the grams present in exactly one mole, so dividing mass by M cancels grams and leaves moles. Then molecules = n × NA, with NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹. Molar masses from atomic masses (O=16, N=14, H=1): O₂ = 2×16 = 32; NO₂ = 14 + 2×16 = 46; N₂ = 2×14 = 28; H₂ = 2×1 = 2 g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Compute each molar mass by adding atomic masses:O₂ = 16×2 = 32; NO₂ = 14 + (16×2) = 14 + 32 = 46; N₂ = 14×2 = 28; H₂ = 1×2 = 2 g mol⁻¹.
2. Convert each mass to moles using n = m ÷ M (substitute each pair):
  • 16 g O₂: n = 16 ÷ 32 = 0.500 mol
  • 16 g NO₂: n = 16 ÷ 46 = 0.348 mol
  • 4 g N₂: n = 4 ÷ 28 = 0.143 mol
  • 32 g H₂: n = 32 ÷ 2 = 16.0 mol
3. Rank the moles: 16.0 ≫ 0.500 > 0.348 > 0.143.H₂ has about 32× the moles of the next-highest sample, so it must have the most molecules.
4. Cross-check with an actual count: molecules(H₂) = 16.0 × 6.022×10²³ = 9.64×10²⁴.
 sample     m(g)   M(g/mol)   n = m/M      molecules = n·N_A
 16 g O₂     16      32        0.500 mol    3.01×10²³
 16 g NO₂    16      46        0.348 mol    2.10×10²³
  4 g N₂      4      28        0.143 mol    8.61×10²²
 32 g H₂     32       2       16.0  mol     9.64×10²⁴   <-- MAX
⚡ JEE Trick For any "maximum/minimum number of molecules (or atoms)" MCQ, never multiply by NA — it is a common factor, so just compute m ÷ M for each choice and pick the extreme. Even faster: for comparable masses the species with the smallest molar mass wins, and H₂ (M = 2) is the lightest common molecule, so it wins on sight. Trap: equal masses (16 g appears twice) tempt you to treat them as equal — always divide by the correct molar mass.
Answer: (d) 32 g of H₂.

The question tests whether you can scale the tiny single-particle mass (9.108×10⁻²⁸ g) up by Avogadro's number and then handle the g → mg unit conversion without slipping a power of ten. Statements that quote the right digits with the wrong unit (g instead of mg, or kg) are the designed distractors.
Formula mass of 1 mol e⁻ = me × NA a mole is by definition NA particles, so total mass = (mass of one) × (how many). Data: me = 9.108×10⁻²⁸ g = 9.108×10⁻³¹ kg; NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹; 1 g = 1000 mg.

4. Judge the statements: "1 mole of electrons weighs 0.548 mg" matches the computed value exactly — statement (b) is correct; versions quoting 0.548 g or 5.48×10⁻⁴ mg mis-place the decade and are wrong.

3Correct expression: n = m·N_A / m = N_A / N = n·N_A / m = n/N_AMoles ↔ moleculesConverting between atoms and moles (N = n·N_A)🟡 SimilarcN = n·NA
Concept The bridge between a countable number of particles (atoms, molecules, ions) and the chemist's bookkeeping unit, the mole, is Avogadro's number NA. Because one mole is defined as exactly NA particles, the particle count N of any sample must equal the moles n times how many particles sit in each mole. Getting this relation the right way round — and checking its units — instantly settles which of the four algebraic expressions is valid.
Formula N = n·NA. Here N = number of particles (a pure, dimensionless count); n = amount of substance in mol; NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹ (particles per mole). Multiplying mol by mol⁻¹ cancels the mole unit and leaves a plain number, which is what a count must be. The inverse relation is n = N ÷ NA. Mass m (in g) is a different physical quantity and can never be equated to a count.
Solution
1. State what NA means: it is the number of particles in exactly one mole = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹.This is the definition of the mole, so it is the only fact we need.
2. Scale up from one mole to n moles: particles = (moles) × (particles per mole) = n × NA.If 1 mol holds NA particles, then n mol holds n times as many — direct proportion.
3. So N = n·NA, matching option (c).
4. Eliminate the rest by a dimensional check — a valid equation must have matching units on both sides:
 option           left side   right side               verdict
 N = n·N_A        count       mol × mol⁻¹ = count      ✓ units match
 m = N_A/N        mass (g)    mol⁻¹ / count = mol⁻¹    ✗ g ≠ mol⁻¹
 n = N_A·N        mol         mol⁻¹ × count            ✗ mol ≠ mol⁻¹
 n = m/N_A        mol         g / mol⁻¹ = g·mol        ✗ mol ≠ g·mol
5. Only N = n·NA is dimensionally consistent, confirming (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Every mole↔particle conversion is just "×NA going mole→count, ÷NA going count→mole." You never need to plug in 6.022×10²³ to answer this kind of MCQ — a one-second units check (mol × mol⁻¹ must give a pure number) kills every wrong option. Trap: options that slip mass m into a count formula look symmetric but are dimensional nonsense — mass needs molar mass M, not NA, to reach moles.
Answer: (c) N = n·NA.

Concept A "g-atom" (gram-atom) is the older name for one mole of atoms of an element. The question asks the reverse of the usual count: how many g-atoms (i.e. how many moles of atoms) does a single atom correspond to? Since one mole contains NA atoms, one atom is just 1/NA of a mole. This reciprocal, 1/NA = 1.66×10⁻²⁴, recurs all over the mole concept, so it pays to recognise it on sight.
Formula g-atoms = number of atoms ÷ NA, where NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹ (atoms per g-atom). Dividing a raw atom count by "atoms per mole" converts a count into an amount in moles (g-atoms). For a single atom, "number of atoms" = 1, so g-atoms = 1 ÷ NA.
Solution
1. Write the definition: 1 g-atom = 1 mole of atoms = NA atoms = 6.022×10²³ atoms.This tells us how many atoms make up one whole g-atom.
2. Invert the relation to find the g-atoms in ONE atom: g-atoms = 1 ÷ NA = 1 ÷ 6.022×10²³.If 6.022×10²³ atoms = 1 g-atom, then 1 atom = 1/(6.022×10²³) g-atom.
3. Do the arithmetic: 1 ÷ 6.022×10²³ = (1 ÷ 6.022) × 10⁻²³ = 0.1661 × 10⁻²³ = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g-atom.1 ÷ 6.022 = 0.1661, and shifting one power of ten turns 10⁻²³ into 10⁻²⁴.
  1 g-atom  ───────────────  6.022×10²³ atoms
  ? g-atom  ───────────────  1 atom
  ⇒ ? = 1 / 6.022×10²³ = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g-atom
⚡ JEE Trick "Per single atom" (or per single molecule) of anything = 1/NA of the per-mole value. Commit 1 ÷ 6.022×10²³ = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ to memory — the same number is the mass of 1 amu in grams and appears in dozens of problems. Trap: don't multiply by NA here; a single atom is a tiny fraction of a mole, so the answer must be a small number (10⁻²⁴), not a huge one.
Answer: (b) 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴.

1What is one amu?Atomic mass unitDefine the atomic mass unit (amu)🟢 Exact—
Concept Single atoms are far too light to describe conveniently in grams, so chemistry uses a bespoke unit sized to atoms themselves: the atomic mass unit (amu, also written u). By international agreement it is anchored to the carbon-12 isotope — chosen because ¹²C is stable, abundant and easy to isolate in a mass spectrometer. Every atomic and molecular mass on the periodic table is a ratio to this standard.
Definition 1 amu = (1/12) × mass of one atom of ¹²C taking one-twelfth of ¹²C makes the amu almost exactly the mass of a single proton or neutron, so mass numbers come out near whole numbers. Numerically, 1 amu = 1.66056×10⁻²⁴ g ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg.
Solution
1. State the definition: one amu is defined as exactly one-twelfth of the mass of a single, unbound atom of carbon-12 in its ground state.
2. Derive its gram value from the mole: 12 g of ¹²C contains NA atoms, so mass of one ¹²C atom = 12 ÷ 6.022×10²³ = 1.9927×10⁻²³ g.Gram atomic mass ÷ Avogadro's number always gives the single-atom mass.
3. Take one-twelfth: 1 amu = 1.9927×10⁻²³ ÷ 12 = 1.66056×10⁻²⁴ g = 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg.
4. Note the reciprocal link: 1 amu = 1/NA gram — which is why 1 mole of 1-u particles weighs exactly 1 g.
5. Example of use: mass of an O atom = 16 amu means one oxygen atom is 16× heavier than 1/12 of a ¹²C atom.
 12 g of ¹²C  = 6.022×10²³ atoms
 1 atom ¹²C   = 12/6.022×10²³ = 1.9927×10⁻²³ g
 1 amu        = (1/12) × 1.9927×10⁻²³
              = 1.66056×10⁻²⁴ g ≈ 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember 1 amu = 1/NA gram = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g — this one number converts any "mass of X atoms/molecules" question into plain multiplication. Trap: writing the definition against ordinary carbon (average mass 12.011 u) instead of the isotope ¹²C, or quoting 1/6 or 1/14 of ¹²C — it is exactly one-twelfth of carbon-12.
Answer: 1 amu = (1/12) × mass of one ¹²C atom = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g (≈1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg).
2Explain the term: Molecular massMolecular massMolecular / molar / formula mass🟡 Similar—
Concept The molecular mass is the quantity that tells us how heavy one molecule of a substance is, but expressed on a relative scale rather than in grams. It matters because the mass of a real molecule (about 10⁻²³ g) is far too small to weigh, so chemists compare it to a fixed reference — 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom — and report a convenient pure number. Every mole calculation (moles = mass ÷ molar mass) ultimately rests on this figure.
Key relation molecular mass = Σ (atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule), measured in u (unified atomic mass units), where 1 u = 1/12 × mass of a C-12 atom. Because it is a ratio of one mass to another, it carries no true dimension — "u" simply flags the reference scale.
Solution
1. A molecule is a group of atoms bonded together; its mass is simply the total of the masses of those atoms.Mass is additive — no mass is created or lost when atoms bond.
2. Each atom's mass is already quoted in u relative to C-12, so adding them keeps the answer on the same relative scale.
3. It therefore states how many times heavier one molecule is than 1/12 of a C-12 atom — a relative, dimensionless number.
4. Worked examples:
 molecule   atoms          sum of atomic masses      molecular mass
 H₂O        2 H + 1 O      2(1) + 16 = 2 + 16        = 18 u
 CO₂        1 C + 2 O      12 + 2(16) = 12 + 32      = 44 u
 CH₄        1 C + 4 H      12 + 4(1) = 12 + 4        = 16 u
⚡ JEE Trick "Molecular mass" is only meaningful for species that truly exist as discrete molecules (covalent substances). For ionic solids like NaCl there is no molecule, so use formula mass instead — the arithmetic is identical, only the name changes. Trap: writing grams — molecular mass is in u; attach grams only when you mean the molar mass.
Key point: molecular mass = sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule, a relative value expressed in u (against 1/12 of a C-12 atom).
3What is formula mass of a substance?Formula massMolecular mass vs formula mass🟡 Similar—
Concept Formula mass is the mass term chemists use for substances that do not exist as separate molecules — chiefly ionic compounds. It matters because talking about a "molecule of NaCl" is physically wrong: in a salt crystal every Na⁺ is surrounded by six Cl⁻ (and vice versa) in an endless lattice, so there is no isolated NaCl unit to weigh. Formula mass gives a meaningful average "unit mass" for such solids so that mole calculations still work.
Key relation formula mass = Σ (atomic masses of the atoms in one formula unit), in u. A "formula unit" is the simplest whole-number (empirical) ratio of ions shown by the chemical formula — e.g. one Na⁺ to one Cl⁻ for NaCl. The calculation is identical to molecular mass; only the name differs because there is no real molecule.
Solution
1. Recognise the bonding: ionic compounds form a continuous 3-D array of ions, not discrete molecules.Electrostatic attraction acts in all directions, so the lattice has no natural "molecule" boundary.
2. Take the simplest formula unit given by the empirical formula (NaCl means a 1:1 Na:Cl ratio).
3. Add the atomic masses of the atoms in that unit — for NaCl: Na = 23, Cl = 35.5.
4. Formula mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u.
 substance   type    formula unit    sum of masses        formula mass
 NaCl        ionic   Na + Cl         23 + 35.5            = 58.5 u
 MgCl₂       ionic   Mg + 2 Cl       24 + 2(35.5)         = 95 u
 CaCO₃       ionic   Ca + C + 3 O    40 + 12 + 3(16)      = 100 u
⚡ JEE Trick Metal + non-metal (or any compound built from polyatomic ions like CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻) ⇒ ionic ⇒ say "formula mass," never "molecular mass." The arithmetic is identical; only the terminology changes. Trap: NaCl's 58.5 is a formula mass — an exam that labels it "molecular mass of NaCl" is using loose language you should not copy.
Key point: formula mass = sum of the atomic masses in one formula unit; used for ionic (non-molecular) substances such as NaCl, expressed in u.
4Explain the term molar massMolar massMolecular / molar / formula mass🟡 Similar—
Concept Molar mass is the practical link between something we can measure — mass in grams on a balance — and something we reason with — amount in moles. Molecular/atomic mass (in u) describes a single particle, which is far too light to weigh; molar mass scales that up by Avogadro's number so it applies to a lab-sized sample. This is the quantity that makes the central equation moles = mass ÷ molar mass usable in every stoichiometry problem.
Key relation M = mass of one mole (NA = 6.022×10²³ particles) of a substance, with units g mol⁻¹. Equivalently M = m ÷ n (grams per mole). Numerically M equals the atomic/molecular/formula mass because scaling both the reference (1 amu) and the particle up by NA and by grams keeps the same ratio — only the unit switches from u to g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Start from the particle mass: O₂ has molecular mass 32 u (16+16).
2. One mole is NA such molecules; multiplying a mass of 32 u by NA and converting u→g gives exactly 32 g.1 u = 1/NA g, so NA × (32 u) = 32 g — the NA and the 1/NA cancel, leaving the same number in grams.
3. Therefore molar mass of O₂ = 32 g mol⁻¹ = the mass of 1 mol of O₂.
4. This is why moles = mass ÷ molar mass works for any substance — dividing grams by g mol⁻¹ leaves mol.
 substance   molecular/atomic mass    molar mass       mass of 1 mole
 O₂          32 u                     32 g mol⁻¹       32 g
 H₂O         18 u                     18 g mol⁻¹       18 g
 C (atom)    12 u                     12 g mol⁻¹       12 g
⚡ JEE Trick Molar mass (g mol⁻¹) is numerically identical to the molecular/atomic/formula mass (u) — never recompute, just swap the unit "u" for "g mol⁻¹." Trap: molar mass is a per-mole quantity (g mol⁻¹), not the mass of one particle (that is 10⁻²³ g scale) — keep the two apart.
Key point: molar mass = mass of one mole of a substance = its atomic/molecular/formula mass expressed in g mol⁻¹.

4. Rule out the distractors: it is not an absolute mass in grams (that needs ÷NA), nor the number of atoms, nor a volume.

4What is formula mass used for? (Ionic compounds)Formula mass — ionicMolar mass (covalent) & formula mass (ionic)🟡 SimilarcIonic compounds
Concept The question tests when to use "formula mass" instead of "molecular mass." The deciding factor is whether the substance is built from discrete molecules or from an extended lattice. Covalent compounds (H₂O, CO₂, glucose) exist as separate molecules, so "molecular mass" is meaningful. Ionic compounds are giant 3-D arrays of ions with no isolated molecule, so we describe them by the mass of their simplest repeating formula unit — the formula mass.
Key relation discrete molecules ⇒ molecular mass ; ionic / network solids ⇒ formula mass. Both are computed the same way — sum of atomic masses in the chosen unit, in u — the label just tracks whether a real molecule exists. Naming an ionic solid's mass "molecular" would falsely imply a molecule is present.
Solution
1. Classify the option types: covalent → molecules; ionic → lattices.
2. Covalent substances (H₂O, CO₂) genuinely have molecules → use molecular mass.
3. Ionic compounds (NaCl, MgCl₂) form lattices, not molecules, so we use the empirical formula unit → formula mass.
4. Therefore formula mass is the term reserved for ionic compounds — option (c).

 CO₂         covalent   molecules       molecular mass
 H₂O         covalent   molecules       molecular mass
 NaCl        ionic      lattice         formula mass   ✓
 MgCl₂       ionic      lattice         formula mass   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Metal + non-metal ⇒ ionic ⇒ formula mass. A fast tell: if the compound has a metal cation or a polyatomic ion, use formula mass. Trap: NaCl's 58.5 is a formula mass, not a molecular mass — the numbers are the same, but only one label is correct for an ionic solid.

Formula M(avg) = f₁m₁ + f₂m₂, f = percent ÷ 100. Each isotope's pull on the average equals its fraction times its mass, and the fractions sum to 1. Data: ¹H — 99.985%, mass 1 u; ²H — 0.015%, mass 2 u.
Solution
1. Fractions: f(¹H) = 0.99985; f(²H) = 0.00015. Check: 0.99985 + 0.00015 = 1 ✓.
2. Protium term: 0.99985 × 1 = 0.99985.
3. Deuterium term: 0.00015 × 2 = 0.00030.Twice the fraction, because each ²H atom weighs 2 u.
4. Add: M = 0.99985 + 0.00030 = 1.00015 u.
5. Shortcut check: M = 1 + f₂×(2−1) = 1 + 0.00015 = 1.00015 — the average exceeds 1 by exactly deuterium's fraction, confirming the sum.

 ¹H        0.99985    1      0.99985
 ²H        0.00015    2      0.00030
                             ───────
 average atomic mass         1.00015 u   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For a dominant isotope, write M = m₁ + f₂(m₂ − m₁): start at the abundant mass and add the rare fraction times the mass gap — here 1 + 0.00015×1 = 1.00015 in one step, no long decimals. Trap: converting 0.015% to 0.015 (forgetting ÷100) gives 1.015, and rounding early gives a flat 1.000 — both planted answers. Keep all five decimals until the end.
Answer: 1.00015 u.

Here A states the definition of the amu (1/12 the mass of one C-12 atom) and R states a fact about carbon's isotopic abundance. Both are true independently, so the whole question hinges on whether "C-12 is most abundant" is the reason the amu is defined via C-12's twelfth part.
Formula 1 amu = (1/12) × m(one ¹²C atom) = 1.66×10⁻²⁴ g. This is a definition adopted by international convention (IUPAC, 1961), not a law derived from abundance. Abundance data: ¹²C ≈ 98.9%, ¹³C ≈ 1.1%, ¹⁴C trace.
Solution
1. Test A: by definition, 1 amu (u) = 1/12 of the mass of a single ¹²C atom — A is true.
2. Test R: carbon in nature is ~98.9% ¹²C, far ahead of ¹³C (1.1%) — R is true.
3. Test the link: does abundance cause the definition? The scale is fixed by choosing one agreed reference atom and the divisor 12; ¹²C was adopted mainly for convenience in mass spectrometry and for keeping the new scale close to the old O-16 scale.Had abundance been the criterion, defining the unit via the rarer ¹³C would still have been possible — the definition is a convention, not a consequence.
4. Verdict: R does not logically necessitate A; it is a related but non-explanatory fact.
5. Map to the standard code: both true, R not the correct explanation ⇒ option (b).
