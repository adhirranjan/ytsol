1Which option correctly differentiates diffusion from effusion?Diffusion vs effusionA-DIFEFF🟡 SimilarcDiffusion is random mixing of gases without a hole, while effusion is gas escape through a small orifice
Concept Diffusion and effusion are two different ways a gas spreads, and the exam wants you to keep them apart. Diffusion is the gradual intermingling of one gas into another (or into empty space) driven only by random molecular motion — no barrier and no hole are involved. Effusion is the escape of gas molecules one at a time through a single tiny orifice into a region of lower pressure (or vacuum). Both obey Graham's law, but the physical picture is distinct: diffusion = mixing throughout a volume; effusion = leaking through a pinhole.
Key relation Both processes share the same rate law r ∝ 1/√M (lighter gas is faster), but the setup differs: diffusion needs no hole (random spreading), effusion needs one small hole whose diameter is smaller than the mean free path. The identical √M dependence is why students confuse them — the distinguishing feature is the presence/absence of an orifice, not the maths.
Solution
1. Define diffusion: spontaneous mixing of gases by random motion, spreading through the whole available volume with no orifice required.e.g. perfume filling a room — molecules wander among air molecules.
2. Define effusion: passage of gas through a single small hole into lower pressure, molecules leaving individually.e.g. a slowly deflating tyre through a pinhole.
3. Match to the options: only (c) states "diffusion = random mixing without a hole" and "effusion = escape through a small orifice" — both halves correct.
4. Reject options that swap the two, or that claim a hole is needed for diffusion / no hole for effusion.
 process     needs a hole?   picture                    rate law
 diffusion   no              gases intermix in a volume  r ∝ 1/√M
 effusion    yes (one hole)  escape through an orifice   r ∝ 1/√M
⚡ JEE Trick One-word memory hook: diffusion = mix, effusion = escape. Both follow r ∝ 1/√M, so any option claiming different rate laws is wrong; the real difference is the orifice. Trap: options often reverse the definitions — read which process is tied to the "small hole."
Answer: (c) Diffusion is random mixing of gases without a hole, while effusion is gas escape through a small orifice.
2H₂ diffuses 4× faster than gas X — molar mass of XRate ↔ molar massGas X diffuses four times slower than hydrogen,calculate molecular weight of gas X.🟢 Exactd32 g mol⁻¹
Concept Graham's law says a lighter gas diffuses faster, with the rate scaling as the inverse square root of the molar mass. Here we are told how many times faster H₂ is than an unknown gas X and asked for MX. The single relation r ∝ 1/√M turns that speed ratio directly into a molar-mass ratio — square the speed factor and multiply by the known molar mass of H₂.
Formula r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁), r in (volume or amount)/time, M in g mol⁻¹. Faster gas has smaller M, so the ratio of rates equals the square root of the inverted mass ratio. With MH₂ = 2 g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Write the law with H₂ as gas 1, X as gas 2: rH₂/rX = √(MX/MH₂).
2. Substitute the given speed ratio rH₂/rX = 4: 4 = √(MX/2).
3. Square both sides to remove the root: 4² = MX/2, i.e. 16 = MX/2.Squaring undoes the square root and isolates the mass ratio.
4. Multiply through by 2: MX = 16 × 2 = 32 g mol⁻¹.M = 32 is O₂ — a lighter-than-expected reminder that "4× faster than H₂" is only a modest 32 g mol⁻¹.
 r_H2/r_X = √(M_X/M_H2)
 4        = √(M_X/2)
 16       = M_X/2
 M_X      = 32 g mol⁻¹
⚡ JEE Trick "n times faster than H₂" ⇒ M = n² × 2 (square the factor, multiply by 2). Here 4² × 2 = 32 in one line. Trap: don't forget to square the rate ratio — using 4 directly gives 8, a common wrong answer.
Answer: (d) 32 g mol⁻¹.

So the problem is: find the rate of O₂ from its 50 mL/10 s data, scale it by the Graham's-law factor to get the H₂ rate, then multiply by the same 10 s. Because the time is identical for both, you can even shortcut straight from volume ratio = rate ratio.

Solution
1. Rate of O₂: rO₂ = 50 mL ÷ 10 s = 5 mL s⁻¹.
2. Graham factor: rH₂/rO₂ = √(MO₂/MH₂) = √(32/2) = √16 = 4.O₂ is 16× heavier than H₂, and √16 = 4, so H₂ diffuses 4× faster.
3. Rate of H₂: rH₂ = 4 × 5 = 20 mL s⁻¹.
4. Volume of H₂ in the same 10 s: VH₂ = rH₂ × t = 20 × 10 = 200 mL.Equivalently VH₂ = 4 × 50 mL = 200 mL directly.

 O₂     50      10     5 mL/s
 H₂      ?      10     20 mL/s   4              200 mL
⚡ JEE Trick Same time ⇒ volume ratio = rate ratio = √(M₂/M₁), so just multiply the given volume by the Graham factor (here ×4). No need to find rates in mL/s at all. Trap: put the heavier gas mass on top inside the root for the lighter gas's speed — √(32/2), not √(2/32).

4Gas A (d=1.8) & B (d=7.2); 100 mL A in 20 s — time for BRate ↔ density (time)A-RATEDENS🟡 Similarb40 s
Concept Graham's law can be written with density instead of molar mass, because for gases M ∝ d: r ∝ 1/√d. Here the two gases diffuse the same volume (100 mL), so their times are inversely proportional to their rates — the denser gas is slower and takes longer. Find gas A's rate, scale by the density factor to get B's rate, then convert to B's time for the same 100 mL.
Formula r ∝ 1/√d, and for equal volume t ∝ 1/r ∝ √d, so tB/tA = √(dB/dA). Denser gas ⇒ slower ⇒ larger time; the time ratio is the square root of the density ratio. dA = 1.8, dB = 7.2.
Solution
1. Density ratio: dB/dA = 7.2 ÷ 1.8 = 4.B is exactly four times as dense as A.
2. Time ratio for the same 100 mL: tB/tA = √(dB/dA) = √4 = 2.
3. Given tA = 20 s, so tB = 2 × 20 = 40 s.Twice as dense-by-4 means √4 = 2× the time, i.e. B is half as fast.
 gas   d     V(mL)   t(s)    t_B/t_A = √(d_B/d_A)
 A     1.8    100     20
 B     7.2    100     40     √(7.2/1.8) = √4 = 2 → 2×20 = 40
⚡ JEE Trick Same volume ⇒ time ∝ √d: just multiply the known time by √(dheavy/dlight). Denser gas always takes longer, so if your answer for the heavier gas is smaller, you inverted the ratio. Trap: rate ∝ 1/√d but time ∝ +√d — flip the proportionality when you switch from rate to time.
Answer: (b) 40 s.
5Gases A & B densities 9:4; 1 L A in 36 s — time for 0.5 L BRate ↔ density (time)A-RATEDENS🟡 Similara12 s
Concept This is a density-form Graham's-law problem, but with a twist: the two gases diffuse different volumes (1 L of A, only 0.5 L of B), so you cannot use the shortcut "time ∝ √d." You must find each gas's actual rate (volume ÷ time), relate the rates by r ∝ 1/√d, then use B's rate to get the time for its specified 0.5 L.
Formula r = V/t, rA/rB = √(dB/dA), then tB = VB/rB. Because the volumes differ, keep rate and volume separate — never equate times to a bare √d ratio here. dA:dB = 9:4.
Solution
1. Rate of A: rA = 1 L ÷ 36 s = 1/36 L s⁻¹.
2. Rate ratio: rA/rB = √(dB/dA) = √(4/9) = 2/3.A is lighter (d=4 side)? No — dA=9 is denser, so A is slower; rA/rB = 2/3 < 1 confirms A is the slower gas.
3. Solve for rB: rB = rA × (3/2) = (1/36) × (3/2) = 3/72 = 1/24 L s⁻¹.
4. Time for 0.5 L of B: tB = VB/rB = 0.5 ÷ (1/24) = 0.5 × 24 = 12 s.B is faster and only half the volume is required, so its time is well under A's 36 s.
 gas   d    V(L)    r = V/t          t
 A     9    1.0     1/36 L/s         36 s
 B     4    0.5     1/24 L/s         0.5 ÷ (1/24) = 12 s
 (r_A/r_B = √(4/9) = 2/3 ⇒ r_B = 3/2 · r_A)
⚡ JEE Trick When the two volumes are different, don't shortcut with √d on the times — compute rates first, then time = volume ÷ rate. Here the denser gas A (d=9) is slower, yet B needs less time because it is both faster and asked for half the volume. Trap: blindly writing tB = 36 × √(4/9) ignores the 1 L vs 0.5 L difference.
Answer: (a) 12 s.

1O₂ density 1.3 kg m⁻³ at 101.3 kPa — density at 2.24×10⁵ N m⁻² (const T)Boyle's law (P–d)A-BOYLE-D🟡 Similar—
Concept At constant temperature a fixed mass of gas obeys Boyle's law, PV = constant. Since density d = mass ÷ volume and the mass is fixed, density is inversely proportional to volume, hence directly proportional to pressure: squeeze the gas (raise P) and it gets denser in exact proportion. So the new density is the old density scaled by the pressure ratio — no need for the gas's molar mass at all.
Formula d ∝ P at constant T, so d₂ = d₁ × (P₂/P₁). From PV = const and d ∝ 1/V, doubling P halves V and doubles d. Keep P in the same unit on top and bottom: P₁ = 101.3 kPa = 101.3×10³ N m⁻², P₂ = 2.24×10⁵ N m⁻².
Solution
1. Convert P₁ to N m⁻² (Pa): 101.3 kPa = 101.3 × 10³ = 1.013×10⁵ N m⁻².Both pressures must share one unit before taking a ratio.
2. Pressure ratio: P₂/P₁ = (2.24×10⁵) ÷ (1.013×10⁵) = 2.24/1.013 = 2.211.
3. Scale the density: d₂ = d₁ × 2.211 = 1.3 × 2.211.
4. Multiply: 1.3 × 2.211 = 2.87 kg m⁻³.Higher pressure compresses the gas, so the density rises from 1.3 to ≈2.87.
 P₁ = 1.013×10⁵ Pa   d₁ = 1.3 kg/m³
 P₂ = 2.24 ×10⁵ Pa   d₂ = ?
 d₂ = 1.3 × (2.24/1.013) = 1.3 × 2.211 = 2.87 kg/m³
⚡ JEE Trick Constant T ⇒ density tracks pressure (d ∝ P) — just multiply by the pressure ratio; molar mass never enters. Trap: convert kPa → Pa first (×10³), or the ratio (and the answer) is off by 1000.
Key point: at constant T, d ∝ P, so d₂ = 1.3 × (2.24×10⁵ ÷ 1.013×10⁵) ≈ 2.87 kg m⁻³.
2Syringe 10.0 cm³ at 1 atm, sealed — final volume at 3.5 atmBoyle's law (P–V)A-BOYLE-V🟡 Similar—
Concept A sealed syringe holds a fixed amount of gas at fixed temperature, so Boyle's law applies directly: the product of pressure and volume stays constant. Push the plunger to raise the pressure and the volume shrinks by exactly the inverse factor. Rearranging PV = const gives the new volume from the pressure ratio.
Formula P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ ⇒ V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂), at constant T and fixed n. Volume is inversely proportional to pressure, so the multiplier is the old-over-new pressure ratio. P₁ = 1 atm, V₁ = 10.0 cm³, P₂ = 3.5 atm.
Solution
1. Write Boyle's law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
2. Solve for V₂: V₂ = P₁V₁ ÷ P₂ = (1 × 10.0) ÷ 3.5.
3. Evaluate: V₂ = 10.0 ÷ 3.5 = 2.857 ≈ 2.86 cm³.Pressure rose 3.5×, so the volume fell to 1/3.5 of the original.
4. Sanity check: P₂V₂ = 3.5 × 2.857 = 10.0 = P₁V₁ ✓.
 P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
 (1)(10.0) = (3.5)(V₂)
 V₂ = 10.0 / 3.5 = 2.86 cm³
⚡ JEE Trick Boyle's law is a one-liner: V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂). Since pressure went up, volume must go down — if your answer is larger than 10 cm³ you flipped the ratio. Trap: units of P cancel, so atm/atm is fine; no conversion needed here.
Key point: at constant T, P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ ⇒ V₂ = (1 × 10.0) ÷ 3.5 ≈ 2.86 cm³.
3Gas density 1.12 g L⁻¹ at 1.15×10⁵ Pa — pressure for 60% higher densityBoyle's law (P–d)A-BOYLE-D🟡 Similar—
Concept Again at constant T, density rises in direct proportion to pressure (d ∝ P). We want the pressure that makes the gas 60% denser, i.e. new density = 1.60 × old density. Because the relationship is a simple proportion, the pressure must also increase by the same factor of 1.60 — the absolute density value 1.12 g L⁻¹ is not even needed for the ratio.
Formula d ∝ P ⇒ P₂ = P₁ × (d₂/d₁). "60% higher" means d₂/d₁ = 1 + 0.60 = 1.60, a pure ratio, so P scales by the same 1.60. P₁ = 1.15×10⁵ Pa.
Solution
1. Interpret "60% higher density": d₂ = d₁ + 0.60 d₁ = 1.60 d₁, so d₂/d₁ = 1.60.A 60% increase multiplies the original by 1.60, not 0.60.
2. Apply d ∝ P: P₂ = P₁ × (d₂/d₁) = 1.15×10⁵ × 1.60.
3. Multiply: 1.15 × 1.60 = 1.84, so P₂ = 1.84×10⁵ Pa.To make the gas 60% denser you must raise the pressure 60%.
4. (For reference the new density would be 1.12 × 1.60 = 1.792 g L⁻¹, but it is not required.)
 d ∝ P  (const T)
 d₂/d₁ = 1.60
 P₂ = 1.15×10⁵ × 1.60 = 1.84×10⁵ Pa
⚡ JEE Trick "x% higher" ⇒ multiply by (1 + x/100); here ×1.60. Since d ∝ P, the pressure scales by the identical factor — the starting density number is a red herring. Trap: using 0.60 instead of 1.60 gives 0.69×10⁵ Pa, which would lower the density.
Key point: at constant T, d ∝ P ⇒ P₂ = 1.15×10⁵ × 1.60 = 1.84×10⁵ Pa (a 60% pressure rise gives a 60% denser gas).
4He balloon 3.2 L at 1.2 bar rises, bursts at 0.25 bar — limiting volumeBoyle's law (rising balloon)A-BOYLE-V🟡 Similar—
Concept As a helium balloon rises, the surrounding atmospheric pressure falls, so the trapped gas expands (Boyle's law, assuming near-constant T). The balloon reaches its "limiting" or bursting volume when the outside pressure has dropped to the stated value. That maximum volume is just the initial volume scaled up by the inverse pressure ratio.
Formula P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ ⇒ V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂). Lower external pressure ⇒ larger volume; the multiplier is high-over-low pressure. P₁ = 1.2 bar, V₁ = 3.2 L, P₂ = 0.25 bar.
Solution
1. Pressure ratio: P₁/P₂ = 1.2 ÷ 0.25 = 4.8.Pressure fell to 0.25/1.2 of its start, so volume grows 4.8×.
2. Limiting volume: V₂ = V₁ × 4.8 = 3.2 × 4.8.
3. Multiply: 3.2 × 4.8 = 15.36 ≈ 15.4 L.The balloon nearly quintuples in volume before bursting.
4. Check: P₂V₂ = 0.25 × 15.36 = 3.84 = 1.2 × 3.2 = P₁V₁ ✓.
 P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
 (1.2)(3.2) = (0.25)(V₂)
 V₂ = 3.84 / 0.25 = 15.36 L ≈ 15.4 L
⚡ JEE Trick Rising balloon ⇒ pressure drops ⇒ volume grows: V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂). A falling external pressure must give a bigger volume, so the ratio's larger pressure sits on top. Trap: bar units cancel — no need to convert to Pa for a pure P–V ratio.
Key point: at constant T, V₂ = 3.2 × (1.2 ÷ 0.25) = 15.36 ≈ 15.4 L at the bursting point.

1Graham's law relates diffusion rate with … (V / M / t / T)Graham's law statementA-GRAHAM🟡 SimilarbMolecular mass
Concept Graham's law of diffusion/effusion is a statement about how the rate of a gas escaping or spreading depends on how heavy its molecules are. At the same temperature and pressure, lighter molecules move faster (they have higher average speed for the same kinetic energy), so they diffuse more quickly. The law fixes the exact dependence: rate varies as the inverse square root of the molar (molecular) mass.
Key relation r ∝ 1/√M at constant T and P (equivalently r ∝ 1/√d, since d ∝ M for gases). Equal kinetic energy ½mv² at a given T means heavier m ⇒ smaller v ⇒ slower diffusion, and the speed goes as 1/√m.
Solution
1. Recall the statement: at constant T and P, the rate of diffusion/effusion is inversely proportional to √(molar mass).
2. So the controlling quantity is the molecular (molar) mass M — option (b).
3. Reject the distractors: volume V and temperature T appear in other gas laws, and time t is a consequence of the rate, not what the law relates rate to.
 option        in Graham's law?
 volume V      no
 mol. mass M   YES  (r ∝ 1/√M)   ✓
 time t        no (t follows from r)
 temperature T no (that is r ∝ √T, a separate result)
⚡ JEE Trick Graham's law = "lighter is faster," r ∝ 1/√M. Whenever a question links diffusion/effusion rate to a property, the answer is molar mass (or its stand-in, density). Trap: temperature does affect rate (r ∝ √T) but that is a different relation — Graham's law itself is stated at constant T.
Answer: (b) Molecular mass.
2Which pair of gases can be separated using Graham's law?Isotope/gas separationA-GRAHAM🟡 SimilarbUF₆-235 and UF₆-238
Concept Graham's law separates gases only when they have different molar masses — the lighter one effuses faster, so repeated effusion gradually enriches a mixture. This is exactly how uranium is enriched: the two isotopes ²³⁵U and ²³⁸U are converted to the gas uranium hexafluoride (UF₆), whose two isotopic forms differ slightly in mass and therefore in effusion rate. A pair of gases with identical molar mass cannot be separated this way.
Key relation r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁) — a ratio ≠ 1 is needed to separate. If M₁ = M₂ the ratio is 1 and no enrichment occurs; the further apart the masses, the easier the separation. ²³⁵UF₆ ≈ 349, ²³⁸UF₆ ≈ 352 g mol⁻¹ — tiny but non-zero difference.
Solution
1. Separation requires the two species to differ in molar mass so their effusion rates differ.
2. ²³⁵UF₆ and ²³⁸UF₆ have molar masses ≈349 and ≈352 g mol⁻¹ — different, so r(²³⁵) > r(²³⁸) slightly.The lighter ²³⁵ form effuses marginally faster; thousands of stages amplify this into usable enrichment.
3. Hence the UF₆-235 / UF₆-238 pair (option b) is separable by Graham's law — the classic gaseous-diffusion enrichment of uranium.
4. Reject any pair of equal molar mass (e.g. CO and N₂, both 28), which effuse at the same rate and cannot be separated.
 pair                     M₁ vs M₂     separable?
 UF₆-235 / UF₆-238        349 vs 352   yes (r differs)   ✓
 gases of equal M         M = M        no (r₁/r₂ = 1)
⚡ JEE Trick Graham's-law separation needs unequal molar masses; the standard textbook example is UF₆ isotope enrichment. Trap: pairs like CO/N₂ or CO/C₂H₄ share M = 28 and are not separable by effusion — watch for equal-mass decoys.
Answer: (b) UF₆-235 and UF₆-238.

Concept Graham's law is most often quoted with molar mass, but for gases at the same T and P the density is directly proportional to molar mass (d = PM/RT), so the two forms are interchangeable. This question asks the density form: since heavier (denser) gases diffuse slower, the rate is inversely proportional to the square root of density.
Key relation r ∝ 1/√M and, because d ∝ M at fixed T, P, r ∝ 1/√d. Density is just molar mass repackaged (d = PM/RT), so wherever √M appears you may substitute √d.

1. Start from Graham's law: r ∝ 1/√M.
2. Use d ∝ M (same T, P for all gases) to replace M by d: r ∝ 1/√d.A denser gas has heavier molecules, so it diffuses more slowly.
3. In words: the rate is inversely proportional to the square root of density — option (b).
4. Reject "proportional to density," "inversely proportional to density" (no root), and "proportional to √d" — all have the wrong power or sign.
 form            expression      option

5Diffusion of a gas increases with …Rate ↔ temperatureA-TEMP🟡 SimilarcIncrease in temperature
Concept The speed of diffusion depends on how fast the molecules move. Raising the temperature increases their average kinetic energy and therefore their average speed, so they spread faster — diffusion rate rises with temperature.
