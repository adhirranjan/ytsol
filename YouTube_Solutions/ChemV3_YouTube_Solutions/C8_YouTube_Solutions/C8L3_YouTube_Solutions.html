1 (i)Fill in the blank: As2S3(s) + NO3−(aq) → AsO43−(aq) + NO(g) + S(s) (acidic medium). On the basis of above redox reaction taking place in acidic medium answer the following question.
The oxidation number of As increases from ________ to ________.		Oxidation number changeBalance the following reaction by ion electrons method ( acidic medium). As_(2)S_(3)-NO_ | Doubtnut🟢 Exact+3 to +5
Concept An oxidation number is fixed by the sum rule: the signed numbers of all atoms add up to the charge on the species. For this reaction two facts are enough — oxygen is −2 and the molecule’s net charge is known. The trap is the sulphur: in As2S3 it is sulphide S at −2, not the +6 it would carry in a sulphate, so arsenic is pinned at +3 there. Treating S as +6 by habit is the single most common slip and it throws the whole answer off.
Key relation Σ(oxidation numbers) = charge on the species Oxygen is −2 in AsO43− and sulphur is −2 in As2S3, both standard values.
Solution
1. As2S3 is neutral, so 2(As) + 3(−2) = 0.
2. That gives 2(As) = +6, so As = +3 in the reactant. S is the sulphide ion here.
3. In the product AsO43− the charge is −3, so As + 4(−2) = −3.
4. As = −3 + 8 = +5 in the product.
5. Arsenic rises from +3 to +5, a loss of 2 electrons per As atom, so it is oxidised.
6. Both As atoms climb, so one formula unit releases 2 × 2 = 4 electrons from the arsenic centres alone.
 As2S3 : 2(As) + 3(-2) = 0  -> As = +3
 AsO4^3- : As + 4(-2) = -3  -> As = +5
 -----------------------------------------
 As : +3 -> +5   (oxidised, 2e- lost each)
⚡ JEE Trick As2S3 is a double reductant: both As (+3→+5) and S (−2→0) are oxidised, so count electrons from both centres when balancing. Reading S as +6 would wrongly force As negative, which is impossible for a metalloid bonded to sulphur. Always assign the clearly electronegative partner (here S as sulphide, O as oxide) first, then back-solve the central atom.
Answer: +3 to +5.

Concept Nitrogen is the oxidant here: it is reduced while arsenic and sulphur are oxidised. Its oxidation number is read from the nitrate ion first and from NO afterwards, with oxygen taken as −2 throughout. Because nitrate supplies the electrons that both As and S demand, its oxidation state must drop, and the size of that drop tells you how many moles of nitrate the balanced equation will need.
Key relation Σ(oxidation numbers) = charge on the species NO3− carries −1; NO is neutral; O is −2 in both.
Solution
1. In NO3− the charge is −1, so N + 3(−2) = −1.
2. N = −1 + 6 = +5 in the reactant nitrate.
3. In NO the molecule is neutral, so N + (−2) = 0.
4. N = +2 in the product. Only one oxygen now, so N drops sharply.
5. Nitrogen falls from +5 to +2, a gain of 3 electrons per N, so it is reduced; nitrate is the oxidising agent.
6. Each N accepts 3 electrons, which is what must balance the 4 electrons released per As2S3 plus the 6 from its three sulphurs.
7. So 10 electrons leave each As2S3 and, at 3 per N, roughly 10/3 nitrate ions are consumed per formula unit — the stoichiometry flows directly from this per-atom change.
 NO3^- : N + 3(-2) = -1   -> N = +5
 NO    : N + (-2) = 0     -> N = +2
 -----------------------------------------
 N : +5 -> +2   (reduced, 3e- gained each)
⚡ JEE Trick Dilute HNO3 typically gives NO (N: +5→+2, 3e−) while concentrated HNO3 gives NO2 (+5→+4, 1e−). Spot the gas and you know the electron count instantly, without balancing the whole equation. A quick sanity check: N in any oxoanion of nitrogen never exceeds +5, so +5 is always the ceiling it falls from.
Answer: +5 to +2.

Concept The sulphur of the sulphide is also oxidised in this reaction, ending up as free sulphur. A free element in its elementary form always has oxidation number zero, which fixes the product side at a glance. The reactant side needs the sum rule with arsenic already known to be +3, so sulphur is forced to −2. Seeing both the As and the S of one species climb is the hallmark of a compound that is its own double reducing agent.

Solution
1. In As2S3 set 2(+3) + 3(S) = 0 using As = +3.
2. 3(S) = −6, so S = −2 in the reactant. Sulphur is less electronegative than oxygen but more than arsenic, so it is the anion here.
3. The product is S(s), elemental sulphur, so S = 0.
4. Sulphur rises from −2 to 0, losing 2 electrons per atom, so it too is oxidised.
5. Three S atoms per formula unit give 3 × 2 = 6 electrons, on top of the 4 from arsenic.
6. One As2S3 therefore releases 4 + 6 = 10 electrons in all, each pair taken up by nitrate.

Here two different atoms of one reductant oxidise at once — add their electrons, do not pick one. Forgetting the sulphur contribution is the classic error that makes the final nitrate coefficient come out wrong.

Concept Balancing by oxidation number means the total electrons lost equal the total gained. Phosphorus is oxidised and nitrogen reduced; the stoichiometric coefficients x and y just make those electron totals match, then the rest follows by atom counting. Here x is the moles of nitric acid and y the moles of phosphoric acid, so finding the electron change on each central atom fixes both at once without trial and error.
Key relation (electrons lost by P) = (electrons gained by N) P: 0→+5 in H3PO4; N: +5→+4 in NO2.
Solution
1. Free P has oxidation number 0; in H3PO4, P = +5, so each P loses 5 electrons.
2. In HNO3, N = +5; in NO2, N = +4, so each N gains 1 electron.
3. To match, 1 P (5 e− lost) needs 5 N (5 e− gained): y = 1 and x = 5.
4. Write P + 5 HNO3 → 1 H3PO4 + 5 NO2 + H2O and check atoms.
5. N: 5 = 5. H: left 5; right 3 + 2(1) = 5. O: left 15; right 4 + 10 + 1 = 15. All balance.
6. So x = 5, y = 1, with one H2O completing the equation.

No need to balance O and H first; they fall out automatically once x and y are set. The lone water molecule is the giveaway that only one P was oxidised, pinning y = 1.
Answer: x = 5, y = 1.

1In the oxidation number method of balancing redox reactions, which of the following is the first step? (Level-1)
(a) Balance hydrogen and oxygen atoms   (b) Equalise total increase and decrease in oxidation numbers   (c) Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers have changed   (d) Multiply oxidising and reducing agents by suitable integers		Oxidation number method stepsRedox Reactions 04 ||Balancing a Chemical Equation By Oxidation Number Method IIT JEE MAINS /NEET🟡 Similar(c) Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers have changed
Concept The oxidation-number method has a fixed order of steps. Before any electron accounting can start you must know which atoms actually changed, so the very first job is to assign oxidation numbers and spot the ones that moved. The other options describe genuine later steps, which is exactly why the question is testing whether you know the sequence rather than the individual actions.
Key relation Step 1: assign ON → find the changed elements Everything after it (equalising, balancing H/O, charges) depends on knowing the redox pair first.
Solution
1. Write oxidation numbers over every atom on both sides of the skeletal equation.
2. Compare them and identify the elements whose oxidation numbers have changed — the oxidised and reduced species. This is step one, option (c).
3. Only then compute the increase and decrease per atom and equalise them, option (b) — that is a later step.
4. Multiplying oxidising and reducing agents by integers, option (d), comes after equalising, not first.
5. Balancing H and O, option (a), is among the final steps once electrons already match.
6. So the correct first step is to locate the atoms that change oxidation number.
 order of the oxidation-number method:
  1. assign ON, find changed elements   <-- (c)
  2. find increase / decrease per atom
  3. equalise total increase = decrease  (b)
  4. balance other atoms, then H and O   (a)
  5. multiply agents as needed           (d)
⚡ JEE Trick Options (a), (b) and (d) are all real steps — just not first. The method always begins by identifying the redox pair; everything else is bookkeeping built on that. A reliable memory hook: nothing about electrons, H or O can be done until you know which atoms actually moved, so "identify changes" has to lead.
Answer: (c) Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers have changed.

3If three electrons are lost by a metal ion M3+, its final oxidation number will be: (Level-3)
(a) zero   (b) +6   (c) +2   (d) +4		Oxidation number changeWCLN - Chemistry - REDOX - number of electrons transferred🟡 Similar(b) +6
Concept Losing electrons is oxidation, which raises the oxidation number. Each electron lost makes the species one unit more positive, so three electrons lost from M3+ push it three units higher, not lower. The trap is to subtract instead of add, which happens when a student confuses the number of electrons with a reduction. An electron carries a −1 charge, so taking one away must leave the ion more positive — the arithmetic is forced by charge conservation alone.
Key relation final ON = initial ON + (electrons lost) Electrons are negative; removing three negatives adds +3 to the charge.
Solution
1. The ion starts at M3+, oxidation number +3.
2. Losing an electron removes negative charge, so the species becomes more positive — this is oxidation.
3. Three electrons lost raise the number by 3: +3 + 3 = +6.
4. So the final oxidation number is +6, option (b).
5. Option (a) 0 and (c) +2 would mean electrons were gained (reduction), the opposite of the stem.
6. Option (d) +4 corresponds to losing only one electron, not three.
7. So M3+ becomes M6+, consistent with charge conservation: 3 electrons of −1 removed shift the ion by exactly +3.
 M^3+  -> lose 3 e-

 -----------------------------------------

1Explain balancing of redox reaction.Balancing redox reactionsRedox Reactions 04 ||Balancing a Chemical Equation By Oxidation Number Method IIT JEE MAINS /NEET🟡 SimilarOxidation-number method (6 steps)
Concept A redox equation is balanced when both atoms and charge balance and the electrons lost equal the electrons gained. The oxidation-number method enforces the last condition directly: equate the total rise in oxidation number to the total fall, then tidy up atoms and charge.
Key relation total increase in ON = total decrease in ON This single rule is what makes the electron transfer balanced before H, O and charge are fixed.
Solution
1. Write the skeletal equation and assign oxidation numbers to every atom.
2. Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers change — one oxidised, one reduced.
3. Compute the increase per oxidised atom and the decrease per reduced atom.
4. Multiply each by suitable integers so the total increase equals the total decrease; place these as coefficients on the redox species.
5. Balance all other atoms, then oxygen with H2O and hydrogen with H+ (acidic) or H2O plus OH− (basic).
6. Finally check that total charge is equal on both sides; a correct charge balance confirms the equation.
 1 assign ON       4 equalise rise = fall
 2 find changed    5 balance other, O(H2O), H
 3 rise / fall     6 check total charge
 -----------------------------------------
 acidic: H+ for H ;  basic: OH- for charge
⚡ JEE Trick The whole method hinges on step 4: make electrons lost equal electrons gained first, and the rest is plain atom and charge bookkeeping. Balancing H and O before equalising electrons is the usual mistake.
Answer: Equate total increase and decrease in oxidation number, then balance atoms, H/O and charge.

Concept The same symbol n appears twice here: as the charge of AO4n− and as the number of oxygens in HAOn2−. Gaining 2 e− means element A's oxidation state must fall by exactly 2; writing that condition in terms of n gives a single equation to solve.
Key relation (state of A in AO4) − (state of A in HAOn) = 2 a 2-electron reduction lowers the central atom's oxidation number by exactly 2.
Solution
1. In AO4n−: A + 4(−2) = −n ⇒ A = 8 − n.
2. In HAOn2−: (+1) + A + n(−2) = −2 ⇒ A = 2n − 3.
3. Gaining 2 e− drops A by 2, so (8 − n) − (2n − 3) = 2.
4. Simplify: 11 − 3n = 2 ⇒ 3n = 9 ⇒ n = 3.
5. Check n = 3: AO43− gives A = +5; HAO32− gives A = +3; the drop is 2, matching 2 e−. a PO43−-type skeleton.
6. So n = 3 is consistent on both sides.
 AO4^n-   : A = 8 - n
 HAOn^2-  : A = 2n - 3
 drop of 2: (8-n) - (2n-3) = 2
 11 - 3n = 2 -> n = 3
 -----------------------------------------
 n = 3                  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick When one unknown plays two roles (charge and subscript), write A's oxidation state for each side and equate the difference to the electrons gained — one line, one solve. Guessing n from the charge alone (−n + 2 = −2 ⇒ n = 0) ignores the changing oxygen count and fails.
Answer: n = 3.
