1Which physical property quantifies a body's resistance to being set into or brought out of rotational motion? (a) Mass (b) Moment of inertia (c) Linear momentum (d) Torque — (Level-1)Meaning of moment of inertiaRotational Motion 04 — Moment of inertia and radius of gyration (JEE/NEET)🟡 SimilarbMoment of inertia
Concept In straight-line motion the reluctance to change velocity is measured by mass alone. On an axis the reluctance depends on how far the matter sits from that axis: a particle at 2r needs four times the torque of an equal particle at r for the same angular acceleration. The sum of all those mr² contributions is the moment of inertia, and it fills exactly the slot mass fills in F = ma.
Formula τ = Iα. rotational twin of F = ma: for a fixed torque, larger I means smaller α — literally resistance to being set into rotation With I = Σmiri². each particle enters with the square of its distance, so shape and axis matter as much as mass
Solution
1. The property wanted is whatever divides torque to give angular acceleration: α = τ/I, so a large I gives a small α for the same twist.
2. Reject (a): a 2 kg ring has I = MR² = 2R² but a 2 kg sphere of the same radius has I = 0.4MR² = 0.8R² — same mass, resistance 2.5× apart.one counter-example kills mass as the answer
3. Reject (c): p = mv reports how much motion a body already has; it is even zero for a body spinning about a fixed axis.
4. Reject (d): τ = rF sinθ is the agent that changes the rotation — the cause, not the opposition to it.
5. Accept (b): I = Σmiri² sits in the denominator of α = τ/I and in L = Iω, KE = ½Iω² — exactly where m sits in the linear forms.
6. Feel-test: a rod spun about its centre has ML²/12, about its end ML²/3 — four times harder with the mass unchanged, so only the distribution can be responsible.
  linear quantity        rotational analogue
  -------------------    -------------------
  mass      m            moment of inertia I
  force     F            torque      tau
  F = m a                tau = I alpha
  p = m v                L = I omega
  KE = (1/2) m v^2       KE = (1/2) I w^2
⚡ JEE Trick The distractors are the three quantities standing next to I on every formula sheet, so a hurried reader grabs a familiar word. Mass tempts because it is the translational answer; torque tempts because torque is what does the "setting into motion". Use the slot test: whatever multiplies α in τ = Iα is the inertia; everything else is cause or state.
Answer: (b) Moment of inertia.
2Calculate the moment of inertia of a 4 kg solid sphere of radius 0.20 m about its diameter. (a) 0.032 kg m² (b) 0.064 kg m² (c) 0.080 kg m² (d) 0.160 kg m² — (Level-2)Solid sphere about a diameterRigid Body Dynamics #6 — Moment of Inertia of a Solid Sphere (JEE & NEET)🟡 Similarb0.064 kg m²
Concept A solid sphere is built from thin discs stacked along the axis, and most of its volume lies at radii well inside R. Integrating those discs gives the 2/5 factor: only two-fifths of MR² survives, far less than a ring’s full MR². The axis here is a diameter, which is the sphere’s own centre-of-mass axis, so no parallel-axis correction is needed — the formula applies as printed.
Formula I = 2/5 MR². standard result for a uniform solid sphere about any diameter; every diameter is equivalent by symmetry Units follow from kg × m². radius must be in metres before squaring, or the answer lands two orders out
Solution
1. List the data: M = 4 kg, R = 0.20 m, axis = a diameter through the centre of mass.
2. Square the radius first: R² = (0.20)² = 0.04 m².
3. Chain the formula: I = (2/5)MR² = (2/5)(4)(0.04) = 0.4 × 0.16 = 0.064 kg m².grouping (2/5)(4) = 1.6 first keeps the arithmetic to one multiplication
4. Sanity-check the size: MR² = 4 × 0.04 = 0.16 kg m², and the answer must be 40 % of that — 0.064 is exactly 0.4 × 0.16.
5. Decode (c) 0.080: that is (1/2)MR², the disc value — right family, wrong body.
6. Decode (d) 0.160 = MR², the ring value (all mass at R); (a) 0.032 is half the correct answer, the slip of using (1/5)MR².
  body (mass M, radius R)    I about diameter
  -----------------------    ----------------
  ring / hoop                1.00 M R^2
  disc                       0.50 M R^2
  hollow sphere              0.67 M R^2
  solid sphere               0.40 M R^2  <-- here
  numbers: 0.4 x 4 x 0.04 = 0.064 kg m^2
⚡ JEE Trick Every distractor is a real formula for the wrong body, so a mis-remembered constant lands on an option instead of on nothing — the paper will not warn you. Fix the ladder 1, 1/2, 2/3, 2/5 (ring, disc, shell, sphere) and note that it only ever falls: the more mass hides near the axis, the smaller I gets.
Answer: (b) 0.064 kg m².
3A 2 kg solid sphere (R = 0.10 m) rotates about an axis 0.30 m from its centre. Find I about this axis. (a) 0.020 kg m² (b) 0.094 kg m² (c) 0.188 kg m² (d) 0.300 kg m² — (Level-3)Parallel axis theorem, sphereRotational Motion 07 — Perpendicular and Parallel Axis Theorem (JEE/NEET)🟡 Similarc0.188 kg m²
Concept Moving the axis away from the centre of mass never decreases I: every particle picks up the same extra displacement d, and the cross-terms cancel because the centre of mass sits at the origin of the body frame. What survives is a rigid Md² penalty added to the centre-of-mass value. Here the sphere still spins about a line parallel to a diameter, so Icm = (2/5)MR² is the starting value and d = 0.30 m is the shift.
Formula I = Icm + Md². valid only when the reference axis passes through the centre of mass and the two axes are parallel With Icm = 2/5 MR². the sphere's own diameter value, independent of where the new axis lies
Solution
1. Data: M = 2 kg, R = 0.10 m, d = 0.30 m, and the axis is parallel to a diameter.
2. Centre-of-mass term: Icm = (2/5)MR² = (2/5)(2)(0.01) = 0.008 kg m².
3. Shift term: Md² = (2)(0.30)² = 2 × 0.09 = 0.18 kg m².this dwarfs Icm because d = 3R, and the shift enters squared
4. Add them: I = 0.008 + 0.18 = 0.188 kg m², matching option (c).
5. Structure-check: I = MR²(2/5 + 9) since d² = 9R², i.e. 9.4 MR² = 9.4 × 0.02 = 0.188 — the same number by a different route.
6. Decode the traps: (d) 0.300 uses d = 0.30 as if I = Md alone or forgets to square correctly; (a) 0.020 is MR² only; (b) 0.094 is exactly half the right answer, the sign of using (1/2)Md².
  term          value (kg m^2)     share
  -----------   ---------------    -----
  (2/5) M R^2   0.008               4 %
  M d^2         0.180              96 %
  -----------   ---------------    -----
  total         0.188
  d = 3R so M d^2 = 9 M R^2 dominates
⚡ JEE Trick When d is a few times R, the Md² term rules and Icm is a rounding correction — but dropping it lands on 0.180, which is not an option, so the paper punishes both shortcuts and sloppy squaring. Never apply the parallel-axis theorem from a non-centre-of-mass axis: the theorem is one-way, from the centre of mass outward.
Answer: (c) 0.188 kg m².
4From a circular disc of radius R and mass 9 M, a small disc of radius R/3 is removed from the disc as shown in the figure (the hole touches the rim, so its centre lies at 2R/3 from the centre C). Find the moment of inertia of the remaining disc about an axis perpendicular to the plane of the disc and passing through the point 'O'. — (Level-4)Disc with a hole, subtraction methodFrom a circular disc of radius R and mass 9M, a small disc of radius R/3 is removed🟢 Exact4MR²
Concept Moment of inertia is an additive sum over mass elements, so a body with a hole equals the full body minus the piece that was taken out, both measured about the same axis. The removed disc has one-ninth the area of the full disc, so it carries one-ninth of 9M, i.e. mass M. Its own centre is off-axis, so its contribution needs a parallel-axis shift before it can be subtracted.
Formula Irem = Ifull − Ihole. both taken about the same axis through the big disc's centre; subtraction is legal because I is a plain sum With Idisc = ½mr² and I = Icm + md². the hole's centre sits 2R/3 from the axis, so its own value must be transported there
Solution
1. Mass of the removed disc: mass ∝ area, so m = 9M × (R/3)²/R² = 9M/9 = M.
2. Full disc about the perpendicular central axis: Ifull = ½(9M)R² = 4.5MR².
3. Geometry: the hole touches the rim, so its centre is at d = R − R/3 = 2R/3 from the centre.this distance, not R/3, is what the figure fixes — read the picture, not the label
4. Hole about the same axis: Ihole = ½M(R/3)² + M(2R/3)² = MR²/18 + 4MR²/9 = MR²/18 + 8MR²/18 = MR²/2.
5. Subtract: Irem = 4.5MR² − 0.5MR² = 4MR².
6. Check the share: the hole removes 1/9 of the mass but only 1/9 of the inertia (0.5 out of 4.5) because its extra distance offsets its small size — a neat coincidence worth noticing.
  piece        mass   I about central perp axis
  ----------   ----   -------------------------
  full disc     9M     (1/2)(9M)R^2 = 4.5 M R^2
  hole (own)     M     (1/2)M(R/3)^2 = M R^2/18
  hole shift     M     M (2R/3)^2 = 8 M R^2/18
  hole total     M     0.5 M R^2
  remaining     8M     4.0 M R^2
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit side by side: taking the hole's mass as 9M/3 instead of 9M/9, and taking its offset as R/3 instead of 2R/3. Either slip still yields a clean-looking multiple of MR², so the answer never looks wrong. Always fix mass by area ratio and read the offset off the tangency condition.
Answer: 4MR².

1A metallic ring of mass 1 kg has moment of inertia 1 kg m² when rotating about one of its diameters. It is molten and re-molded into a thin uniform disc of the same radius. How much will its moment of inertia be, when rotated about its own axis. — (Level: Board)Ring recast into a discA metallic ring of mass 1 kg has moment of inertia 1 kg m² about a diameter🟢 Exact1 kg m²
Concept Melting changes the shape but not the mass, and the problem fixes the radius as well, so the only thing that changes is how the mass is spread. The given datum is a ring about a diameter, an in-plane axis, which the perpendicular axis theorem ties to the ring’s own axis value MR². From that one number MR² can be extracted, and the disc’s own-axis value is a fixed fraction of the same MR².
Formula Iring,dia = ½MR². from Iz = Ix + Iy with Ix = Iy by symmetry and Iz = MR² And Idisc,axis = ½MR². the disc's perpendicular central axis value, derived by stacking rings from 0 to R
Solution
1. Given for the ring: M = 1 kg and I about a diameter = 1 kg m².
2. Use the ring's diameter formula: ½MR² = 1, so MR² = 2 kg m².MR² is the transferable quantity — mass and radius survive the melting unchanged
3. Read off the radius if wanted: R² = 2 m², R = √2 ≈ 1.41 m, though the answer never needs it.
4. After recasting, the disc has the same M = 1 kg and the same R, hence the same MR² = 2 kg m².
5. Disc about its own axis: I = ½MR² = ½(2) = 1 kg m².
6. So the number is unchanged at 1 kg m² — a coincidence of the two ½ factors, not a conservation law.a ring's diameter axis and a disc's own axis happen to share the same coefficient
  stage      body   axis            I
  --------   ----   -------------   ------------
  before     ring   diameter        (1/2)MR^2 = 1
  extract    --     --              MR^2 = 2
  after      disc   own (perp) axis (1/2)MR^2 = 1
  M and R unchanged by melting
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is to treat the given 1 kg m² as the ring's own-axis value MR², which makes the disc come out at 0.5 kg m². The words "about one of its diameters" are the whole question — always convert a given I into MR² first, then apply the new body's coefficient.
Answer: 1 kg m².

3Which of the following has the smallest moment of inertia about the central axis if all have equal masses and radii? (a) Ring (b) Disc (c) Spherical shell (d) Sphere — (Level-1)Comparing standard I coefficientsA, B and C are disc, solid sphere and spherical shell with same radii — JEE Main 2025🟡 SimilardSphere
Concept Every standard body writes its central-axis moment of inertia as I = kMR², where the pure number k records nothing but how far from the axis the mass sits on average. Fixing M and R fixes MR², so the whole comparison collapses to comparing the four k values. A body that pushes mass out to the rim earns a large k; a body that packs mass near the centre earns a small one. The solid sphere is the only one of the four with material filling the interior right up to the axis, so it must win.
Formula I = kMR² with k = 1 (ring), ½ (disc), ⅔ (spherical shell), 2/5  (solid sphere). the coefficients follow from integrating r²dm over each mass distribution; only their ordering is needed here Smallest I ⇔ smallest k. M and R are common to all four, so MR² is a constant multiplier
Solution
1. Ring, axis through centre perpendicular to its plane: every element is exactly at R, so I = MR² and k = 1.000 — the theoretical maximum for radius R.
2. Disc, same axis: mass is spread evenly from 0 to R, giving I = ½MR², k = 0.500.a disc is a stack of rings of every radius, and averaging r² over area halves the ring value
3. Hollow (spherical) shell about a diameter: I = ⅔MR², k = 0.667 — the shell material is at R but most of it lies off the equator, closer to the axis than R.
4. Solid sphere about a diameter: I = 2/5 MR², k = 0.400.filling the interior adds mass at small r, which drags the average r² down
5. Order the four: 0.400 (sphere) < 0.500 (disc) < 0.667 (shell) < 1.000 (ring), so the sphere is smallest and the ring is largest.
6. Reading the distractors: (a) ring is the exact opposite — the largest; (b) disc beats only the shell and the ring; (c) is the trap of assuming "hollow" automatically means less inertia, when hollow actually means more.hollow lowers the mass, not the coefficient — and here the mass is fixed by the question
  body              central axis        k in I = k M R^2
  ---------------   -----------------   ----------------
  solid sphere      diameter            0.400  <-- least
  disc              perpendicular       0.500
  spherical shell   diameter            0.667
  ring              perpendicular       1.000  <-- most
⚡ JEE Trick The whole family of "which rolls fastest / which has least I" questions is decided by one memorised ladder: 2/5 < 1/2 < 2/3 < 1. The tempting wrong turn is (c), because "spherical shell" sounds light and empty; but the question fixes the mass, so emptiness only moves that same mass further from the axis and raises I.

4The moment of inertia of a thin uniform circular disc about one of the diameters is I. Two perpendicular diameters are exactly such a pair, and a uniform disc looks identical along every diameter, so the two contributions are equal. Concept Superposition again: full disc minus the removed disc, both about the same in-plane line. Two details decide the answer. First, the stated mass M is that of the drilled body, and a hole of half the radius removes a quarter of the area, so the original mass was 4M/3. Second, XX′ is an in-plane axis, so both discs start from the diameter coefficient ¼, and the hole's centre must be projected perpendicular onto XX′ — the 45° tilt cuts that distance by √2.

2. Full disc about XX′: Ifull = ¼M₀R² = 16M₀R²/64.
3. Offset of the hole from the axis: its centre is R/2 from O along the line of centres, and XX′ is at 45° to that line, so d = (R/2)sin45° and d² = R²/8.use the perpendicular distance, not the full R/2 — the 45° is precisely what this step is for

10A uniform rod of mass 2M is bent into four adjacent semi-circles each of radius r, all lying in the same plane. The moment of inertia of the bent rod about an axis through one end A and perpendicular to plane of rod is (a) 22Mr² (b) 88Mr² (c) 44Mr² (d) 66Mr² — (Level-6)Rod bent into a chain of semicirclesMoment of inertia of a uniform semicircular wire of mass M and radius r about a perpendicular line🟡 Similarc44Mr²
Concept Treat the wavy wire as four separate semicircular arcs strung along a straight baseline, each spanning 2r, and add their contributions about the one axis at A. For an arc, every element lies at r from its own circle-centre, so about a perpendicular axis through that centre the value is exactly mr². Shifting to A is not a plain parallel axis step, since a semicircle's centre of mass is not at its circle-centre — but the centre-of-mass offset points perpendicular to the baseline while the displacement to A runs along it, so the cross term vanishes and the shift behaves as if it were.
Formula IA = ∫|rC + d|²dm = mr² + 2m d⋅rcm + md² for one arc. expanding about the arc's own circle-centre C, whose value is mr² The middle term is zero, so IA = m(r² + d²). the centre of mass sits 2r/π perpendicular to the baseline, while d lies along it, so their dot product vanishes
Solution
1. Mass per arc: the whole wire is 2M split into four equal arcs, so m = 2M/4 = M/2 each.
2. Geometry: each semicircle spans 2r along the baseline, so with A at the origin the four circle-centres sit at r, 3r, 5r and 7r.the arcs alternate above and below the baseline, but that only flips the sign of an offset that already contributes nothing
3. Per-arc value: I = m(r² + d²), giving m(2r²), m(10r²), m(26r²) and m(50r²) for d = r, 3r, 5r, 7r.
4. Sum the brackets: 2 + 10 + 26 + 50 = 88, so I = 88 m r².
5. Substitute m = M/2: I = 88(M/2)r² = 44 M r² — option (c).option (b) 88Mr² is exactly this line with the per-arc mass left as M instead of M/2
6. Sanity: total mass 2M, farthest material 8r from A, so I < 2M(8r)² = 128Mr²; 44Mr² is about a third of that, right for mass spread evenly from 0 to 8r.

    '   '-'     '-'      centres at r, 3r, 5r, 7r from A
