Concept The multiplicative inverse of a complex number z is the number z−1 satisfying z·z−1 = 1; every complex number except 0 has exactly one. Division by a complex number is not defined directly, so the standard move is to rationalise — multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate z̄, which turns the denominator into the real number |z|². That single trick converts any reciprocal into a + ib form. Here z = −i is purely imaginary, so the arithmetic collapses to a sign change.
Formula z−1 = z̄/|z|² = (a − ib)/(a² + b²). Valid because z·z̄ = a² + b² is real, so the last division is ordinary real division.
Solution
1. Put z = −i into standard form: z = 0 + (−1)i, so a = 0 and b = −1.
2. Conjugate: z̄ = a − ib = 0 − (−1)i = i.
3. |z|² = a² + b² = 0² + (−1)² = 1. −i lies on the unit circle, so its modulus is 1 and squaring keeps it 1.
4. Hence z−1 = z̄/|z|² = i/1 = i.
5. By direct rationalising: 1/(−i) = (1/(−i))×(i/i) = i/(−i²) = i/1 = i. −i² = −(−1) = +1 — that is exactly where the sign flip is created.
6. Check: (−i)(i) = −i² = 1, so i is indeed the inverse of −i.
rule:   z^-1 = conj(z) / |z|^2

⚡ JEE Trick For any z on the unit circle |z|² = 1, so the inverse is simply the conjugate — no algebra needed: 1/i = −i and 1/(−i) = i. The trap is "cancelling" the minus sign and answering −i; the reciprocal of a purely imaginary number reverses its sign, it does not preserve it. Keep 1/i = −i memorised and every such blank falls out instantly.

Concept A complex number z = x + iy is drawn in the Argand plane as the single point (x, y): the horizontal coordinate carries the real part and the vertical coordinate carries the imaginary part. That is why the horizontal axis is called the real axis and the vertical axis the imaginary axis. So a geometric statement about where the point sits translates directly into an algebraic statement about x and y. "Lies on the x-axis" is a statement about the ordinate only.
Formula z = x + iy ↔ P(x, y), with Re(z) = x and Im(z) = y. The correspondence is one-to-one, so a point determines the number and the number determines the point.
Solution
1. Represent z = x + iy by the point P(x, y); abscissa = Re(z), ordinate = Im(z).
2. Every point of the x-axis has ordinate zero, i.e. it has the form (x, 0). The x-axis is defined by the equation y = 0.
3. Matching the two descriptions gives Im(z) = y = 0.
4. Then z = x + 0·i = x, an ordinary real number, so the x-axis is precisely the real axis. This is why the reals are viewed as a subset of the complex numbers.
5. Test cases: 7, −3 and 0 all lie on the x-axis; 7 + 2i does not, because its imaginary part is 2 ≠ 0.
6. Mirror statement: a number on the y-axis has Re(z) = 0 and is purely imaginary. Only z = 0 lies on both axes, being real and purely imaginary at once.

1 (iii)z₁ = 3 + 4i, |z| = ____.Modulus of a complex numberComplex Numbers 02| Conjugate & Modulus of Complex Numbers | Class 11 | JEE | Pace Series🟡 Similar5
Concept The modulus of z = x + iy is its distance from the origin in the Argand plane, so it is obtained from the right triangle with legs x and y by Pythagoras. Being a distance, |z| is always a non-negative real number, never complex and never negative. It can also be defined algebraically through z·z̄ = |z|², which is the version used when the number is buried inside a product or a quotient. For 3 + 4i the legs are the famous 3-4-5 pair.
Formula |z| = √(x² + y²), equivalently |z|² = z·z̄. The first is Pythagoras on the point (x, y); the second follows since (x+iy)(x−iy) = x² + y².
Solution
1. Compare z₁ = 3 + 4i with x + iy: x = 3, y = 4.
2. |z₁| = √(x² + y²) = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.
3. Cross-check with the conjugate: z₁·z̄₁ = (3+4i)(3−4i) = 9 − 16i² = 25, so |z₁| = √25 = 5. −16i² = +16 is what makes the cross terms vanish and leaves a real value.
4. The point (3, 4) sits 3 units right and 4 units up, so the hypotenuse is 5 — the 3-4-5 triangle.
5. Note |z₁| = 5 is positive even though signs may vary: |−3 − 4i| is also 5. Both coordinates are squared, so all four sign combinations give the same modulus.
6. Hence |z₁| = 5.

1 (iv)(1 + 2i)(2 + 2i) = ____.Multiplication of complex numbersChapter 5 - Complex Numbers Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division|CBSE Class 11 Maths|GS🟡 Similar&minus;2 + 6i
Concept Two complex numbers are multiplied exactly like two binomials in a real variable: expand every term, then apply the one extra rule i² = −1. That single substitution is what moves a term out of the imaginary column and into the real column, usually with a sign change. Once all four products are formed, real parts are collected together and imaginary parts together, because 1 and i are independent over the reals. The result is again of the form a + ib, so the complex numbers are closed under multiplication.
Formula (a + ib)(c + id) = (ac − bd) + i(ad + bc). It is plain distribution; the −bd appears only because the product of the two imaginary terms carries i² = −1.
Solution
1. Expand term by term: (1 + 2i)(2 + 2i) = 1·2 + 1·2i + 2i·2 + 2i·2i = 2 + 2i + 4i + 4i².
2. Replace i² by −1: 4i² = −4. This is the only place the answer differs from ordinary binomial expansion.
3. Real parts: 2 + (−4) = −2. Imaginary parts: 2 + 4 = 6.
4. Hence (1 + 2i)(2 + 2i) = −2 + 6i.
5. Formula check with a = 1, b = 2, c = 2, d = 2: (ac − bd) + i(ad + bc) = (2 − 4) + i(2 + 4) = −2 + 6i. Agreement with the closed form confirms no term was dropped.
6. Modulus sanity check: |1+2i||2+2i| = √5 · 2√2 = 2√10, and |−2+6i| = √40 = 2√10. Moduli multiply, so a mismatch here would expose an arithmetic slip at once.

  1 * 2            = 2          real
  1 * 2i           = 2i         imag

  2i * 2i  = 4i^2  = -4         real

⚡ JEE Trick Factor first when you can: (1+2i)(2+2i) = 2(1+2i)(1+i) = 2(1 + i + 2i + 2i²) = 2(−1 + 3i) = −2 + 6i, which halves the arithmetic. The classic error is treating 4i² as +4 and answering 6 + 6i. A quick guard is the modulus check in step 6 — it catches that sign error immediately.
Answer: −2 + 6i.

Once the number is written as a genuine a + ib, it can be compared term by term with the printed template. Comparison is legitimate only after both sides are in the same shape, so the denominator is fixed first.

Concept To divide by a complex number, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator; the denominator then becomes the real number |z|² and the quotient reaches standard form. When the two numbers are conjugates of each other, the numerator conveniently becomes a perfect square. A quotient of conjugates always has modulus 1, which gives a free and very reliable check on the final answer. Only after the expression is genuinely in the printed shape may coefficients be compared.
Formula z₁/z₂ = z₁z̄₂/|z₂|², and |z₁/z₂| = |z₁|/|z₂|. The first clears i from the denominator; the second lets the size of the answer be predicted before computing it.
Solution
1. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate 2 + i: (2+i)/(2−i) = (2+i)(2+i)/((2−i)(2+i)).
2. Denominator: (2 − i)(2 + i) = 4 − i² = 4 + 1 = 5.
3. Numerator: (2 + i)² = 4 + 4i + i² = 4 + 4i − 1 = 3 + 4i. i² = −1 pulls the real part from 4 down to 3.
4. So (2+i)/(2−i) = (3 + 4i)/5; comparing with (p + qi)/r gives p = 3, q = 4 and r = 5.
5. Modulus check: |2+i| = |2−i| = √5, so the quotient has modulus 1, and |(3+4i)/5| = 5/5 = 1. With r = 3 the modulus would be 5/3, so r = 3 cannot be right.

Concept In a rhombus the diagonals bisect each other and meet at right angles, so M is the midpoint of both AC and BD, and the segment MA is perpendicular to MD. Building A and C as M plus and minus one perpendicular displacement of the right length then gives their coordinates outright.
Formula displacement = (head) − (tail);  multiplying by i rotates it 90° about the origin. Rhombus diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other, so MA = ±(MD length ratio)·i·MD.
Solution
1. Displacement from M to D: MD = (1 + i) − (2 − i) = −1 + 2i, so |MD| = √(1 + 4) = √5.
