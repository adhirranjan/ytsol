1Is the angular velocity of rotation of hour hand of a watch greater or smaller than the angular velocity of Earth's rotation about its own axis? — (Level-1)Angular velocity comparisonIs the angular speed of rotation of hour hand of a watch greater or smaller🟢 ExactGreater (hour hand ω = 2π/12 h is twice the Earth's 2π/24 h)
Concept Angular velocity measures how fast an angle is swept, not how far a point travels, so the physical size of the clock or of the Earth is irrelevant. For uniform rotation the only thing that fixes ω is the time period T — the time for one full 2π turn. An hour hand laps the dial once in 12 h; the Earth spins once about its own axis in 24 h. The shorter period wins.
Formula ω = 2π/T = 2πn, with T the period of one complete revolution. ω ∝ 1/T, so the whole comparison collapses to comparing two periods — no radii and no linear speeds are needed.
Solution
1. Hour hand: it sweeps the whole dial once every 12 h, so Th = 12 × 3600 = 43200 s.A dial is marked 1–12, so one lap of the hour hand is 12 h, not 24 h — the step most students fumble.
2. Earth spinning about its own axis: one rotation per day, TE = 24 × 3600 = 86400 s.
3. ωh = 2π/Th = 2π/43200 = 1.454 × 10−4 rad/s.
4. ωE = 2π/TE = 2π/86400 = 7.272 × 10−5 rad/s.
5. Ratio: ωh/ωE = TE/Th = 86400/43200 = 2.The 2π cancels, so the ratio of angular velocities is simply the inverse ratio of periods.
6. Ratio > 1, hence ωh > ωE: the hour hand is angularly faster, by exactly a factor of 2.

Second trap: reading the hour hand's period as 24 h; one lap of a 12-mark dial is 12 h.
Answer: Greater — the hour hand's ω = 2π/12 h is exactly twice the Earth's ω = 2π/24 h.
2The motor of an engine is rotating about its axis with an angular velocity of 100 rev/min. It comes to rest in 15 s, after being switched off. Assuming constant angular deceleration, what are the numbers of revolutions made by it before coming to rest? — (Level-2)Uniform angular decelerationThe motor of an engine is rotating about its axis with an angular velocity of 100 rpm. It comes to rest in 15 s🟢 Exact12.5 revolutions
Concept After the switch-off the motor decelerates uniformly, so the angular kinematic equations are exact twins of the linear SUVAT set with s→θ, u→ω0, a→α. Coasting from ω0 to zero in a known time fixes α at once; the angle swept follows, and dividing it by 2π converts the angle into a revolution count.
Formula α = (ω − ω0)/t, θ = ω0t + ½αt², N = θ/2π. These hold only for constant α, which the problem states outright.
Solution
1. Convert the initial rate: ω0 = 100 rev/min = 100/60 = 1.667 rev/s = 2π(1.667) = 10π/3 = 10.47 rad/s.rev/min must become rev/s (or rad/s) before any SI equation is used, since t is given in seconds.
2. Final state: the motor comes to rest, so ω = 0 at t = 15 s.
3. α = (ω − ω0)/t = (0 − 10π/3)/15 = −2π/9 = −0.698 rad/s² (negative = deceleration).
4. θ = ω0t + ½αt² = (10π/3)(15) + ½(−2π/9)(225) = 50π − 25π = 25π rad.
5. N = θ/2π = 25π/2π = 12.5 revolutions.Dividing by 2π is the only place the "revolution" unit enters; skipping it leaves the bogus number 78.5.
6. Cross-check with the average rate: N = ½(n0 + n)t = ½(1.667 + 0)(15) = 12.5 rev — identical.For constant α the mean angular velocity is exactly (ω0+ω)/2.

Concept With constant angular acceleration the ω–t graph is a straight line, so the angle swept is simply the area under that line — a trapezium of parallel sides ω0 and ω and width t. That area gives θ in radians; the question asks for complete revolutions, so the very last operation must be a division by 2π.

Solution
1. List the data in SI: ω0 = 10π rad/s, ω = 30π rad/s, t = 8 s, α constant.

6. Option scan: (a) 80 ✓; (b) 120 and (d) 200 fit no consistent slip; (c) 160 is the trap — it is θ = 160π rad misread as a revolution count.

5A point on a rotating wheel covers an angular displacement given by: θ = 4t² − t³. Find the angular acceleration of the point at the end of the second second (in SI units). — (Level-5)Angular acceleration from θ(t)Angular position of a point on the rim of a rotating wheel theta = 4t - 3t^2 + t^3 | angular acceleration🟡 Similar−4 rad/s²
Concept Here θ is handed over as an explicit function of time, so the rotation is not uniformly accelerated and no SUVAT-style formula may be used. Angular velocity and angular acceleration are defined as successive time derivatives of θ, so the entire problem is two differentiations followed by one substitution.
Formula ω = dθ/dt and α = dω/dt = d²θ/dt². These definitions hold for any θ(t) whatever; the constant-α equations are only the special case θ = ω0t + ½αt², which this cubic is not.
Solution
1. Given θ = 4t² − t³ in SI units (θ in rad, t in s).
2. Differentiate once: ω = dθ/dt = 8t − 3t² rad/s.
3. Differentiate again: α = dω/dt = 8 − 6t rad/s².α still contains t, confirming the acceleration is non-uniform — a single "α value" only exists at a stated instant.
4. "At the end of the second second" means the instant t = 2 s.The n-th second ends at t = n; it is the interval 1 s → 2 s, so its end is t = 2 s, not t = 1 s.
5. Substitute: α(2) = 8 − 6(2) = 8 − 12 = −4 rad/s².
6. Interpret the sign: ω(2) = 8(2) − 3(4) = 4 rad/s is still positive, so the wheel spins the same way but is slowing. Negative α means deceleration, not reversal — ω itself turns negative only at t = 8/3 s.

1If a body is moving in a circle of radius r with a constant speed v m/s, its angular velocity is: (a) v²/r (b) vr (c) v/r (d) r/vv = ωrIf a body is moving in a circle of radius r with a constant speed v, its angular velocity is🟢 Exactcv/r
Concept Linear speed and angular speed are two descriptions of the same motion, bridged by the geometry of a circle. In a short time dt the particle covers an arc ds while the radius sweeps an angle dθ, and the very definition of the radian ties those together as ds = r dθ. Dividing that identity by dt converts the arc relation into a speed relation.
Formula Radian definition: s = rθ (θ in radians); differentiating, v = ωr, hence ω = v/r. s = rθ is an identity only when θ is measured in radians — in degrees an extra π/180 would appear and v = ωr would fail.
Solution
1. Let the particle move on a circle of radius r; in time dt it travels arc length ds and the radius turns through dθ.
2. By the radian definition, ds = r dθ, with r constant for a circular path.r sits outside the derivative only because the path is a circle; on a spiral an extra term would appear.
3. Divide by dt: ds/dt = r(dθ/dt), i.e. v = ωr.
4. Rearrange for the asked quantity: ω = v/r rad/s — and sensibly, at fixed v a bigger circle is swept more slowly in angle.
5. Unit check: v/r = (m/s)/m = s−1 = rad/s ✓, since the radian is dimensionless.Only one option can carry s−1, so the units alone settle the question without any derivation.
6. Eliminate the rest: (a) v²/r = m/s² is the centripetal acceleration ac, not ω; (b) vr = m²/s is meaningless here; (d) r/v = s is a time, in fact 1/ω.

Concept Angular velocity measures how much angle is swept per second, exactly as ordinary speed measures how much distance is covered per second. One full revolution is the largest angle that closes back on itself, 2π radians, and the object takes 4 s to sweep it. Since nothing suggests the rate varies, the motion is uniform and the average rate is the instantaneous rate, so a single division finishes the problem.
Formula ω = Δθ/Δt, and for one complete revolution Δθ = 2π in a time equal to the period T, giving ω = 2π/T = 2πn. Radians are dimensionless, so ω carries the unit s−1, written rad/s to remind you it is an angle rate and not a frequency in Hz.
Solution

2. Apply the definition: ω = Δθ/Δt = 2π/4 = π/2 rad/s ≈ 1.57 rad/s.
