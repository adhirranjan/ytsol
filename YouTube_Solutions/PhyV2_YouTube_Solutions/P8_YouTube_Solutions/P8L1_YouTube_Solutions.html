1In which of the following is the work done by the mentioned force negative? The work done by (a) the tension of the cable while the lift is ascending (b) the gravitational force when a body slides down an inclined plane (c) the applied force in maintaining uniform motion of a block on a rough horizontal surface (d) the gravitational force when a body is thrown up — (Level-1)Sign of work, angle F–SWork done by a man lifting a bucket out of a well — sign of work🟡 Similardthe gravitational force when a body is thrown up
Concept Work is a scalar whose sign is carried entirely by cos θ, θ being the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector. Nothing about "helping" or "resisting" in a loose sense matters — you only have to ask: does the force have a component along the displacement (positive), opposite to it (negative), or none at all (zero)? Screening four options this way takes about ten seconds and needs no numbers.
Formula W = F·S = F S cos θ — F in N, S in m, W in J (= N·m), θ dimensionless. N × m = J on both sides, and cos θ is a pure number, so the equation is dimensionally clean. Sign rule: 0° ≤ θ < 90° ⇒ W > 0; θ = 90° ⇒ W = 0; 90° < θ ≤ 180° ⇒ W < 0.
Solution
1. Option (a) — lift ascending: the cable tension T points up, the displacement is up ⇒ θ = 0°, cos 0° = +1 ⇒ W > 0. Rejected.The cable is what raises the lift, so it must be feeding energy in.
2. Option (b) — body slides down an incline: gravity mg points down; the displacement is down the slope, i.e. it has a downward component. θ = (90° − incline angle) < 90° ⇒ cos θ > 0 ⇒ W > 0. Rejected.The block speeds up going down, and gravity is the only force doing that — so gravity's work must be positive.
3. Option (c) — applied force keeping a block in uniform motion on a rough floor: the applied force is along the motion (it is what overcomes friction) ⇒ θ = 0° ⇒ W > 0. Rejected.Careful: the net work is zero here (uniform velocity ⇒ ΔKE = 0), but the question asks about the applied force alone, and its work is +FS, exactly cancelled by friction's −FS.
4. Option (d) — body thrown up: gravity mg points down while the displacement is up ⇒ θ = 180°, cos 180° = −1 ⇒ W = −mgh < 0. This is the negative one.Consistent with energy bookkeeping: the body slows down on the way up, so the only force acting must be draining its kinetic energy.

       T ↑              ↘ S                F →                  ↑ S
       S ↑          mg ↓  (θ<90°)          S →              mg ↓ (θ=180°)
   θ = 0°           cos θ > 0           θ = 0°              cos θ = −1
   W = +TS          W = +mgh            W = +FS             W = −mgh   <-- NEGATIVE
⚡ JEE Trick Reduce every "sign of work" option to one question: is the force helping the displacement or fighting it? A force fights the displacement only when the body is slowing down because of it or being raised against it. Trap: option (c) tempts because the net work in uniform motion is zero — but "zero" is not "negative", and the question names the applied force, not the net force. Second trap: (b) reads like "gravity opposing a slide", but a body sliding down is going the way gravity pulls.
Answer: (d) the gravitational force when a body is thrown up — W = −mgh.

Concept The word "constrained to move along the z-axis" is the whole question: it tells you the displacement vector has only a k̂ component. In a dot product, each axis contributes independently, so the î and ĵ parts of the force — which are perpendicular to the motion — contribute exactly nothing. Students who compute |F⃗| = √14 and multiply by 4 are answering a question that was not asked.
Formula W = F⃗ · S⃗ = FxSx + FySy + FzSz — each F component in N, each S component in m, W in J. Every product is N·m = J, so summing them is legal and the result is in joules. Here S⃗ = 4k̂ m because the constraint kills Sx and Sy.
Solution
1. Write the force components: Fx = −1 N, Fy = +2 N, Fz = +3 N.
2. Write the displacement: the body moves 4 m along z only, so S⃗ = 0î + 0ĵ + 4k̂ m ⇒ Sx = 0, Sy = 0, Sz = 4 m.This is where the constraint enters; the guide/rail supplies whatever sideways force is needed, and that constraint force is perpendicular to the motion so it does no work either.
3. Take the dot product term by term:
  W = (−1)(0) + (2)(0) + (3)(4) = 0 + 0 + 12
4. Therefore W = 12 J.Positive, because Fz and Sz are both positive — the force pushes the body the way it is being made to go.

3A cyclist comes to a skidding stop in 10 m. During this process, the force on the cycle due to the road is 200 N and is directly opposed to the motion. (i) How much work does the road do on the cycle? (ii) How much work does the cycle do on the road? — (Level-3)Negative work, Newton's third lawA cyclist comes to a skidding stop in 10 m … force 200 N (NCERT)🟢 Exact(i) −2000 J  (ii) Zero
Concept Part (i) is a routine W = FS cos 180° computation. Part (ii) is the conceptual sting: Newton's third law guarantees the cycle pushes the road with an equal and opposite 200 N, yet the road does not move — its point of application undergoes zero displacement. Work needs both a force and a displacement of the thing the force acts on, so a third-law pair does not in general do equal and opposite work.
Formula W = F S cos θ — F in N, S in m, W in J. For (i): F = 200 N, S = 10 m, θ = 180° (force "directly opposed to the motion"). For (ii): Sroad = 0 m ⇒ W = F × 0 = 0 J. Both give N·m = J; the second is zero not because the force is zero but because the displacement factor is.
Solution
1. (i) Work done by the road on the cycle. The road's force on the cycle is friction, 200 N, pointing backwards; the cycle's displacement is 10 m forwards ⇒ θ = 180°.
2. Substitute: W = (200 N)(10 m)(cos 180°) = (200)(10)(−1) = −2000 J.Negative work means energy leaves the cycle — exactly the kinetic energy it had, which is why it stops. By the work–energy theorem the cyclist + cycle must have started with 2000 J of KE.
3. (ii) Work done by the cycle on the road. By Newton's third law the cycle pushes the road forwards with 200 N.
4. But the road (rigidly attached to the Earth) does not move: S = 0 m.
5. W = (200 N)(0 m) = 0 J.This is why "action and reaction do equal and opposite work" is FALSE as a general statement — it holds only when the two contact points share the same displacement.

      ┌──────┐
      │cycle │
      └──┬───┘
   f = 200 N ◂── (road on cycle, backwards)
  ══════╧═══════════════════ road (does NOT move,  S = 0)
        │
        ▾ 200 N (cycle on road, forwards — third-law partner)

  (i)  W_road→cycle = 200 × 10 × cos180° = −2000 J
  (ii) W_cycle→road = 200 × 0            =     0 J
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question pairs "work done by A on B" with "work done by B on A", check the displacement of each body separately — the forces are equal and opposite, the displacements usually are not. Anything in contact with the ground/Earth has S ≈ 0, so its work is 0. Trap: answering +2000 J for (ii) by blindly negating (i). Second trap: reading the 200 N as the cyclist's braking effort and trying to find the mass — the mass is never needed.
Answer: (i) −2000 J  ·  (ii) Zero.

5. Money = 100 × 10 paise = 1000 paise.

1If a number of forces act on a body and the body is in static or dynamic equilibrium, then: (a) work done by individual forces must be zero (b) net work done is +ve (c) net work done is −ve (d) net work done is zero — (Level-1)Equilibrium ⇒ zero net workWork Energy Theorem | Work, Energy | Mechanics Lecture 1🟡 Similardnet work done is zero
Concept "Equilibrium" — static or dynamic — means ΣF⃗ = 0, hence a = 0, hence the speed never changes, hence ΔKE = 0. The work–energy theorem then forces the net work to be zero. The distractor (a) is the classic confusion between "the sum of the works is zero" and "each work is zero" — individual forces can and usually do work, they just cancel in total.
Formula Equilibrium: ΣF⃗ = 0 (N). Work–energy theorem: W_net = ΔKE = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i² — m in kg, v in m/s, W in J. Also directly W_net = (ΣF⃗)·S⃗ = 0⃗ · S⃗ = 0. kg·(m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = N·m = J ✓ — the theorem is dimensionally an energy statement.
Solution
1. Static equilibrium: body at rest, ΣF⃗ = 0. Dynamic equilibrium: body moving at constant velocity, also ΣF⃗ = 0.Both are covered by the same condition; only the constant value of v differs (zero or non-zero).
2. Because the forces are constant along the path, the net work is W_net = (ΣF⃗)·S⃗. With ΣF⃗ = 0⃗, this is 0 J for any displacement S⃗.
3. Independent check with the theorem: v is constant ⇒ ½mv² is constant ⇒ ΔKE = 0 ⇒ W_net = 0 ✓.
4. Why (a) fails: take a block dragged at constant speed on a rough floor. The applied force does +FS, friction does −FS. Neither individual work is zero, yet they sum to zero.This single counter-example is enough to kill option (a) in an exam.
   dynamic equilibrium (constant v):

        f = −F ◂──┌──────┐──▸ F        ΣF = 0
                  │  m   │
                  └──────┘
        ────────────────────────▸ S

   W_applied = +F S        (individual works NOT zero)
   W_friction= −F S
   ---------------------------------
   W_net     =   0  J       ⇔  ΔKE = 0   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Convert "equilibrium" into "ΔKE = 0" the moment you read it — that phrase answers every net-work question instantly, static or dynamic. Trap: option (a); "net zero" never implies "each zero". Watch for the reverse trick too — a question may state ΔKE = 0 and ask you to conclude equilibrium, which is not valid (a body thrown up and caught back has ΔKE = 0 without ever being in equilibrium).
Answer: (d) net work done is zero.
2A weightlifter lifts a weight off the ground and holds it up: (a) work is done in lifting as well as holding the weight (b) no work is done in both lifting and holding the weight (c) work is done in lifting the weight but no work is required to be done in holding it up (d) no work is done in lifting the weight but work is required to be done in holding it up — (Level-1)Zero displacement ⇒ zero workIn which case will work done be zero — a weight-lifter while holding a weight🟢 Exactcwork is done in lifting the weight but no work is required to be done in holding it up
Concept This is the textbook illustration that physics work ≠ everyday effort. Holding a barbell overhead is exhausting — your muscle fibres repeatedly contract and relax, burning chemical energy — but the barbell does not move, so S = 0 and the mechanical work on it is exactly zero. During the lift, however, the applied force and the displacement are both upward, so real positive work is done.
Formula W = F S cos θ — F in N, S in m. Lifting phase (raised through height h at essentially constant speed): F = mg (N), S = h (m), θ = 0° ⇒ W = mgh (J). Holding phase: F = mg but S = 0 m ⇒ W = mg × 0 = 0 J. kg·(m/s²)·m = J ✓ for the lift; the hold is zero because of the metre factor, not the newton factor.
Solution
1. Phase 1 — lifting. The weightlifter applies an upward force at least equal to mg while the weight rises through h.
2. θ between force and displacement = 0°, so W = mgh cos 0° = +mgh > 0. Work is done.Equivalently, the gravitational potential energy of the weight has increased by mgh, and that energy had to come from the lifter.
3. Phase 2 — holding. The force is still mg upward, but the weight is stationary: S = 0 m.
4. W = (mg)(0) = 0 J, whatever the value of mg and however long he holds it.Time does not appear anywhere in W = FS cos θ — a 1-second hold and a 1-hour hold both give zero mechanical work.
5. Combine: work done in lifting, none in holding ⇒ option (c).

       │ ▓▓ │  ↑ S = h                 │ ▓▓ │    S = 0

   (biological energy IS spent while holding — but not as

⚡ JEE Trick The single test for zero work is "did the point of application move?" — not "was it tiring?". The same logic answers: pushing a wall (W = 0), carrying a suitcase on level ground (W = 0, force vertical ⊥ horizontal S), a satellite in a circular orbit (W = 0). Trap: option (a), which is what everyday language suggests; examiners rely on that instinct.
Answer: (c) work is done in lifting the weight but no work is required to be done in holding it up.
3When a body moves in a circular path, no work is done by the centripetal force since: (a) there is no net force (b) there is no displacement (c) force is always from the centre (d) force and displacement are perpendicular to each other — (Level-1)Centripetal force does no workWork Done by Centripetal Force in Circular Motion | Class 11 Physics🟢 Exactdforce and displacement are perpendicular to each other
Concept In circular motion the velocity is always tangential while the centripetal force is always radial (inward) — the two are perpendicular at every instant. Since the infinitesimal displacement dS⃗ is along v⃗, the dot product F⃗·dS⃗ vanishes at every point of the path, so the accumulated work is zero. The three wrong options each name a true-sounding fact that is either false or irrelevant.
Formula dW = F⃗ · dS⃗ = F dS cos θ — F in N, dS in m, dW in J. Centripetal force F_c = mv²/r (N) directed toward the centre; displacement dS⃗ = v⃗ dt directed tangentially ⇒ θ = 90° always ⇒ dW = F dS cos 90° = 0. kg·(m/s)²/m = kg·m/s² = N ✓ for F_c; the work integrates to 0 J because the integrand is identically zero, not because the limits coincide.
Solution
1. At any instant the particle's velocity v⃗ is tangent to the circle, so its displacement in time dt is dS⃗ = v⃗ dt, also tangential.
2. The centripetal force points along the inward radius, which is by definition perpendicular to the tangent.
3. Therefore θ = 90° and dW = F dS cos 90° = 0 at every point.
4. Integrating over any arc: W = ∫ dW = 0 J. The centripetal force does no work.Consistent with the fact that in uniform circular motion the speed — and hence the kinetic energy — never changes, so W_net must be zero.
5. Reject (a): there is a net force (that is what bends the path). Reject (b): there is displacement (the body moves along the arc). Reject (c): the force is toward the centre, and in any case direction alone does not make the work zero.

5In order to do work: (a) force must act at any angle to the displacement (b) force must not be normal to displacement (c) must act along the direction of displacement (d) must act normal to the direction of displacement — (Level-1)Condition for non-zero workIs Work Positive, Negative, or Zero?🟡 Similarbforce must not be normal to displacement
Concept This is a precision-of-language question. W = FS cos θ is non-zero for every θ except 90°. So the necessary-and-sufficient condition is simply "θ ≠ 90°", i.e. the force must not be normal to the displacement. Option (c) states a sufficient but far too strong condition (θ = 0° only), and option (a) is self-defeating because "any angle" includes 90°.
Formula W = F S cos θ — F in N, S in m, W in J. W ≠ 0 ⇔ F ≠ 0, S ≠ 0 and cos θ ≠ 0 ⇔ θ ≠ 90°. cos θ is the only factor that can vanish while F and S are non-zero, and it does so only at θ = 90°.
Solution

Concept Two completely different energy accounts must be compared. Sliding costs friction × distance — energy irretrievably lost as heat. Tipping costs only the rise of the centre of mass to the highest point of the flip, because after the block passes over the diagonal it falls back down and gravity returns that energy. Since a 2 m square flipped through 90° advances exactly 2 m, the two options move the block the same distance and can be compared directly.
Formula Sliding: W_slide = μ m g d — μ dimensionless, m in kg, g in m/s², d in m, W in J. Tipping about a corner of a square of side a: the CM is at the centre, a distance r = (a/2)√2 = a/√2 from the pivot, so the CM rises from a/2 to a/√2:
