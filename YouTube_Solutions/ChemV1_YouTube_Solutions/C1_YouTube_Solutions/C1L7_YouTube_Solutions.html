1At fixed T, what does the area under the Maxwell–Boltzmann speed-distribution curve represent?Distribution curve meaningA-MAXWELL🟡 SimilarcFraction of gas molecules having all possible speeds (total probability = 1)
Concept The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve plots the fraction of molecules (per unit speed interval) against molecular speed. Because every molecule in the gas must have some speed between 0 and ∞, if you add up the fractions over all speeds you must recover the whole population. On the graph "adding up over all speeds" is the same as taking the area under the curve, so that area is the total fraction — which is exactly 1 (100% of the molecules).
Formula The y-axis is dN/N per du = fraction of molecules per unit speed; the area of a thin strip is (dN/N per du) × du = dN/N. Summing all strips gives ∫₀^∞ (dN/N) = N/N = 1, i.e. total probability = 1.
Solution
1. Interpret the axes: height = fraction of molecules at a given speed, width = a small speed range du.Height × width = the fraction of molecules whose speed lies in that little band.
2. Add the areas of every band from u = 0 to u = ∞ (i.e. integrate).Every molecule is counted exactly once because all possible speeds are covered.
3. The sum of all fractions = 1 = 100% of molecules, so the whole area under the curve represents the total fraction of molecules over all speeds.
4. Match to options: this is "fraction of gas molecules having all possible speeds (total probability = 1)" — option (c). It is not the number at one speed, nor the average speed, nor the pressure.
⚡ JEE Trick Area under any normalized distribution curve = 1 (total probability). Raising T spreads and flattens the curve, but the enclosed area stays fixed at 1 — the molecules are only redistributed, never created or lost. Trap: the peak height is the fraction at the most probable speed, not the total — don't confuse the tallest point with the whole area.
Answer: (c) Fraction of gas molecules having all possible speeds (total probability = 1).
2Which correctly shows the change in the distribution when T increases T₁→T₂ (T₂>T₁)?Effect of temperatureA-TEMP🟡 SimilarbThe peak moves to higher speed and becomes shorter
Concept Temperature controls the spread of molecular speeds. Heating a gas gives every molecule more kinetic energy, so the whole population shifts to higher speeds — the peak (the most probable speed) slides to the right. But the total area under the curve is fixed at 1 (all the molecules are still there), so if the distribution stretches out sideways it must get shorter: the curve flattens and broadens as it moves right.
Formula ump = √(2RT/M), so ump ∝ √T. Raising T raises the most probable speed, pushing the peak toward larger u; conservation of total probability (area = 1) then forces the peak height down.
Solution
1. Increase T from T₁ to T₂ (T₂ > T₁): each molecule speeds up because average KE ∝ T.More thermal energy means the typical molecule moves faster.
2. The most probable speed ump = √(2RT/M) rises with √T, so the peak of the curve moves to a higher speed (rightward).
3. Area under the curve must stay = 1 (molecule count unchanged), so as the curve widens it must lower — the peak becomes shorter.Same total area spread over a wider speed range ⇒ smaller maximum height.
4. Both effects together = "peak moves to higher speed and becomes shorter" = option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick "Heat ⇒ peak right & down, curve broadens; area unchanged." Memorise the pairing: rightward shift always comes with a shorter, flatter peak because probability is conserved. Trap: options claiming the peak grows taller as T rises violate the fixed-area rule — reject them on sight.
Answer: (b) The peak moves to higher speed and becomes shorter.
3Two gases A & B at same T, MA=4MB — ratio ump,A:ump,BSpeed ↔ molar massA-SPEEDCALC🟡 Similara1 : 2
Concept At a fixed temperature, molecular speed depends only on molar mass: heavier molecules move slower. The most probable speed follows ump = √(2RT/M), so at the same T the constant 2RT is shared and speed varies as 1/√M. Comparing two gases therefore reduces to comparing the square roots of their molar masses — the numbers 2, R, T all cancel in a ratio.
Formula ump = √(2RT/M) ⇒ at same T, ump,A / ump,B = √(MB / MA). Same T means 2RT is common, so it cancels; only the molar masses remain, inverted under the root. Units: M in kg mol⁻¹, but any consistent unit works since it is a ratio.
Solution
1. Write the ratio from the formula (same T ⇒ 2RT cancels): ump,A/ump,B = √(MB/MA).Speed is inversely proportional to √M, so the lighter gas appears in the numerator of the mass ratio.
2. Substitute the given relation MA = 4MB: MB/MA = MB/(4MB) = 1/4.
3. Take the square root: ump,A/ump,B = √(1/4) = 1/2.√(1/4) = 1/2, so A (the heavier gas) is half as fast as B.
4. Therefore ump,A : ump,B = 1 : 2 — option (a).
⚡ JEE Trick Same T ⇒ speed ratio = inverse square root of the mass ratio. A 4× heavier gas is exactly ½ as fast (√4 = 2); a 9× heavier gas is ⅓ as fast. Trap: the heavier gas A goes with the larger denominator, so its speed is the smaller one — don't flip the ratio and write 2 : 1.
Answer: (a) 1 : 2.
4Gases X (400 K, M=20) & Y (M=50) — T of Y so ump curves coincideump ↔ T & MA-SPEEDCALC🟡 Similard1000 K
Concept Two gases have the same most probable speed only when their speed-setting ratio T/M is equal. Since ump = √(2RT/M), the constants 2R are shared and the peaks "coincide" precisely when T/M matches for both gases. So we don't need the actual speed — just set (T/M) for X equal to (T/M) for Y and solve for the unknown temperature.
Formula ump = √(2RT/M); equal ump ⇒ TX/MX = TY/MY. Equal speeds ⇒ equal T/M, because 2R cancels; solve for TY = TX · MY/MX.
Solution
1. Set the T/M ratios equal (condition for coinciding ump curves): TX/MX = TY/MY.
2. Substitute knowns: TX = 400 K, MX = 20, MY = 50 ⇒ 400/20 = TY/50.
3. Simplify the left side: 400 ÷ 20 = 20, so 20 = TY/50.
4. Multiply both sides by 50: TY = 20 × 50 = 1000 K.Y is 2.5× heavier than X, so it needs 2.5× the temperature (400 × 2.5 = 1000 K) to reach the same peak speed.

5300 K, M=28 gives urms; at what T does M=7 gas have same uav as that urms?Mixed speed relationA-SPEEDCALC🟡 Similard88 K
Concept This mixes two different speed types. First find the target value — the rms speed of an M = 28 gas at 300 K. Then ask: at what temperature does a lighter M = 7 gas have an average speed equal to that number? Because urms and uav use different numerical factors (3 vs 8/π), you must square both, keep those factors, and solve for the unknown T. The trick is that R cancels when you equate the two squared speeds.
Formula urms = √(3RT/M) and uav = √(8RT/πM). Setting uav(M=7, T) = urms(M=28, 300) and squaring: 8T/(π·7) = 3·300/28. R cancels from both sides; only the numeric factors, temperatures and molar masses remain.
Solution
1. Write the equality of the two speeds: √(8RT/(π·7)) = √(3R·300/28).The M = 7 gas's average speed must equal the M = 28 gas's rms speed.
2. Square both sides and cancel R: 8T/(π·7) = 3·300/28.
3. Evaluate the right side: 3 × 300 = 900; 900 ÷ 28 = 32.14.
4. Evaluate the coefficient of T on the left: 8 ÷ (π × 7) = 8 ÷ 21.99 = 0.3638.
5. Solve: 0.3638 · T = 32.14 ⇒ T = 32.14 ÷ 0.3638 = 88.3 K ≈ 88 K.The M = 7 gas is lighter and uav carries the smaller factor (8/π ≈ 2.55 vs 3), so a much lower temperature suffices to match the rms speed.
 target : u_rms(M=28, 300 K)²  = 3·R·300/28  = R·32.14
 want   : u_av (M=7,  T)²      = 8·R·T/(π·7) = R·0.3638·T
 equate : 0.3638·T = 32.14  ⇒  T = 88 K
⚡ JEE Trick When two different speed types are set equal, keep only their numeric factors (urms→3, uav→8/π, ump→2) and the T/M pairs — R always cancels. Square first, then solve linearly for T. Trap: don't equate 3RT/M with 8RT/πM using the same factor; the 3 vs 8/π difference is the whole point.
Answer: (d) 88 K.

1Derive a relation between density, molar mass and pressure of a gasDensity–M–P relationA-DENSITY🟡 Similar—
Concept The ideal-gas law connects pressure, volume and amount, but "amount" (moles) can be rewritten in terms of things we can measure directly — mass and density. By replacing the mole count n with mass ÷ molar mass, and then mass ÷ volume with density, the gas law turns into a compact relation linking pressure, density and molar mass. This is the working formula behind vapour-density and gas-identification problems.
Formula Start from PV = nRT with n = m/M and ρ = m/V. Result: ρ = PM/RT (equivalently M = ρRT/P). P in Pa, ρ in kg m⁻³, M in kg mol⁻¹, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹K⁻¹, T in K.
Solution
1. Write the ideal-gas law: PV = nRT.
2. Replace the mole count by mass over molar mass, n = m/M: PV = (m/M)RT.One mole weighs M grams, so m grams is m/M moles.
3. Rearrange to gather m/V on one side: P = (m/V)(RT/M).Divide both sides by V; the group m/V is the density ρ.
4. Substitute ρ = m/V: P = ρRT/M, hence ρ = PM/RT.Density rises with pressure and molar mass, and falls as temperature rises.
 PV = nRT ,  n = m/M ,  ρ = m/V
 PV = (m/M)RT
 P  = (m/V)(RT/M) = ρ(RT/M)
 ⇒  ρ = PM/RT      (and  M = ρRT/P)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise ρ = PM/RT and its rearrangement M = ρRT/P — the latter instantly gives an unknown molar mass from a measured density. At fixed T and P, ρ ∝ M, so a denser gas is the heavier one. Trap: keep units SI (P in Pa, ρ in kg m⁻³, M in kg mol⁻¹) or the numbers will be off by powers of ten.
Key point: from PV = nRT with n = m/M and ρ = m/V, the density–molar-mass–pressure relation is ρ = PM/RT (⇔ M = ρRT/P).
220 L holds 0.650 mol He at 37 °C, 628.3 bar — new P if V→12 L, T→177 °C, +1.25 mol addedIdeal gas equationA-IDEALGAS🟡 Similar—
Concept When a gas changes state, the ideal-gas law lets us compare the two states with a single combined ratio: PV/nT is a constant (= R) for an ideal gas, so P₁V₁/(n₁T₁) = P₂V₂/(n₂T₂). Rearranged, the new pressure is the old pressure scaled by the ratio of moles, the ratio of temperatures and the inverse ratio of volumes. Temperatures must be in kelvin, and any gas added increases n.
Formula P₂ = P₁ · (n₂/n₁) · (T₂/T₁) · (V₁/V₂). Direct in n and T (more moles or higher T raise P), inverse in V (smaller volume raises P). Convert °C → K: 37 °C = 310 K, 177 °C = 450 K.
Solution
1. Convert temperatures: T₁ = 37 + 273 = 310 K; T₂ = 177 + 273 = 450 K.Gas laws require absolute temperature.
2. Update the mole count: n₁ = 0.650 mol; adding 1.25 mol gives n₂ = 0.650 + 1.25 = 1.90 mol.
3. Assemble the three scaling factors:n₂/n₁ = 1.90/0.650 = 2.923; T₂/T₁ = 450/310 = 1.452; V₁/V₂ = 20/12 = 1.667.
4. Multiply the factors: 2.923 × 1.452 = 4.243; 4.243 × 1.667 = 7.072.
5. Scale the original pressure: P₂ = 628.3 × 7.072 = 4.44 × 10³ bar (≈ 4443 bar).All three changes (more moles, hotter, compressed) push pressure up, so the large multiplier is expected.

⚡ JEE Trick For any "before → after" gas problem, skip re-deriving n: use P₂ = P₁·(n₂/n₁)(T₂/T₁)(V₁/V₂) and just plug ratios — R never appears. Remember P is direct in n and T, inverse in V. Trap: forgetting to add the extra 1.25 mol to n, and using °C instead of K, are the two classic errors here.
Key point: comparing states with P₂ = P₁(n₂/n₁)(T₂/T₁)(V₁/V₂) and n₂ = 1.90 mol, T in K, gives P₂ ≈ 4.44 × 10³ bar.

Concept Counting molecules is a two-stage conversion: the ideal-gas law first turns the measured P, V, T into moles, and Avogadro's number then turns moles into molecules. Concept Molecules in a gas do not all move at one speed — they span a whole distribution. The speed shared by the largest number of molecules is the one sitting at the very peak of the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, and that speed has a special name: the most probable speed ump. "Attained by the majority" points to where the curve is tallest, i.e. the mode of the distribution.
Formula ump = √(2RT/M) — the speed at the maximum of the distribution. It is the smallest of the three characteristic speeds: ump < uav < urms, in the fixed ratio 1 : 1.128 : 1.224.
Solution
1. Read the curve: its height at any speed = fraction of molecules with that speed.The tallest point marks the speed most molecules have.
2. "Speed of the majority" = the peak of the curve = the most probable speed by definition.
3. That is option (b). The average speed and rms speed lie slightly to the right of the peak, so neither is the modal speed.
⚡ JEE Trick Peak of the Maxwell curve = most probable speed (the smallest of the trio). Order to memorise: ump < uav < urms (1 : 1.128 : 1.224). Trap: "majority / peak / maximum" all point to ump; don't reflexively answer "average," which is a slightly larger value not at the peak.

5The values ump, uav, urms have the ratio …Speed ratioA-RATIO🟡 Similarc1 : 1.128 : 1.224
Concept The three characteristic speeds all equal (a constant) × √(RT/M); they differ only in the leading constant under the root — 2 for ump, 8/π for uav, 3 for urms. Because RT/M is common, their ratio is just the ratio of √2 : √(8/π) : √3, a fixed number independent of gas or temperature.
