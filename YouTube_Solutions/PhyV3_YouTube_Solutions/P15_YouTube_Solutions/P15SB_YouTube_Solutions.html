In a surface tension experiment with a capillary tube water rises upto 0.1 m. If the same experiment is repeated in an artificial satellite, which is revolving around the earth; water will rise in the capillary tube upto a height of: (a) 0.1 m (b) 0.2 m (c) 0.98 m (d) full length of tube
P15SBV7Q1Capillary rise in weightlessness (geff = 0)
🟢 ExactIn a surface tension…
dfull length of tube
Concept Capillary rise is a balance between the upward surface-tension pull along the contact line and the weight of the raised column. In a freely orbiting satellite everything is in free fall, so the effective gravity is zero and nothing stops the climb.
Formula Jurin’s law h = 2T cosθ / (rρgeff); in orbit geff = g − a = 0, so h → ∞ and only the tube length limits the rise.
Solution
1. On Earth: h = 2T cosθ/(rρg) = 0.1 m, so 2T cosθ/(rρ) = 0.1g.
2. In an orbiting satellite the tube, the beaker and the water all fall freely together, so geff = 0.
3. Weight of a raised column = ρ(πr2h)geff = 0 for any h, so the upward force 2πrT cosθ is never balanced by weight.
4. Water therefore keeps rising until it reaches the top end of the tube.
5. It does not overflow: at the top the meniscus flattens (its radius of curvature grows), so the net surface force falls to zero and the liquid simply stops at the rim.
6. Height of water = full length of the tube.
⚡ JEE Trick Free fall / satellite = geff 0 ⇒ tube fills completely, never spills. Lift accelerating up ⇒ geff = g + a ⇒ h decreases.
Answer: (d) full length of tube

A capillary tube (A) is dipped in water. Another identical tube (B) is dipped in a soap-water solution. Which of the following shows the relative nature of the liquid columns in the two tubes? (a)–(d): four sketches of the liquid columns in A and B (see book figure)
📝 PYQ: AIEEE 2008Solution ↗
P15SBV7Q2Capillary rise: water vs soap solution
🟢 ExactA capillary tube (A)…
cBoth rise; water in A rises higher than soap solution in B
Concept Both liquids wet clean glass (contact angle about 0°), so both rise with a concave meniscus. The height of rise is proportional to the surface tension, and soap lowers the surface tension of water sharply.
Formula h = 2T cosθ / (rρg) ∝ T; r, θ and (almost) ρ are the same for both tubes, so only T decides the height.
Solution
1. Tubes A and B are identical, so r is the same; both liquids are essentially water, so ρ is nearly the same.
2. Both wet glass (θ ≈ 0°): both columns rise and both menisci are concave.
3. T(water) ≈ 0.072 N/m; soap is a surfactant and drops it to about 0.025–0.035 N/m.
4. hB/hA = Tsoap/Twater ≈ 0.03/0.072 ≈ 0.4.
5. So B also rises, but only to well under half the height of A.
6. The sketch with a tall column in A and a shorter (non-zero) column in B is option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Same tube, same θ ⇒ h ∝ T. Detergent/soap lowers T, so it rises less but still rises; mercury would be the one that dips.
Answer: (c) water (A) rises higher than soap solution (B)

In the siphon shown in the figure the ends A and B of the tube are at same horizontal level. Water fills the entire tube but it does not flow out of the end B. With the help of a diagram show how the water surface at end B changes if the end B were slightly lower than the position shown.
P15SBV7Q3Siphon held by surface tension; curvature at the open end
🟡 SimilarSurface Tension of W…
Surface at B bulges out (convex) more — its radius of curvature decreases
Concept Water at the open end B is held in by its own curved surface. The excess pressure inside the water at B must be balanced by the Laplace pressure 2T/R of the bulging surface. Lowering B raises the hydrostatic pressure there, so the surface must curve more sharply.
Formula Balance at B ρg y = 2T/R; y = depth of B below the free surface in the beaker, R = radius of curvature of the bulge at B.
Solution
1. Statics: the pressure just inside B equals the pressure at the same level in the beaker, P0 + ρg y.
2. Just outside B the pressure is P0, so the water surface at B must supply an excess pressure ρg y = 2T/R; it bulges outward (convex).
3. Lower B slightly by Δy: the excess becomes ρg(y + Δy), which is larger.
4. 2T/R must increase with it, so R decreases: the drop at B bulges out more (more hemispherical).
5. This continues until R reaches the tube radius r (a hemisphere). Beyond that 2T/R cannot grow any more, the surface breaks and water starts flowing out of B.
6. Diagram: at B draw a convex bulge that becomes more curved (smaller radius) as B is lowered.
7. Scale check: for a tube of radius 1 mm the bulge can hold at most 2T/r = 2(0.072)/10−3 = 144 Pa, so B can sit only about 144/(1000×9.8) ≈ 1.5 cm below the beaker surface before the siphon starts running.
⚡ JEE Trick Hanging drop at a tube end: depth up ⇒ radius down. The hemisphere (R = r) is the largest pressure a meniscus can hold; past it, flow starts.
Answer: the convex surface at B curves more — its radius of curvature decreases

The material of wire has specific gravity 8. If it is not wetted by water, what is the maximum diameter of the wire that will float on the surface of water? (T = 70 dyne/cm) (a) 0.75 mm (b) 1.5 mm (c) 1.5 cm (d) None of these
P15SBV7Q4Floating wire: weight vs surface-tension support
🟡 SimilarThe meterial of a wi…
b1.5 mm
Concept A non-wetted wire laid on water sits in a small dent of the surface. Surface tension acts along both edges of the wire; at most it can pull straight up on each edge, giving a maximum support of 2T per unit length.
Formula Per unit length (πd2/4)ρg = 2T ⇒ d = √(8T/(πρg)); two contact lines, each pulling up with at most T per metre.
Solution
Given wire of specific gravity 8, not wetted by water, floating on water; T = 70 dyne/cm
Find the maximum diameter of the wire (MCQ)
1. Specific gravity 8 ⇒ ρ = 8×103 kg/m3. T = 70 dyne/cm = 0.07 N/m.
2. Weight per metre of wire = (πd2/4)ρg.
3. Largest upward surface force per metre = 2T (both sides, surface turned vertical).
4. Floating limit: (πd2/4)ρg = 2T ⇒ d2 = 8T/(πρg).
5. d2 = 8(0.07)/(π×8000×9.8) = 0.56/2.463×105 = 2.27×10−6 m2.
6. d = 1.51×10−3 m ≈ 1.5 mm.
7. Trap check: (c) 1.5 cm is 10 times wider, so it weighs 100 times more per metre (weight ∝ d2), far beyond the 2T = 0.14 N/m the surface can give. (a) 0.75 mm would float, but it is not the maximum.
⚡ JEE Trick Floating needle/wire: support per length is 2T, so dmax = √(8T/πρg). Buoyancy of the tiny dent is neglected.
Answer: (b) 1.5 mm

Two soap bubbles of radii r1 and r2 are attached as shown. Find the radius of curvature of the common film ACB. (r1 > r2)
P15SBV7Q5Double bubble: radius of common interface
🟢 ExactRADIUS OF INTERFACE…
r = r1r2 / (r1 − r2)
Concept The smaller bubble has the higher inside pressure. The common film separates the two gases, so its own Laplace jump 4T/r must equal the difference of the two inside pressures. The film bulges into the larger bubble.
Formula P2 − P1 = 4T/r; a soap film has two surfaces, so each film gives 4T/(radius).
Solution
Given two soap bubbles of radii r₁ and r₂ (r₁ > r₂) attached with a common film ACB
Find the radius of curvature of the common film ACB
1. Large bubble: P1 = P0 + 4T/r1.
2. Small bubble: P2 = P0 + 4T/r2; since r2 < r1, P2 > P1.
3. Common film ACB: P2 − P1 = 4T/r.
4. So 4T/r = 4T/r2 − 4T/r1 ⇒ 1/r = 1/r2 − 1/r1.
5. r = r1r2/(r1 − r2), bulging from the small bubble into the large one.
6. Check: equal bubbles (r1 = r2) give r → ∞, a flat wall, as expected.
⚡ JEE Trick Interface of two bubbles: 1/r = 1/rsmall − 1/rbig — same shape as a lens/mirror formula. Equal bubbles ⇒ flat wall.
Answer: r = r1r2/(r1 − r2)

A soap bubble of radius R is surrounded by another soap bubble of radius 2R, as shown. Take, surface tension = S. Then, the pressure inside the smaller soap bubble, in excess of the atmospheric pressure, will be (a) 4S/R (b) 3S/R (c) 6S/R (d) None of these
P15SBV8Q1Concentric soap bubbles: stacked excess pressure
🟢 ExactA soap bubble of rad…
c6S/R
Concept Going inward through each soap film adds a Laplace jump of 4S/(radius), because a film has two surfaces. Add the jumps across the outer film and then the inner film.
Formula Pin − P0 = 4S/(2R) + 4S/R; one 4S/r jump per film, crossed from outside in.
Solution
Given soap bubble of radius R surrounded by a larger soap bubble of radius 2R; surface tension S
Find the excess pressure inside the smaller bubble (MCQ)
1. Outside the big bubble: P0.
2. Cross the outer film (radius 2R): Pgap = P0 + 4S/(2R) = P0 + 2S/R.
3. Cross the inner film (radius R): Pin = Pgap + 4S/R.
4. Pin = P0 + 2S/R + 4S/R = P0 + 6S/R.
5. Excess over atmosphere = 6S/R.
6. Option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Nested bubbles: just add 4S/r for every film you cross (2S/r for a single liquid surface).
Answer: (c) 6S/R

A soap bubble is being blown on a tube of radius 1 cm. The surface tension of the soap solution is 0.05 N/m and the bubble makes an angle of 60° with the tube as shown. The excess of pressure over the atmospheric pressure in the tube is: (a) 1 Pa (b) 5 Pa (c) 10 Pa (d) 20 Pa
P15SBV8Q2Bubble cap on a tube: radius from contact angle
🟡 SimilarFluid 10 || Surface…
c10 Pa
Concept The bubble is a spherical cap sitting on the tube rim. The angle it makes with the tube fixes the sphere’s radius R from the tube radius r; then the excess pressure is the soap-film value 4T/R.
Formula r = R sinα, ΔP = 4T/R; α = angle between the sphere’s radius to the rim and the tube axis.
Solution
Given soap bubble blown on a tube of radius 1 cm; T = 0.05 N/m; bubble makes 60° with the tube
Find the excess pressure over atmospheric pressure in the tube (MCQ)
1. The film leaves the rim at 60° to the (vertical) tube wall, so its tangent there is 60° from vertical.
2. The sphere’s radius to the rim is perpendicular to that tangent, so it makes α = 30° with the tube axis.
3. Rim point: r = R sin30° = R/2 ⇒ R = 2r = 2 cm = 0.02 m.
4. Soap film, two surfaces: ΔP = 4T/R.
5. ΔP = 4(0.05)/0.02 = 10 Pa.
6. Trap check: if the film met the rim at 90° to the wall (a hemisphere), R = r = 1 cm and ΔP = 4T/r = 20 Pa, which is option (d). That is the largest pressure a bubble on this tube can ever have; at 60° the cap is flatter, so the pressure is lower.
7. Option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Radius ⊥ tangent: angle with the wall θ ⇒ R = r/cosθ. Here R = 1/cos60° = 2 cm.
Answer: (c) 10 Pa

By inserting a capillary tube upto depth ℓ in water, the water rises to a height h. If the lower end of the capillary is closed inside water and the capillary is taken out and closed end opened, to what height water will remain in the tube? (Here ℓ > h) (a) Zero (b) ℓ + h (c) 2h (d) h
P15SBV8Q3Capillary taken out: column held by two menisci
🟢 ExactWater rises to a hei…
c2h
Concept Out of the water, the column has a free surface at both ends. The top meniscus (concave) holds up a height h, and the drop formed at the bottom end (convex downward) can hold up another h. So at most 2h stays; the rest drips out.
Formula ρg Hmax = 2T/r + 2T/r ⇒ Hmax = 2h; h = 2T/(rρg) for one meniscus (θ = 0).
Solution
Given capillary inserted to depth ℓ in water, water rises to height h; lower end closed inside water, tube lifted out and opened (ℓ > h)
Find the height to which water remains in the tube (MCQ)
1. Taken out with the bottom closed, the tube carries a column of length ℓ + h.
2. Open the bottom. Top meniscus: pressure just below it is P0 − 2T/r.
3. Bottom: a convex drop forms; pressure just inside it is P0 + 2T/r.
4. Hydrostatics: (P0 + 2T/r) − (P0 − 2T/r) = ρgH ⇒ H = 4T/(rρg) = 2h at most.
5. Since ℓ > h, the column ℓ + h > 2h, so water drips out until H = 2h.
6. Height remaining = 2h.
⚡ JEE Trick Two free menisci hold 2h. If the column were shorter than 2h it would all stay (bottom drop flattens).
Answer: (c) 2h

An air bubble of radius 3.5 μm is at a depth of 6 m below the water surface. Taking atmospheric pressure as 105 Pa, surface tension of water as 0.07 N/m, and density of atmospheric air as 1.2 kg/m3. Find the density (in kg/m3) of air inside the air bubble.
P15SBV8Q4Air density inside a deep, tiny bubble
🟡 SimilarWhat would be the ex…
2.4 kg/m3
Concept The air inside the bubble is at atmospheric pressure plus the water head plus the Laplace pressure of its single surface. At the same temperature, gas density is proportional to pressure.
Formula Pin = P0 + ρwgh + 2T/r, ρair ∝ P; an air bubble in water has one surface: 2T/r, not 4T/r.
Solution
Given air bubble of radius 3.5 μm at depth 6 m; P₀ = 10⁵ Pa, T = 0.07 N/m, air density outside 1.2 kg/m³
Find the density of the air inside the bubble
1. Hydrostatic: ρwgh = 1000×10×6 = 6×104 Pa.
2. Laplace: 2T/r = 2(0.07)/(3.5×10−6) = 4×104 Pa.
3. Pin = 105 + 6×104 + 4×104 = 2×105 Pa.
4. Same temperature: ρ/P = constant (PV = nRT).
5. ρin = 1.2 × (2×105/105) = 2.4 kg/m3.
6. Note how large 2T/r is for a micron-sized bubble: it adds as much as 4 m of water.
7. Leaving out surface tension would give P = 1.6×105 Pa and ρ = 1.92 kg/m3, which is wrong. For bubbles this small the Laplace term is not a correction; it is a quarter of the total pressure.
⚡ JEE Trick Air bubble in liquid → 2T/r; soap bubble in air → 4T/r. Isothermal gas: density scales with pressure.
Answer: 2.4 kg/m3

A soap bubble of radius r is formed inside another soap bubble of radius R (> r). The atmospheric pressure is P0 and surface tension of the soap solution is T. Calculate change in radius of the smaller bubble if the outer bubble bursts. Assume that the excess pressure inside a bubble is small compared to P0.
P15SBV8Q5Inner bubble expands when outer bursts (Boyle)
🟢 ExactA soap bubble of rad…
Δr = 4Tr / (3P0R) (increase)
Concept When the outer bubble bursts, the inner bubble loses the 4T/R pressure step that was squeezing it, so it expands isothermally. With small excess pressures, Boyle’s law linearises to ΔP/P = −3Δr/r.
Formula P r3 = const ⇒ ΔP/P0 = −3Δr/r; volume ∝ r3, and P ≈ P0 because the excess pressures are small.
Solution
Given soap bubble of radius r inside another of radius R > r; atmospheric pressure P₀, surface tension T
Find the change in radius of the smaller bubble when the outer bubble bursts
1. Before: Pi = P0 + 4T/R + 4T/r.
2. After the outer bursts: Pf = P0 + 4T/r′ ≈ P0 + 4T/r (the change in 4T/r is second order).
3. So the inside pressure falls by ΔP = −4T/R.
4. Boyle: P r3 = constant ⇒ ΔP/P + 3Δr/r = 0, with P ≈ P0.
5. −4T/(RP0) + 3Δr/r = 0 ⇒ Δr = 4Tr/(3P0R).
6. Positive: the inner bubble grows slightly.
⚡ JEE Trick Small-change Boyle for a sphere: ΔP/P = −3Δr/r. Lose a pressure step ⇒ the bubble swells.
Answer: Δr = 4Tr/(3P0R), an increase

There is an air bubble of radius R inside a drop of water of radius 3R. Find the ratio of gauge pressure at point A (inside the air bubble) to the gauge pressure at point B (in the water).
P15SBV9Q1Air bubble inside a drop: stacked gauge pressures
🟢 ExactThere is an air bubb…
4
Concept Gauge pressure is measured from the outside air. Moving inward, the drop’s outer surface adds 2T/(3R) to the water, and the bubble’s single water–air surface adds a further 2T/R to the air inside it. Hydrostatic differences in a small drop are negligible.
Formula PB = 2T/(3R), PA = 2T/(3R) + 2T/R; each is a single liquid surface, so 2T/r, not 4T/r.
Solution
Given air bubble of radius R inside a water drop of radius 3R
Find the ratio of gauge pressure at A (inside the bubble) to that at B (in the water)
1. Outside: atmospheric, gauge 0.
2. Water (point B): crossing the drop surface of radius 3R gives PB = 2T/(3R).
3. Air bubble (point A): crossing the bubble surface of radius R adds 2T/R.
4. PA = 2T/(3R) + 2T/R = 2T/(3R) + 6T/(3R) = 8T/(3R).
5. PA/PB = (8T/3R)/(2T/3R) = 4.
6. Ratio = 4.
⚡ JEE Trick Work outside-in and add one Laplace jump per surface you cross. Single liquid surface 2T/r, soap film 4T/r.
Answer: 4

The radii of the two columns in U-tube are r1 and r2. When a liquid of density ρ (angle of contact is 0°) is filled in it the level difference of liquid in two arms is h. The surface tension of liquid is: (g = acceleration due to gravity) (a) ρghr1r2/(2(r2 − r1)) (b) ρgh(r2 − r1)/(2r1r2) (c) 2(r2 − r1)/(ρghr1r2) (d) ρgh/(2(r2 − r1))

🟡 SimilarThe internal radius…
aρghr1r2 / 2(r2 − r1)
Concept Both limbs are open to the air. Just under each concave meniscus the pressure is P0 − 2T/r, so the narrower limb has the lower pressure and its column stands higher. The level difference is set by the difference of the two Laplace pressures.
Formula ρgh = 2T/r1 − 2T/r2; r1 < r2, contact angle 0 so cosθ = 1.

2. Wide limb (r2): P0 − 2T/r2.

4. ρgh = 2T(r2 − r1)/(r1r2).
5. T = ρghr1r2/[2(r2 − r1)].

A soap bubble of radius r and surface tension T is given a potential of V volt. If the new radius R of the bubble is related to its initial radius by equation, P0[R3 − r3] + λT[R2 − r2] − (1/2)ε0V2R = 0, where P0 is the atmospheric pressure. Then, find λ.
P15SBV9Q4Charged soap bubble: electrostatic pressure + Boyle
🟡 SimilarA soap bubble (surfa…
λ = 4
Concept Charging the bubble adds an outward electrostatic pressure σ2/(2ε0) on the film, so it swells. The trapped air expands isothermally (Boyle), and the new radius satisfies a pressure balance across the two-surface film.
Formula Pgas + σ2/(2ε0) = P0 + 4T/R; σ = ε0V/R for a sphere at potential V.
Solution
1. Initially (uncharged): Pgas = P0 + 4T/r.
2. Isothermal: Pgas′ = (P0 + 4T/r)(r/R)3.
3. Charged: V = σR/ε0 ⇒ σ = ε0V/R, outward pressure σ2/2ε0 = ε0V2/(2R2).
4. Why outward and why the 1/2: the field is σ/ε0 just outside and 0 inside, so the charge feels the average σ/2ε0. The force per area is σ×σ/2ε0, and like charges repel, so it pushes outward whatever the sign of V.
5. Balance: (P0 + 4T/r)r3/R3 + ε0V2/(2R2) = P0 + 4T/R.
6. Multiply by R3: P0r3 + 4Tr2 + (1/2)ε0V2R = P0R3 + 4TR2.
7. P0(R3 − r3) + 4T(R2 − r2) − (1/2)ε0V2R = 0 ⇒ λ = 4.
⚡ JEE Trick The 4 is just the soap-film 4T/R. Electrostatic pressure on any charged conductor surface = σ2/2ε0, always outward.
Answer: λ = 4

A glass prism has its principal section in form of an equilateral triangle of side length ℓ. The length of the prism is L (see fig.). The prism, with its base horizontal, is supported by a vertical spring of force constant k. Half the slant surface of the prism is submerged in water. Surface tension of water is T and contact angle between water and glass is 0°. Density of glass is d and that of water is ρ (< d). Calculate the extension in the spring in this position of equilibrium.
P15SBV9Q5Prism on a spring: weight, buoyancy, surface tension
🟢 ExactA glass prism has it…
x = (1/k)[(√3/4)ℓ2Ldg − (3√3/16)ℓ2Lρg + √3TL + Tℓ]
Concept Three vertical forces besides the spring: the weight down, buoyancy up on the submerged part, and surface tension pulling down along the whole water line. With contact angle 0°, the water surface pulls along the glass face, into the water.
Formula kx = W − B + FST; the spring carries whatever the water does not.
Solution
Given glass prism with equilateral principal section of side ℓ, length L, on a vertical spring of constant k; half the slant surface submerged in water; T, contact angle 0°, glass density d, water density ρ (< d)
Find the extension of the spring at equilibrium
1. Weight: W = (√3/4)ℓ2L d g.
2. Half of each slant face is under water, so the water line is at half the height. The dry top part is a triangle of side ℓ/2 with area (1/4) of the whole.
3. Submerged area = (3/4)(√3/4)ℓ2 = (3√3/16)ℓ2, so B = (3√3/16)ℓ2Lρg.
4. Long contact lines: two lines of length L on faces inclined at 60°. The pull T per metre acts down the face, vertical part T sin60°, giving 2TL(√3/2) = √3TL.
5. End faces (vertical): each water line is ℓ/2 long and pulls straight down, giving 2T(ℓ/2) = Tℓ.
6. kx = (√3/4)ℓ2Ldg − (3√3/16)ℓ2Lρg + √3TL + Tℓ.
⚡ JEE Trick Contact angle 0° ⇒ surface pulls along the solid face into the liquid: take the face-direction component (sin60° for the slant faces, full T for vertical faces).
Answer: x = (1/k)[(√3/4)ℓ2Ldg − (3√3/16)ℓ2Lρg + √3TL + Tℓ]

Concept Work out the free rise in each material, then ask where the meniscus ends up. The meniscus sits in whichever section it reaches first; if the column reaches the joint but the upper material cannot support that height, the water stops at the joint.

Given cylindrical capillary of radius 0.2 mm made by joining T₁ (contact angle 0°) and T₂ (60°); dipped in water in case I and case II; T = 0.075 N/m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s²
Find which of the options (a)–(d) on the water heights in case I and case II are correct (multiple correct)
1. 2T/(rρg) = 2(0.075)/(2×10−4×104) = 0.075 m: T1 (0°) gives 7.5 cm, T2 (60°) gives 3.75 cm.
2. (a) Case II, T2 below, joint at 5 cm: water reaches 3.75 cm inside T2, below the joint. True.
3. (b) Case I, T1 below, joint at 8 cm: water reaches 7.5 cm inside T1, below the joint. True.
4. (c) The meniscus weight correction depends on the meniscus shape (0° hemispherical vs 60° flatter cap), and the menisci in (a) and (b) are in different materials. True.

Concept Read every statement straight off Jurin’s law. In a lift accelerating upward, effective gravity increases.
Formula h = 2σ cosθ/(rρgeff); geff = g + a in a lift accelerating up.
Solution
1. (a) h ∝ 1/r: larger r gives smaller h. True.
2. (b) h ∝ σ, so it depends on σ. False.
3. (c) Lift accelerating up: geff = g + a > g, so h decreases. True.
4. (d) h ∝ cosθ, not θ. False.
5. Correct statements: (a) and (c).
6. Contrast with L7-1: in free fall geff = 0 and the tube fills completely.
