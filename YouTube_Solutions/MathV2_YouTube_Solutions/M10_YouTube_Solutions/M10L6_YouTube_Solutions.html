Concept A quadratic f(x) = ax² + bx + c with real roots α, β factorises as f(x) = a(x − α)(x − β). Multiplying through by a gives a·f(x) = a²(x − α)(x − β), and because a² > 0 the sign of a·f(k) is decided purely by (k − α)(k − β). So a·f(k) reports whether k lies outside the roots (positive) or between them (negative), no matter which way the parabola opens. Locating both roots on one side of 2 therefore begins with the sign of a·f(2).
Formula a·f(k) = a²(k − α)(k − β). The a² factor removes the ambiguity of the leading sign, so one single inequality covers upward and downward parabolas alike.
Solution
1. Both roots greater than 2 means α > 2 and β > 2, hence 2 − α < 0 and 2 − β < 0.
2. Their product (2 − α)(2 − β) = (negative)(negative) > 0, so a·f(2) = a²(2 − α)(2 − β) > 0 — the blank is 0.
3. This is only one leg of the standard trio; the full condition for both roots > 2 is D = b² − 4ac ≥ 0, a·f(2) > 0 and −b/(2a) > 2. D ≥ 0 is what makes the roots real at all — a complex pair cannot be compared with 2.
4. The vertex leg is indispensable: a·f(2) > 0 also holds when both roots are smaller than 2, and only −b/(2a) = (α + β)/2 > 2 separates those two pictures.
5. Test f(x) = x² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4): a·f(2) = 1·(4 − 14 + 12) = 2 > 0, −b/(2a) = 3·5 > 2, D = 49 − 48 = 1 ≥ 0. All three hold and indeed both roots 3, 4 exceed 2.
6. Flip the sign: g(x) = −x² + 7x − 12 has the same roots and a·g(2) = (−1)(−2) = 2 > 0 again, confirming the criterion is leading-coefficient free.

And never stop at the sign test alone — without −b/(2a) > 2 the very same inequality is satisfied by a parabola whose two roots both lie to the left of 2.

Concept A quadratic is continuous, so it changes sign only by crossing the x-axis. If exactly one of its two roots is captured inside (k₁, k₂), the graph enters the strip on one side of the axis and leaves on the other, forcing f(k₁) and f(k₂) to carry opposite signs. Conversely opposite signs guarantee an odd number of crossings inside, and a quadratic has at most two — so odd means exactly one, making the product test a complete criterion.
Formula exactly one root in (k₁, k₂) ⇔ f(k₁)·f(k₂) < 0. Multiplying by a² changes nothing, so a·f(k₁) and a·f(k₂) must also have opposite signs — leading coefficient irrelevant.
Solution
1. Factorise: f(x) = 3x² + 5x + 2 = (3x + 2)(x + 1), so the roots are α = −1 and β = −2/3, with a = 3 > 0 and D = 25 − 24 = 1 > 0.
2. Suppose exactly one root, say β, satisfies k₁ < β < k₂ while α is outside. Then k₁ lies on one side of β and k₂ on the other, so f(k₁) and f(k₂) sit on opposite sides of the axis.
3. Therefore f(k₁)·f(k₂) < 0, and the blank is 0.
4. Algebraically f(k₁)f(k₂) = a²(k₁ − α)(k₁ − β)(k₂ − α)(k₂ − β); exactly one endpoint being inside the root interval makes exactly one of the four bracket-products negative. Two roots inside would flip the sign back to positive, which is why the criterion is exclusive to the "one root" case.
5. Check k₁ = −0·8, k₂ = 0: f(−0·8) = 3(0·64) − 4 + 2 = −0·08 < 0 and f(0) = 2 > 0, product < 0; indeed only −2/3 lies in (−0·8, 0). The root −1 is safely outside the strip.
6. Contrast k₁ = −2, k₂ = 0: f(−2) = 12 − 10 + 2 = 4 > 0 and f(0) = 2 > 0, product > 0 — here both roots are inside, and that case is detected by the trio a·f(k₁) > 0, a·f(k₂) > 0, k₁ < −b/2a < k₂, D ≥ 0. Positive product is ambiguous: it means zero or two roots inside.

Concept Every location-of-roots condition is assembled from the same three ingredients: D ≥ 0 so the roots exist, the sign of a·f at each boundary point to say whether it is inside or outside the root interval, and the vertex −b/(2a) = (α + β)/2 to say which side the roots collect on. Formula a·f(k) = a²(k − α)(k − β),   −b/(2a) = (α + β)/2,   D = b² − 4ac. The vertex is the midpoint of the roots, so it always lies inside [α, β] and fixes the left/right ambiguity.
Solution
1. (A) both roots < k: k is outside the roots ⇒ a·f(k) > 0; the roots collect to the left of k, so their midpoint obeys −b/(2a) < k; and D ≥ 0 for existence. That is exactly (q).
2. (B) k between the roots: (k − α) and (k − β) have opposite signs, so a·f(k) < 0 — matching (s). No D or vertex clause is needed: a negative value forces two distinct real crossings by itself.
3. (C) both roots inside (k₁, k₂): each endpoint is outside the roots, so a·f(k₁) > 0 and a·f(k₂) > 0; the midpoint must be caught in the strip, k₁ < −b/(2a) < k₂; plus D ≥ 0 — that is (r).

Concept For x² + 7x + 10 = 0 the coefficients are small integers, so the fastest route is to split the middle term rather than run the quadratic formula. We need two numbers whose product is c = 10 and whose sum is b = 7; both must be positive, and 2 × 5 = 10 with 2 + 5 = 7 does it. Once factorised, the roots are read off directly, and the sum can be cross-checked against Vieta’s relation −b/a without any arithmetic on the roots themselves.
Formula α + β = −b/a,   αβ = c/a. Vieta gives the sum instantly from the coefficients, which is the independent check that the factorisation is right.
Solution
1. D = b² − 4ac = 7² − 4(1)(10) = 49 − 40 = 9 > 0, a perfect square, so the roots are real, distinct and rational. A perfect-square D is the signal that factorisation over the integers will succeed.
2. Split the middle term: x² + 7x + 10 = x² + 2x + 5x + 10 = x(x + 2) + 5(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x + 5).
3. Setting each factor to zero gives the roots x = −2 and x = −5, so p = −2 and q = −5.
4. Sum of roots r = p + q = (−2) + (−5) = −7, which matches Vieta: −b/a = −7/1 = −7. Two independent routes to the same number confirm no sign slip.
5. Product check: pq = (−2)(−5) = 10 = c/a = 10/1 — consistent, so the factorisation is certainly correct.
6. Sign reading without solving: c/a = 10 > 0 forces the roots to share a sign, and −b/a = −7 < 0 makes that shared sign negative. Both roots negative was predictable straight from the coefficients.
7. The labelling p = −2, q = −5 follows the book’s order; since the equation is symmetric in its roots, p = −5, q = −2 is equally valid and r = −7 either way.

Concept Whether "one root common" can stay one root depends entirely on the nature of the roots. Test the discriminant of x² + 2x + 7 = 0 first: a negative D makes the roots a complex-conjugate pair, and a polynomial with real coefficients cannot own one member of such a pair without the other. So "a root common" is silently upgraded to "both roots common", forcing the two quadratics to be proportional.
Formula both roots common ⇒ p/a₁ = q/b₁ = r/c₁. Two quadratics with the same root pair differ only by a non-zero scalar multiple, since a monic quadratic is determined by its roots.
Solution
1. For x² + 2x + 7 = 0, D = b² − 4ac = 2² − 4(1)(7) = 4 − 28 = −24 < 0, so its roots are the complex pair α = −1 + i√6 and ᾱ = −1 − i√6.
2. Let α be the common root of px² + qx + r = 0. Conjugating that equation and using p̄ = p, q̄ = q, r̄ = r (they are integers) gives pᾱ² + qᾱ + r = 0. Real coefficients commute with conjugation, so the conjugate root is forced in.
3. Hence both α and ᾱ satisfy px² + qx + r = 0, i.e. the two quadratics share their entire root set and must be proportional: p/1 = q/2 = r/7 = λ.
4. So (p, q, r) = (λ, 2λ, 7λ) with λ ≠ 0, and λ must be an integer for p, q, r to be integral. λ = 0 is rejected because px² + qx + r = 0 would cease to be an equation with roots at all.
5. The smallest positive integral choice is λ = 1, giving p = 1, q = 2, r = 7 — the second equation is simply the first one repeated.
6. Verify: 1·α² + 2α + 7 = 0 holds by construction, and the pair (1, 2, 7) has gcd 1, so no smaller positive integral triple exists. Any λ with |λ| ≥ 2 only scales the triple upward.

The domain matters as much as the algebra: |x| sits in a denominator, so x = 0 is barred from the outset, and that single exclusion is what separates the correct option from its nearest rival.
Formula |u/v| = |u|/|v|, (x+1)² = |x+1|², and |x+1| = 1 + |x| ⇔ x = |x|. Squaring is safe in the last step because both sides are already non-negative, so no extraneous root can be introduced.
Solution
1. Domain: |x| appears in a denominator, so x ≠ 0 throughout.
2. Rewrite both sides in terms of |x+1|: LHS = |x+1|/|x| + |x+1|, RHS = |x+1|²/|x|.
3. Multiply through by |x| > 0: |x+1| + |x+1|·|x| = |x+1|², hence |x+1|·(1 + |x| − |x+1|) = 0. Multiplying by a strictly positive quantity cannot create or destroy solutions.
4. First branch |x+1| = 0 gives x = −1, which is in the domain, so it is a genuine solution.
5. Second branch |x+1| = 1 + |x|; squaring gives x² + 2x + 1 = 1 + 2|x| + x² ⇒ 2x = 2|x| ⇒ x = |x| ⇒ x ≥ 0, and x ≠ 0 leaves x > 0. x = |x| is precisely the statement that x is non-negative.
6. So the solution set is {x : x > 0} ∪ {−1}, which is option (b).
7. Spot-checks: x = 1 gives 2 + 2 = 4 and 4/1 = 4 ✓; x = −1 gives 0 + 0 = 0 ✓; x = −2 gives 1/2 + 1 = 3/2 against 1/2 ✗. The failure at x = −2 is what rules out option (d).

⚡ JEE Trick Option (a) is the trap: it is the same ray but written closed at zero, and x = 0 makes the original expression undefined, so it must be dropped. Option (c) keeps only the two obvious integers and loses the entire ray, while option (d) fails already at x = −2. The efficient route is to resist the four-case sign split and factor out |x + 1| instead — one factorisation replaces a page of casework, and the isolated solution x = −1 falls out of it automatically.
Answer: (b) {x | x > 0} ∪ {−1}.

Concept There is a world of difference between “holds for all x” and “holds for at least one x”. The first is about the whole parabola and needs the discriminant; the second needs one witness point. So bring everything to one side and evaluate the quadratic at a convenient place instead of hunting for roots. If it is already negative on the boundary of the required region, continuity supplies a whole interval of witnesses inside it.
Formula f(x) = (a²+3)x² + (a+2)x − 6; f(0) < 0 with lead > 0 ⇒ roots straddle 0. Product of roots = c/lead, so a negative constant term with a positive leading coefficient forces one root on each side of the origin.
Solution
1. Bring the 2 across: (a²+3)x² + (a+2)x − 4 < 2 ⇔ f(x) = (a²+3)x² + (a+2)x − 6 < 0, and we need this at some x < 0.
2. Leading coefficient a² + 3 ≥ 3 > 0 for every real a, so f is always a genuine upward-opening parabola. There is no degenerate linear case to check, unlike parameters that sit alone in front of x².
3. f(0) = −6 < 0, a value not involving a at all.
4. Product of the roots = −6/(a²+3) < 0, so the roots are real, distinct and of opposite sign: x₁ < 0 < x₂. A negative product also silently confirms D > 0, so no discriminant computation is needed.
5. An upward parabola is negative exactly between its roots, so f < 0 on (x₁, x₂), which contains the whole stretch (x₁, 0) of negative numbers.
6. Every such x is a negative witness, so the inequality holds for at least one negative x whatever a may be: a ∈ (−∞, ∞).
7. Concrete checks: a = 0 and x = −1 give 3 − 2 − 6 = −5 < 0; a = 100 and x = −0.01 give 1.0003 − 1.02 − 6 < 0.
