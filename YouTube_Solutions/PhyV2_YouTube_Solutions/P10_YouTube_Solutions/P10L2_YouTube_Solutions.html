1Which of the following equations represents the angular displacement in circular motion with uniform acceleration? (a) ω = ω0 + αt (b) θ = ω0t + ½αt² (c) ω² = ω0² + 2αθ (d) ac = v²/r — (Level-1)Angular kinematic equationsEquation of Motion for a body in Rotational Motion for JEE and NEET Physics🟡 Similarbθ = ω0t + ½αt²
Concept Rotation with constant angular acceleration α mirrors straight-line motion with constant a: swap s→θ, u→ω0, v→ω, a→α. There are exactly three such equations, each answering a different question. We need the one giving angular displacement θ as a function of time.
Formula ω = ω0 + αt, θ = ω0t + ½αt², ω² = ω0² + 2αθ, got by integrating α = dω/dt, then ω = dθ/dt, then eliminating t. All three need α constant; none survives a time-varying α.
Solution
1. α = dω/dt ⇒ ω = ω0 + αt — angular velocity vs time, true but not what is asked → (a) out.
2. Integrate again: θ = ∫ωdt = ∫(ω0 + αt)dt = ω0t + ½αt² — θ on the left, only t on the right.Rotational twin of s = ut + ½at², so it inherits the displacement-vs-time role.
3. Put t = (ω − ω0)/α into step 2 ⇒ ω² = ω0² + 2αθ: it holds θ, but as a function of ω, not of time → (c) out.
4. (d) ac = v²/r contains neither α nor t.A constraint on direction change, valid even at constant speed — not an equation of motion.
5. Dimension check: ω0t = (rad/s)(s) = rad and ½αt² = (rad/s²)(s²) = rad — both terms are angles.
6. Hence θ = ω0t + ½αt² → option (b).
  linear (a = const)      |   angular (alpha = const)
  ------------------------+---------------------------
  v = u + a t             |   w = w0 + alpha t
  s = u t + 1/2 a t^2     |   TH = w0 t + 1/2 alpha t^2
  v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s       |   w^2 = w0^2 + 2 alpha TH
  ------------------------+---------------------------
  asked: displacement vs time -> row 2 -> option (b)
⚡ JEE Trick (a) and (c) are not false — they answer the wrong question, the classic "all true, one relevant" trap. Match the stem's output (θ) and input (t) to the equation's two ends. (d) is the odd one out: it survives even when α = 0.
Answer: (b) θ = ω0t + ½αt².
2A circular disc of radius R is rotating about its axis O with a uniform angular velocity ω rad/s as shown in the fig. The magnitude of the relative velocity of point A relative to point B on the disc is (A and B both lie on the rim, the radii OA and OB subtending an angle θ). — (Level-2)Relative velocity of two points on a discRelative velocity of two points on a rigid body with some angular velocity🟡 Similar2Rω sin(θ/2)
Concept A rigid disc has one angular velocity: every particle turns through the same angle in the same time, so ω is common to A and B. Both lie on the rim, so both move with speed ωR, each velocity tangential, i.e. perpendicular to its own radius. Turning both radii by 90° leaves the angle between them unchanged, so the velocity vectors are separated by the same angle θ that OA and OB subtend. Relative velocity is then the difference of two equal vectors inclined at θ.
Formula v = ωr for a rigid body, and |vA − vB| = √(vA² + vB² − 2vAvBcosθ). The identity 1 − cosθ = 2sin²(θ/2) collapses the equal-magnitude case to one sine.
Solution
1. Rigidity ⇒ one ω, and both points are at radius R, so vA = vB = ωR in magnitude.
2. vA ⊥ OA and vB ⊥ OB, so the angle between the velocities equals the angle between the radii = θ.A common 90° rotation of both vectors cannot change the angle between them.
3. |vAB|² = (ωR)² + (ωR)² − 2(ωR)²cosθ = 2ω²R²(1 − cosθ).
4. With 1 − cosθ = 2sin²(θ/2): |vAB|² = 4ω²R²sin²(θ/2) ⇒ |vAB| = 2Rω sin(θ/2).
5. Cross-check: vA − vB = ω × (rA − rB) = ω × AB, of magnitude ω·AB = ω(2R sin(θ/2)) — identical.AB is the chord subtending θ at the centre, so AB = 2R sin(θ/2).
6. Limits: θ = 0 gives 0 (same point); θ = 180° gives 2Rω, right for opposite ends of a diameter whose velocities are antiparallel.

3Fig. shows a circular path taken by a particle. If the instantaneous velocity of the particle is v = (2 m/s)î − (2 m/s)ĵ, through which quadrant is the particle moving when it is travelling (i) clockwise and (ii) counter clockwise around the circle? (Circle centred at the origin.) — (Level-3)Quadrant from the velocity vectorFigure shows a circular path taken by a particle. If the instantaneous velocity is (2 m/s)i - (2 m/s)j🟢 Exact(i) First quadrant (ii) Third quadrant
Concept On a circle centred at the origin the velocity is always tangential, hence perpendicular to the position vector r. So knowing v pins r to a single line through the centre perpendicular to v, which meets the circle at two diametrically opposite points. The sense of travel (clockwise or anticlockwise) is what breaks that 180° tie.
Formula v = ω × r with ω = +ωk̂ for anticlockwise and −ωk̂ for clockwise. Componentwise: anticlockwise v = ω(−y, x), clockwise v = ω(y, −x). k̂ × (x, y, 0) = (−y, x, 0): v is r turned a quarter-turn in the sense of the motion.
Solution
1. v = (2, −2) m/s: speed 2√2 ≈ 2.83 m/s, pointing at −45° (down-right).
2. v·r = 0 ⇒ 2x − 2y = 0 ⇒ y = x, so the particle sits on the line y = x — either the first-quadrant point or the third-quadrant point.Only two points of the circle have their radius perpendicular to this particular v.
3. Clockwise: v = ω(y, −x) ⇒ ωy = 2 ⇒ y > 0 and −ωx = −2 ⇒ x > 0. Both positive → first quadrant.
4. Anticlockwise: v = ω(−y, x) ⇒ −ωy = 2 ⇒ y < 0 and ωx = −2 ⇒ x < 0. Both negative → third quadrant.
5. Geometric check: anticlockwise means v is r turned +90°, so r is v turned −90°: (2, −2) → (−2, −2), third quadrant. Clockwise reverses it: (2, −2) → (2, 2), first quadrant.Rotating by −90° sends (x, y) to (y, −x); by +90° to (−y, x).
6. So (i) clockwise → first quadrant, (ii) anticlockwise → third quadrant.

4A particle is moving in a circular path. The acceleration and momentum vectors at an instant are a = 4î − ĵ m/s², P = 3î + 5ĵ kg m/s. The motion of the particle is: (a) uniform circular motion (b) circular motion with tangential acceleration (c) circular motion with tangential retardation (d) we cannot conclude the nature of motion from a and P at one instant only. — (Level-4)Sign of a·pCircular Motion 02 | Centripetal and Tangential Acceleration | Angular Acceleration | Class 11 Chap 4🟡 Similarbcircular motion with tangential acceleration
Concept Momentum P = mv with m > 0, so P points exactly along the velocity: it is the direction of motion. Split the acceleration into a part along v (which changes speed) and a part perpendicular to it (which bends the path). The sign of a·P therefore decides everything: positive means speeding up, negative means slowing down, zero means uniform speed.
Formula at = dv/dt = (a·v̂) = a·P/|P| and ac = √(|a|² − at²). d(v²)/dt = 2v·a, so the sign of a·v alone fixes whether the speed rises or falls.
Solution
1. a·P = (4)(3) + (−1)(5) = 12 − 5 = +7 (units kg·m²/s³) — strictly positive.
2. |P| = √(9 + 25) = √34 ≈ 5.83, so at = 7/√34 ≈ +1.20 m/s² along the motion ⇒ the speed is increasing.at is the projection of a on the direction of P, and a positive projection means dv/dt > 0.
3. |a| = √(16 + 1) = √17 ≈ 4.12, so ac = √(17 − 49/34) ≈ 3.94 m/s² ≠ 0 — a real centripetal part, so the path is genuinely curved.
4. Uniform circular motion demands a ⊥ v, i.e. a·P = 0; we got 7, so (a) is out.
5. Tangential retardation demands a·P < 0; we got +7, so (c) is out.Retardation would mean a leaning backwards against P, giving an obtuse angle between them.
6. (d) is wrong because the classification asked for is instantaneous: the sign of a·P at that instant is precisely the definition of speeding up. Hence (b).

5A particle moves on a circle of radius r with centripetal acceleration as function of time as ac = k²rt² where k is a positive constant. Find the following quantities as function of time at an instant: (i) the speed of the particle (ii) the tangential acceleration of the particle (iii) the resultant acceleration (iv) angle made by the resultant with tangential acceleration — (Level-5)ac = k²rt² — four derived quantitiesA particle moves on a circle of radius r with centripetal acceleration as function of time ac = k²rt²🟢 Exact(i) v = krt (ii) at = kr (iii) ares = kr√(k²t⁴ + 1) (iv) α = tan−1(kt²)
Concept On a circle the acceleration splits into two always-perpendicular pieces: ac = v²/r pointing at the centre, and at = dv/dt along the tangent. Here ac is given as a function of time, so the first relation is not a formula to apply but an equation to solve for v. Differentiating v(t) then gives at, and the two perpendicular legs build the right triangle that answers (iii) and (iv).
Formula ac = v²/r, at = dv/dt, ares = √(at² + ac²), tanφ = ac/at. Pythagoras is legal only because ac ⊥ at, and φ is measured from the tangential leg as the stem demands.
Solution
1. Equate the given law to the definition: v²/r = k²rt² ⇒ v² = k²r²t² ⇒ v = krt (positive root, since v is a speed).
2. Differentiate: at = dv/dt = d(krt)/dt = kr — a constant.k and r are constants, so the speed grows linearly — like uniformly accelerated straight-line motion.
3. ares = √(at² + ac²) = √(k²r² + k⁴r²t⁴) = kr√(k²t⁴ + 1).
4. tanφ = ac/at = k²rt²/(kr) = kt² ⇒ φ = tan−1(kt²).
5. Dimension check: ac = k²rt² forces [k] = T−2, so v = krt is LT−1, at = kr is LT−2, and kt² is dimensionless.
6. Limits: at t = 0 the motion is purely tangential (φ = 0); as t grows ac swamps at and φ → 90°.
   a_c = k^2 r t^2  (given, radial, inward)
   a_t = k r        (constant, tangential)
        a_res
          ^  /|
          | / | a_c = k^2 r t^2
          |/phi
          +-------> a_t = k r

⚡ JEE Trick Two traps. First, v = krt, not krt² — the square root must act on t² as well. Second, read which leg the angle is measured from: from the tangent it is tan−1(ac/at) = tan−1(kt²), while from the radius it would be its complement tan−1(1/kt²).

Calculate the time taken by it to stop assuming that the frictional torque is constant.Time to stop from a revolution countA fan is rotating at 90 rpm. Concept A constant frictional torque on a fixed moment of inertia means a constant angular retardation α = τ/I, so the fan is the rotational copy of a car braking uniformly to rest. We know the initial spin (90 rpm), the final spin (zero) and the total angle turned (21 revolutions), and we want the time — three knowns, one unknown, exactly what the uniform-α equations are for.
Formula ω = ω0 + αt, ω² = ω0² + 2αθ, and the average-rate form θ = ½(ω0 + ω)t. The last one is exact only for constant α, where the ω–t graph is a straight line and the mean rate is the mean of the endpoints.
Solution
1. Convert the spin: ω0 = 90 rpm = 90 × 2π/60 = 3π rad/s ≈ 9.42 rad/s; final ω = 0.
2. Convert the count: θ = 21 rev = 21 × 2π = 42π rad ≈ 131.9 rad.Every kinematic equation needs radians, because α is defined per radian, not per revolution.
3. ω² = ω0² + 2αθ ⇒ 0 = (3π)² + 2α(42π) ⇒ α = −9π²/84π = −3π/28 ≈ −0.337 rad/s².
4. ω = ω0 + αt ⇒ 0 = 3π − (3π/28)t ⇒ t = 28 s.
5. One-step cross-check: θ = ½(ω0 + 0)t ⇒ 42π = ½(3π)t ⇒ t = 28 s, and α never had to be found.Same answer by an independent route, so the radian conversions were consistent.
6. Revolution shortcut: 90 rpm = 1.5 rev/s, mean rate = 0.75 rev/s, t = 21/0.75 = 28 s — the 2π cancels, so no conversion is needed at all.

4. Finite form agrees: α = (ω2 − ω1)/t = 0 over any interval, 1 s or 6 hours.Interval-independence is why the result cannot depend on the hand's length.
5. So r = 0.015 m never enters: tip, mid-point and pivot share one ω and one α. Also at = αr = 0.

Concept On a bend the acceleration splits into two mutually perpendicular pieces: a radial (centripetal) part that only turns the velocity vector, and a tangential part that only changes its length. Because the radius is perpendicular to the tangent everywhere, the resultant is a Pythagorean sum, never an arithmetic one.
Formula ac = v²/r, at = dv/dt, a = √(ac² + at²), tanφ = at/ac. The last two hold only because ac ⊥ at; at any other angle the cosine rule would be needed.
Solution

3. The two are at 90°, so they combine in quadrature, not by addition.
4. a = √(ac² + at²) = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5 m/s².No cross term survives, because 2acatcos90° = 0.

Concept Jai rides a merry-go-round at constant ω; at t = 0 he is on the +x axis with his velocity along +y, so the rotation is anticlockwise and θ = ωt. Every quantity plotted is then a fixed function of ωt, and each option is settled by two cheap checks: its value at t = 0 and the sign of its slope just after.
Formula θ = ωt for constant ω, (x,y) = R(cosωt, sinωt); differentiating gives v = ωR(−sinωt, cosωt), and the net force is centripetal, F = −mω²r, so Fy = −mω²R sinωt.
Solution
1. The t = 0 data fix the sense: on +x with v along +y is anticlockwise, so x = R cosωt, y = R sinωt. Clockwise would give v along −y there.
2. vx = −ωR sinωt, vy = ωR cosωt; at t = 0 this is (0, ωR), along +y as required.
3. (a): Fy = −mω²R sinωt starts at 0 and turns negative at once — a clean −sin, exactly the plotted curve. F points at the centre, opposite to r.
4. (b): x = R cosωt must start at its maximum +R with zero slope, but the graph starts at 0 and goes negative. Wrong.
5. (c): constant ω ⇒ θ = ωt, a straight line from the origin of slope ω. Correct.
6. (d): vx = −ωR sinωt is the same function of time as Fy (in fact Fy = mωvx), so (d) can only be excluded on how its curve is drawn — the printed (d) has a short shallow negative lobe and a much larger positive hump, i.e. it is not a true sinusoid. The exclusion rests on the figure, not the physics.
