1Which statement correctly expresses Avogadro's Law?Avogadro's lawA-AVOG🟡 SimilarcAt the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules.
Concept Avogadro's law is a statement about counting, not weighing. It says that what fixes the volume of a gas (at a given temperature and pressure) is purely how many particles are present, never their individual mass or size. So two different gases — say light H₂ and heavy CO₂ — occupying equal volumes under the same T and P must contain the same number of molecules. This is why gas volumes can stand in for mole ratios in reactions.
Formula V ∝ n (at constant T, P), i.e. V = k·n where n = amount in mol and k depends only on T and P. Because k is the same for every gas at fixed T and P, equal V forces equal n, and equal n means equal molecule count (N = n·NA).
Solution
1. State the law precisely: at fixed T and P, volume is directly proportional to the number of molecules (moles).Molecular mass and identity drop out — only the count matters.
2. Test each option against this: a claim tying volume to mass or to type of gas contradicts the law.
3. The only statement that says "equal volumes ⇒ equal number of molecules (same T, P)" is option (c).
4. Consistency check: since N = n·NA and equal V ⇒ equal n, equal V must give equal N — exactly what (c) asserts.
 condition                 what is equal        Avogadro's law?
 same T, P, equal V        number of molecules  ✓ (option c)
 same T, P, equal mass     NOT molecule count   ✗
⚡ JEE Trick Read Avogadro's law as "equal volumes → equal molecules" at the same T and P — mass is irrelevant. So 1 L of H₂ and 1 L of CO₂ (same T, P) hold identical molecule counts even though CO₂ weighs 22× more. Trap: options that sneak in "equal mass" or "same density" are wrong — the law is about number, not weight.
Answer: (c) At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules.
2Identical conditions: 10 L of gas A = 0.5 mol; moles of gas B in 25 L?Avogadro's law (V ∝ n)A-AVOG🟡 Similarc1.25 mol
Concept Under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, Avogadro's law makes gas volume and amount directly proportional — the gas's identity is irrelevant. So gas A and gas B share the same "volume per mole." That single proportionality constant lets us scale from A's known volume-to-mole figure straight to B, just by ratio.
Formula V ∝ n ⇒ VA/nA = VB/nB (same T, P), so nB = nA × (VB/VA). V in litres, n in mol. Equal molar volume for both gases means the ratio of moles equals the ratio of volumes.
Solution
1. Find the molar volume from gas A: 10 L ↔ 0.5 mol, so each mole occupies 10 ÷ 0.5 = 20 L mol⁻¹.This "L per mole" is identical for gas B at the same T and P.
2. Apply it to gas B's 25 L: nB = 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25 mol.
3. Cross-check by direct ratio: nB = 0.5 × (25 ÷ 10) = 0.5 × 2.5 = 1.25 mol.B has 2.5× the volume of A, hence 2.5× the moles.
 gas   volume(L)   moles      L per mole
 A     10          0.50       20
 B     25          ?          20   ⇒ n = 25/20 = 1.25 mol
⚡ JEE Trick Same T and P ⇒ moles scale exactly like volumes — just multiply by the volume ratio (25/10 = 2.5). No need for R, T or P at all. Trap: don't bring in molar masses; Avogadro's law is mass-independent, so the gases being "A" and "B" changes nothing.
Answer: (c) 1.25 mol.
3Fixed mass, 2.5 atm at 300 K — pressure at 600 K, constant volumeGay Lussac's lawA-GAYLUS🟡 Similarc5.0 atm
Concept For a fixed mass of gas held at constant volume, Gay Lussac's law says pressure and absolute temperature rise and fall together in strict proportion. Physically, heating the gas makes molecules strike the walls harder and more often, so pressure climbs. Because it is a proportionality, doubling the (Kelvin) temperature doubles the pressure — the numbers here are chosen to make that exact.
Formula P/T = constant ⇒ P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂, so P₂ = P₁ × (T₂/T₁). P in atm, T in kelvin (already given). Temperature MUST be absolute (K); ratios of °C would give nonsense.
Solution
1. List the data: P₁ = 2.5 atm, T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 600 K, V and mass constant.
2. Form the temperature ratio: T₂/T₁ = 600 ÷ 300 = 2.0.The absolute temperature doubles.
3. Multiply the initial pressure by this ratio: P₂ = 2.5 × 2.0 = 5.0 atm.
4. Sanity: pressure and T are directly proportional, so a 2× temperature gives a 2× pressure. ✓
 state    P(atm)   T(K)     P/T
 initial  2.5      300      0.008333
 final    ?        600      must stay 0.008333 ⇒ P = 0.008333×600 = 5.0
⚡ JEE Trick Constant volume ⇒ P ∝ T (in kelvin) — just multiply P by the K-temperature ratio. Since 600 K is exactly twice 300 K, the pressure simply doubles. Trap: if a temperature is given in °C, add 273 first; using 300 vs 600 straight from Celsius would be a coincidence here but usually wrong.
Answer: (c) 5.0 atm.
4Gases A & B, same T & P, molar masses 16 & 64 — ratio dA:dBDensity ∝ molar massA-DENS🟡 Similara1 : 4
Concept At the same temperature and pressure every gas has the same molar volume (same litres per mole). So the only thing that makes one gas denser than another is how much a mole of it weighs — its molar mass. Density is therefore directly proportional to molar mass under identical conditions, and the density ratio is just the molar-mass ratio.
Formula d = PM/RT; at fixed P, T this gives d ∝ M, so dA/dB = MA/MB. d in g L⁻¹, M in g mol⁻¹. P, R, T are identical for both gases and cancel, leaving the mass ratio.
Solution
1. Write the densities: dA = P·MA/RT and dB = P·MB/RT.
2. Divide — the common factor P/RT cancels: dA/dB = MA/MB.Same T and P means identical molar volume, so density tracks molar mass alone.
3. Insert the masses: dA/dB = 16/64 = 1/4.
4. So dA : dB = 1 : 4 — the heavier gas B is four times as dense.
 gas   M(g/mol)   d ∝ M
 A     16         1
 B     64         4       ⇒ d_A : d_B = 16 : 64 = 1 : 4
⚡ JEE Trick Same T and P ⇒ density ratio = molar-mass ratio, nothing else. Just reduce 16 : 64 to 1 : 4 on sight. Trap: don't invert it — the heavier gas is the denser one, so B (64) must have the larger density.
Answer: (a) 1 : 4.
5Unknown gas d = 1.25 g/L at 1 atm & 300 K — molar mass MMolar mass from densityA-DENS🟡 Similara30.8 g·mol−1
Concept Gas density and molar mass are two views of the same ideal-gas equation. Rearranging PV = nRT with n = mass/M and density d = mass/V produces a direct link: knowing how heavy a litre of gas is (its density) plus the conditions P and T lets you back out the molar mass. This is the standard "identify the gas from its density" tool.
Formula M = dRT/P. Units: d in g L⁻¹, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T in K, P in atm ⇒ M in g mol⁻¹. Starting from d = PM/RT and solving for M just moves P and RT across.
Solution
1. List: d = 1.25 g L⁻¹, P = 1 atm, T = 300 K, R = 0.0821.
2. Compute RT first: 0.0821 × 300 = 24.63 L atm mol⁻¹.
3. Multiply by density: d·RT = 1.25 × 24.63 = 30.79 g atm mol⁻¹.
4. Divide by pressure: M = 30.79 ÷ 1 = 30.8 g mol⁻¹.P = 1 atm leaves the value unchanged.
 quantity   value
 RT         0.0821 × 300      = 24.63
 d·RT       1.25 × 24.63      = 30.79
 M = d·RT/P 30.79 / 1         = 30.8 g/mol
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise M = dRT/P (density → molar mass). With P = 1 atm and T = 300 K, RT ≈ 24.6, so M ≈ 24.6 × d — a one-multiply shortcut. Trap: keep density in g L⁻¹ (not g mL⁻¹) and temperature in kelvin, or the exponent of the answer goes wrong.
Answer: (a) 30.8 g·mol⁻¹.

1Gay Lussac's law: at constant volume, pressure of a gas is …Gay Lussac's lawA-GAYLUS🟡 SimilarbDirectly proportional to temperature
Concept Gay Lussac's law describes what happens to the pressure of a fixed amount of gas kept in a rigid container (constant volume) as you change its temperature. Heating raises the average speed of the molecules, so they collide with the walls more forcefully and more frequently — pressure rises. The law states this dependence is a direct proportionality to the absolute (kelvin) temperature.
Formula P ∝ T at constant V and n, i.e. P/T = constant. T must be in kelvin. Only absolute temperature makes the ratio constant; at T = 0 K the pressure would extrapolate to zero.
Solution
1. Fix the constraint the law assumes: volume (and amount) held constant.Change the volume too and you are no longer testing Gay Lussac's law.
2. Relationship: as T increases, P increases in the same proportion (P/T stays fixed).
3. That matches option (b): pressure is directly proportional to temperature.
4. Reject "inversely proportional" (that is pressure–volume, Boyle's law) and "independent of T" (false for a gas).
 constant   variables linked   relationship
 volume     P and T            P ∝ T   (Gay Lussac)  ✓
 temperature P and V           P ∝ 1/V (Boyle)
 pressure   V and T            V ∝ T   (Charles)
⚡ JEE Trick Tie each gas law to what is held constant: V fixed → P ∝ T (Gay Lussac), T fixed → P ∝ 1/V (Boyle), P fixed → V ∝ T (Charles). The word "directly proportional to temperature" is the Gay Lussac fingerprint. Trap: always read T as kelvin — the proportionality fails on the Celsius scale.
Answer: (b) Directly proportional to temperature.
2Isochores are graphs plotted between …Isochores (P–T graph)A-GAYLUS🟡 SimilarcPressure and temperature at constant volume
Concept The prefix "iso-" means "same" and "-chore" refers to volume, so an isochore is a curve traced at constant volume. Because Gay Lussac's law (P ∝ T at fixed V) governs a gas held at constant volume, its natural graph plots pressure against temperature. Each different fixed volume gives a different straight line through the origin — a family of isochores.
Formula P = (nR/V)·T — a straight line P vs T with slope nR/V. Axes: P (y) versus T in kelvin (x); V held constant. Fixing V makes nR/V a constant slope, so P–T is linear.
Solution
1. Decode the name: isochore = constant-volume curve.
2. At constant V, the variables free to change are P and T (Gay Lussac).Volume is pinned, so the graph must involve the remaining two variables.
3. Hence an isochore is a plot of pressure vs temperature at constant volume — option (c).
4. Its slope nR/V is larger for a smaller volume, so lower-volume isochores are steeper.
 curve name   held constant   plotted axes
 isochore     volume          P vs T     ✓ (option c)
 isotherm     temperature     P vs V
 isobar       pressure        V vs T
⚡ JEE Trick Match the "iso-" word to its constant: isochore = const V (P–T graph), isobar = const P (V–T), isotherm = const T (P–V). Trap: don't confuse isochore with isotherm — the P–T straight line is the constant-volume one, and its slope steepens as the fixed volume shrinks.
Answer: (c) Pressure and temperature at constant volume.
3Avogadro's law relates …Avogadro's lawA-AVOG🟡 SimilarbVolume and number of moles
Concept Avogadro's law connects the amount of gas (its number of moles) to the volume it occupies, provided temperature and pressure are held fixed. Add more molecules and, to keep P and T constant, the gas must expand in exact proportion. It says nothing directly about mass or molecular identity — the link is strictly between volume and mole number.
Formula V ∝ n (constant T, P), i.e. V/n = constant = molar volume. V in L, n in mol. At fixed T and P the same volume houses the same number of molecules for any gas.
Solution
1. Identify the two quantities the law ties together: volume V and amount n (moles).
2. Their relation is direct proportionality at constant T and P.Double the moles ⇒ double the volume.
3. That is exactly option (b): volume and number of moles.
4. Rule out "pressure and temperature" (Gay Lussac) and "pressure and volume" (Boyle) — those are different laws.
 law         links            condition
 Avogadro    V and n          const T, P     ✓ (option b)
 Boyle       P and V          const T, n
 Gay Lussac  P and T          const V, n
⚡ JEE Trick Avogadro = "more moles, more volume" at fixed T, P (V ∝ n). It is the only common gas law whose two variables are V and n. Trap: options pairing V with pressure or temperature belong to Boyle or Charles — the mole is the tell for Avogadro.
Answer: (b) Volume and number of moles.
4Ideal gas equation is …Ideal gas equationA-IDEAL🟡 SimilarbPV = nRT
Concept The ideal gas equation is the single relation that fuses Boyle's, Charles's and Avogadro's laws into one. It ties together all four state variables of a gas sample — pressure P, volume V, amount n and absolute temperature T — through a universal constant R. Any one of the four can be found once the other three are known, which is why this equation underpins almost every gas numerical.
Formula PV = nRT. Units (one consistent set): P in atm, V in L, n in mol, T in K, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. It reduces to each simpler law when the appropriate variables are held constant (e.g. fix n and T ⇒ PV = const, Boyle).
Solution
1. Combine the empirical laws: V ∝ 1/P (Boyle), V ∝ T (Charles), V ∝ n (Avogadro) ⇒ V ∝ nT/P.
2. Insert the proportionality constant R: V = nRT/P.R makes the proportionality an equation valid in chosen units.
3. Rearrange to the standard form: PV = nRT — option (b).
4. Reject look-alikes such as PV = RT/n or P = nRT (dimensionally wrong).
 source law   holds        contributes
 Boyle        n,T const    V ∝ 1/P
 Charles      n,P const    V ∝ T
 Avogadro     P,T const    V ∝ n
 combine  ⇒   V ∝ nT/P ⇒ PV = nRT
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the master equation PV = nRT and derive every sub-law by holding two variables fixed. Keep one consistent unit set (atm–L–K with R = 0.0821, or Pa–m³–K with R = 8.314). Trap: temperature is always kelvin, and n is moles — never grams — inside this equation.
Answer: (b) PV = nRT.
5Density (d) formula derived from ideal gas lawd = PM/RTA-DENS🟡 Similarcd = PM/RT
Concept The density form of the gas law comes straight from PV = nRT by writing the number of moles as mass ÷ molar mass. Since density is mass per unit volume, a couple of substitutions turn the mole equation into one relating density to pressure, molar mass and temperature. This is the working formula for gas density and for finding molar mass from density.
Formula derive d = PM/RT. Units: P (atm), M (g mol⁻¹), R = 0.0821, T (K) ⇒ d in g L⁻¹. Density rises with pressure and molar mass, and falls as temperature increases (gas expands).
Solution
1. Start from PV = nRT and replace n by m/M (mass ÷ molar mass): PV = (m/M)RT.
2. Divide both sides by V: P = (m/V)(RT/M).Grouping m/V isolates the density term.
3. Recognise m/V = d (density): P = d·RT/M.
4. Solve for d: d = PM/RT — option (c).
 step                     expression
 PV = nRT, n = m/M        PV = (m/M)RT
 divide by V              P = (m/V)(RT/M)
 m/V = d                  P = dRT/M
 solve for d              d = PM/RT
⚡ JEE Trick Derive d = PM/RT from PV = nRT by putting n = m/M — no need to memorise it separately. Its two rearrangements, M = dRT/P and P = dRT/M, cover most density numericals. Trap: don't flip it to d = RT/PM; density grows with P and M and shrinks with T.
Answer: (c) d = PM/RT.

1Volume at STP of 1 mole of gasMolar volumeA-MOLVOL🟡 Similara22.4 L
Concept Because Avogadro's law makes volume depend only on the number of moles (at fixed T and P), one mole of any ideal gas occupies the same volume under the same conditions. At STP (the older definition, 0 °C and 1 atm) this "molar volume" works out to the famous 22.4 L. It is a direct plug-in of n = 1 into the ideal gas equation.
Formula V = nRT/P. STP: P = 1 atm, T = 273 K, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, n = 1 mol. The gas identity never enters — only n, R, T, P do.
Solution
1. Set STP conditions: n = 1 mol, P = 1 atm, T = 273 K.
2. Compute RT: 0.0821 × 273 = 22.41 L atm mol⁻¹.
3. Volume: V = nRT/P = 1 × 22.41 ÷ 1 = 22.4 L.
4. This is the molar volume — the same for O₂, N₂, CO₂ or any ideal gas at STP.
 n(mol)   P(atm)   T(K)   V = nRT/P
 1        1        273    1×0.0821×273/1 = 22.4 L
⚡ JEE Trick Commit the molar volumes to memory: 22.4 L at STP (0 °C, 1 atm), 22.7 L at NTP (0 °C, 1 bar), 24.79 L at SATP (25 °C, 1 bar). Then n mol ⇒ n × 22.4 L directly. Trap: 22.4 L holds only at 0 °C and 1 atm — change either and you must recompute with PV = nRT.
Answer: (a) 22.4 L.

1Volume occupied by 2 moles of gas at STPMolar volumeA-MOLVOL🟡 Similarb44.8 L
Concept Once you know the molar volume at STP (22.4 L per mole), the volume of any number of moles is a single multiplication, because volume scales linearly with amount (Avogadro's law). Two moles therefore occupy twice the molar volume. No pressure or temperature juggling is needed — the conditions are already STP.
Formula V = n × Vm, with molar volume Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP. n in mol, V in L. Directly from V ∝ n: total volume is moles times volume-per-mole.
Solution
