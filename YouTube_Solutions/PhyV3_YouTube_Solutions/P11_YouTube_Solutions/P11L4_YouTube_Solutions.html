1A body at rest explodes into two pieces of unequal mass. The parts will move (a) Arbitrarily (b) In the same direction (c) In opposite directions with equal speeds (d) In opposite directions with unequal speedsExplosion, momentum conservation6. A body at rest explodes into two pieces of unequal mass. The parts will move🟢 ExactdIn opposite directions with unequal speeds
Concept An explosion is driven purely by internal forces — the chemical push each piece gives the other. Internal forces cancel in pairs, so the total momentum of the system cannot change: it was zero before, it stays zero after. Zero total momentum with two pieces forces the two momentum vectors to be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so the pieces must fly apart back-to-back. The tempting wrong answer is (c), “equal speeds” — but momentum is mv, not v, and the masses are explicitly unequal, so equal momenta demand unequal speeds.
Formula p⃗1 + p⃗2 = 0 ⇒ m1v⃗1 = −m2v⃗2. The explosion happens in a time so short that gravity's impulse is negligible, so momentum is conserved even though gravity is technically external.
Solution
1. Before: the body is at rest, so p⃗initial = M×0 = 0. Conservation then gives m1v⃗1 + m2v⃗2 = 0.This one equation carries every conclusion below — you never need the explosion energy.
2. Rearranged: v⃗2 = −(m1/m2)v⃗1. The minus sign is a vector statement — the two velocities are antiparallel, which already kills (a) “arbitrarily” and (b) “same direction”.If they moved in the same direction the momenta would add, giving a non-zero total from a body that started at rest.
3. Take magnitudes: v1/v2 = m2/m1 — the speed ratio is the inverse of the mass ratio, so the lighter piece is the faster one.Speeds are inversely proportional to masses; this is the single line examiners want you to write.
4. Numerical feel: split 3 kg into 1 kg and 2 kg. If the 1 kg piece leaves at 4 m/s, then 1×4 = 2×v2 = 4 ⇒ v2 = 2 m/s — opposite direction, half the speed.Plugging one concrete pair of numbers is the quickest way to convince yourself (c) is impossible.
5. Kill (c) directly: equal speeds v with unequal masses would give total momentum (m1 − m2)v ≠ 0, contradicting the start-from-rest condition. Only if m1 = m2 would (c) be right.This is exactly why the question says “unequal mass” — the word is the whole question.
6. Centre-of-mass view: with no net external force the COM stays exactly where it was, permanently at rest. Two pieces can straddle a fixed COM only by moving oppositely, and the heavier one must stay nearer, i.e. move slower. Hence (d).The COM argument and the momentum argument are the same statement in two languages.

          m1*v1 = m2*v2   (equal magnitudes)
          v1/v2 = m2/m1   -> lighter piece is faster
 example: 2kg at 2 m/s left, 1kg at 4 m/s right

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is reading “equal and opposite” as being about speeds when it is about momenta. Decode the options: (a) “arbitrarily” would need momentum not to be conserved; (b) “same direction” gives a non-zero total momentum from rest; (c) “equal speeds” is right only for equal masses, which the question deliberately excludes. That leaves (d).
Answer: (d) In opposite directions with unequal speeds.
2A ball of mass 1 kg strikes a heavy platform elastically, moving upwards with a velocity of 5 m/sec. The speed of the ball just before the collision is 10 m/s downwards. Then find the impulse imparted by the platform on the ball.Impulse, elastic bounce off moving wallA ball of mass 1 kg strikes a heavy platform elastically, moving upwards with a🟢 Exact15 N·s ⚠
Concept “Heavy platform” means its mass is effectively infinite, so the collision cannot change the platform's velocity at all — it keeps moving up at 5 m/s before and after. “Elastically” then means the coefficient of restitution is 1, which is a statement about the relative velocity: the ball leaves the platform as fast as it approached it, measured in the platform's own frame. The classic error is to work in the ground frame and simply flip the ball's 10 m/s to +10 m/s; that ignores the fact that the platform is rushing up to meet the ball, which pumps extra speed into the bounce.
Formula e = 1 ⇒ (vball − vplat)after = −(vball − vplat)before, and J = Δp = m(vf − vi). Impulse is defined as the change of momentum of the body it acts on, so only the ball's velocities enter the final arithmetic.
Solution
1. Fix a sign convention — take upward as positive: vi = −10 m/s for the ball, vplat = +5 m/s throughout.Every slip in this problem is a sign slip; writing the convention down first is the cheapest insurance.
2. Approach speed in the platform frame: urel = vi − vplat = −10 − 5 = −15 m/s, i.e. the ball closes on the platform at 15 m/s.Relative velocity, not ground velocity, is what restitution governs — this is the whole trick.
3. Elastic (e = 1) reverses that relative velocity: vrel,after = +15 m/s, so vf − 5 = +15 ⇒ vf = 20 m/s upward.The ball leaves at 20 m/s, not 10 — the moving platform adds 2×5 = 10 m/s, exactly like a tennis racket driven into the ball.
4. Impulse on the ball: J = m(vf − vi) = 1×(20 − (−10)) = 1×30 = 30 N·s, directed upward.Subtracting a negative is where the second common slip lives: 20 − 10 = 10 is wrong, the initial velocity is −10.
5. Cross-check with the general result for a wall of speed V: vf = 2V − vi = 2(5) − (−10) = 20 m/s, so J = m(2V − 2vi) = 2m(V − vi) = 2(1)(5 + 10) = 30 N·s — the same number from a one-line formula.J = 2m × (relative approach speed) is worth memorising; it collapses the whole problem to one multiplication.
6. Discrepancy with the printed key: the book prints 15 N·s, which is m(5 − (−10)) — it assumes the ball leaves at the platform's own 5 m/s instead of at 20 m/s. The correct impulse is 30 N·s; standard sources for this exact problem also give 30 N·s.Note 15 N·s is exactly half of 30 N·s, the signature of a dropped factor of 2 from restitution.

Concept Water is still and drag is ignored, so no horizontal external force acts on the boy–boat system; the centre of mass therefore stays pinned at one fixed point on the water for the whole walk. The boy's step forward must be paid for by the boat sliding backward, in exactly the proportion that keeps ΣmΔx = 0. The tempting wrong answer is 2.7 m — the boat does not move by the boat's own length; the 2.7 m is how far the boy moves relative to the boat, and that relative displacement is shared between the two bodies in inverse proportion to their masses.
Formula Δxboat = mL/(m + M), from mΔxboy + MΔxboat = 0 with Δxboy − Δxboat = L. The constraint “end to end” fixes the relative displacement at L = 2.7 m, not the ground displacement of either body.
Solution
1. Apply the ready formula: Δxboat = mL/(m + M) = 36×2.7/(36 + 72) = 97.2/108 = 0.9 m, directed opposite to the boy's walk.The mass in the numerator is the mass that moves along the boat — putting 72 there is the standard reciprocal slip and would give 1.8 m.
2. Derive it rather than trust it: let the boat slide back by b, then the boy's ground displacement is (2.7 − b) forward. COM fixed ⇒ 36(2.7 − b) = 72b ⇒ 97.2 = 108b ⇒ b = 0.9 m.Writing the boy's ground displacement as L − b is the one step students skip; using 2.7 m for the boy in the ground frame double-counts the boat's motion.
3. Sanity-check the split: the boy's ground displacement is 2.7 − 0.9 = 1.8 m forward, the boat's is 0.9 m back, and 1.8 + 0.9 = 2.7 m recovers the boat length as it must.This closure check catches almost every arithmetic slip in man-on-boat problems.
4. Check the momentum ledger: 36×1.8 = 64.8 kg·m forward and 72×0.9 = 64.8 kg·m backward — equal and opposite, so the COM really did not budge.Mass×displacement is the time-integral of momentum, so a balanced ledger is exactly the statement “COM at rest”.
5. Note the mass ratio shortcut: M/m = 72/36 = 2, so the boy moves twice as far as the boat, splitting 2.7 m as 1.8 : 0.9 — the answer with no algebra at all.For any 1 : 2 mass ratio the length splits 2 : 1 in displacement; recognising this makes the problem a 5-second one.
6. Kill the traps: 2.7 m would need an infinitely heavy boy, 1.35 m is the naive half-length, and 1.8 m is the boy's displacement, not the boat's. Only 0.9 m answers the question asked.Always re-read which body's displacement is wanted — the boat's, here.

4Two bodies of masses m1 and m2 (< m1) are connected to the ends of a massless cord and allowed to move as shown. The pulley is both massless and frictionless. Determine the acceleration of the centre of mass.Atwood machine, a of COMFind the acceleration of centre of mass of the blocks of masses m1, m2 in Atwood's machine.🟢 Exactacm = [(m1 − m2)2/(m1 + m2)2] g, downward
Concept The two blocks have accelerations of the same magnitude a but opposite directions, because one string of fixed length runs over the pulley. So the COM acceleration is not a, and it is not zero either — the cancellation is only partial, since the masses that carry the two opposite accelerations are different. The obvious wrong answer is acm = 0 (“one goes up, one goes down”), which would be true only for equal masses; the other wrong answer is acm = a, which forgets that the lighter block accelerates the other way.
Formula a⃗cm = (m1a⃗1 + m2a⃗2)/(m1 + m2) with the Atwood result a = (m1 − m2)g/(m1 + m2). The pulley and cord are massless, so they carry no momentum and drop out of the mass-weighted average completely.
Solution
1. Standard Atwood result, taking down as positive for m1: a = (m1 − m2)g/(m1 + m2), with m1 descending and m2 rising at the same rate since m2 < m1.Same magnitude comes from the inextensible cord; opposite sign comes from the cord passing over the pulley.
2. Mass-weight the two accelerations with down positive: acm = [m1(+a) + m2(−a)]/(m1 + m2) = a(m1 − m2)/(m1 + m2).Signs, not magnitudes, do all the work here — entering both as +a would wrongly give acm = a.
3. Substitute a: acm = [(m1 − m2)/(m1 + m2)]×[(m1 − m2)g/(m1 + m2)] = (m1 − m2)2g/(m1 + m2)2, directed downward.The factor appears twice — once from the dynamics, once from the weighting — which is why it comes out squared.
4. Cross-check by Newton's second law for the system: the external forces are the two weights and the pulley's support force, and (m1 + m2)acm = (m1 + m2)g − 2T with T = 2m1m2g/(m1 + m2). This gives the same squared expression.The support force on the system is the 2T pulled down on the pulley's axle — forgetting it is the usual reason this check “fails”.
5. Test the limits: m1 = m2 gives acm = 0 (nothing moves), while m2 → 0 gives acm → g (free fall of a single block). Both are exactly what physics demands.Limit checks are the fastest way to distinguish this squared answer from the linear one, which fails the m2 → 0 test only subtly.
6. Note the direction and the bound: acm is always downward (the square makes it positive regardless of which mass is bigger) and always less than g.A ratio squared is ≤ 1, so the COM can never out-accelerate free fall — a useful physical guard-rail.

1A 15 g bullet is fired from a gun of mass 2 kg with a speed of 1000 m/s. Find the recoil velocity of the gun.Recoil, momentum conservationA bullet of mass 10 g is fired from a gun of mass 6 kg with a velocity of 300 m/s.🟡 Similar7.5 m/s (opposite to the bullet)
Concept The explosion of the propellant is an internal force pair inside the gun–bullet system, so during the very short firing time the total horizontal momentum is conserved. It was zero before the trigger, so it must be zero after: the forward momentum of the bullet is matched exactly by the backward momentum of the gun. The obvious wrong instinct is to expect a huge recoil because the muzzle speed is huge — but the gun is about 133 times heavier than the bullet, so it recoils about 133 times slower.
Formula m1u1 + m2u2 = 0 ⇒ u2 = −m1u1/m2. Gravity and the shooter's grip are external but act for far too short a time to change the horizontal momentum appreciably, so conservation is legitimate.
Solution
1. Convert to SI first: m1 = 15 g = 0.015 kg, u1 = 1000 m/s, m2 = 2 kg.Mixing grams with kilograms is the single most common way to lose this mark — the answer would come out 1000× too big.
2. Apply conservation directly: u2 = m1u1/m2 = (0.015×1000)/2 = 15/2 = 7.5 m/s, directed opposite to the bullet.The bullet's momentum 15 kg·m/s is the whole content of the problem; everything else is one division.
3. Check the ledger: bullet 0.015×1000 = +15 kg·m/s, gun 2×(−7.5) = −15 kg·m/s, total 0 — matching the initial rest state.A zero-sum ledger is the definition of “momentum conserved”; it also catches a dropped factor instantly.
4. Ratio shortcut: u2/u1 = m1/m2 = 0.015/2 = 1/133.3, so u2 = 1000/133.3 = 7.5 m/s — the same answer read straight off the mass ratio.Speeds are inversely proportional to masses, so a heavy gun is precisely what makes a rifle usable.
5. State the direction explicitly: the gun moves backwards (into the shoulder), because the two momenta must be antiparallel to cancel.Board examiners award a separate mark for naming the direction, not just the magnitude.
6. Energy footnote — the bullet carries ½(0.015)(1000)2 = 7500 J while the gun carries only ½(2)(7.5)2 = 56.25 J, so momentum splits equally but kinetic energy overwhelmingly favours the light body.This is why recoil is survivable while the bullet is lethal — KE = p2/2m, so the heavier body gets less of it.

1Consider a system of two identical particles. One of the particles is at rest and the other has an acceleration f⃗. Concept The COM acceleration is the mass-weighted average of the individual accelerations, never their sum. “Identical particles” means equal masses, so the weighting collapses to a plain arithmetic mean of the two vectors — one of which is zero. The trap answer (a) Zero comes from confusing “one particle is at rest” with “the system is at rest”: a particle that is instantaneously at rest can still be accelerating, and here the other particle is definitely accelerating, so the system's COM must accelerate too.
Formula a⃗cm = (m1a⃗1 + m2a⃗2)/(m1 + m2). This is just F⃗ext = Ma⃗cm rewritten, so it holds whatever internal forces the two particles exert on each other.
Solution
1. Set m1 = m2 = m, a⃗1 = 0 (the particle at rest is unaccelerated) and a⃗2 = f⃗: a⃗cm = (m×0 + m×f⃗)/(m + m) = mf⃗/2m = f⃗/2.The mass m cancels, which is why the answer contains no mass symbol — a quick way to spot that the algebra went right.
2. Note the vector character: f⃗/2 points the same way as f⃗, since dividing a vector by a positive scalar cannot rotate it.The options are written as vectors on purpose; direction is part of the answer.
3. Cross-check through force: the net external force on the system is F⃗ = mf⃗ (only the second particle is being pushed), total mass is 2m, so a⃗cm = mf⃗/2m = f⃗/2.Same number from Newton's second law for systems — the two routes are the same theorem.
4. Kill (a): zero would require mf⃗ = 0, i.e. no force anywhere. “At rest” constrains velocity, not acceleration, so one stationary particle cannot freeze the COM.This is the single most common misread of the question — rest and equilibrium are different statements.
5. Kill (b) and (d): f⃗ would be the answer if the resting particle were massless (then the average is over one particle only), and 2f⃗ comes from adding the accelerations instead of averaging them.acm can never exceed the largest individual acceleration, so 2f⃗ is impossible on inspection.
6. Bracketing check: 0 ≤ acm ≤ f, and equal masses put it exactly halfway — acm = f/2 sits precisely where symmetry demands.Any weighted average lies between its extremes; this one-line bound eliminates two options without arithmetic.

Concept The surface is smooth, so no horizontal external force acts on the man–plank system and the total momentum stays at its initial value of zero. The one thing that must be handled carefully is the phrase “with respect to plank”: v is a relative velocity, so the man's ground velocity is V + v where V is the plank's own (backward) ground velocity. Feeding v straight into momentum conservation as if it were a ground velocity is the standard error and produces option (b) or (c).
Formula m(V + v) + MV = 0, using v⃗man,ground = v⃗man,plank + v⃗plank,ground. Momentum conservation is only valid in a ground (inertial) frame, so every velocity in the equation must first be converted to ground velocities.
Solution
1. Let the plank's ground velocity be V (take the man's walking direction as positive). Then the man's ground velocity is V + v, by the relative-velocity addition rule.This single substitution is the whole problem; everything after it is algebra.
2. Conserve momentum from rest: m(V + v) + MV = 0 ⇒ mV + mv + MV = 0 ⇒ V(m + M) = −mv ⇒ V = −mv/(m + M).The system starts at rest, so the right-hand side is 0 — that is what “starts moving” buys you.
3. The magnitude is therefore mv/(m + M), and the minus sign says the plank slides backwards, opposite to the man — option (d).Direction is not offered in the options, so only the magnitude is compared, but stating it is what makes the answer physical.
4. Consistency check on the man: vman,ground = V + v = v − mv/(m + M) = Mv/(m + M), and indeed m×Mv/(m + M) + M×(−mv/(m + M)) = 0.Note that Mv/(m + M), option (a), is the man's ground speed — the distractor is a real quantity, just the wrong one.
5. Concept An explosion is driven entirely by internal forces, so it cannot move the centre of mass off the trajectory the particle was already on. The particle was travelling along the x-axis, meaning its velocity had no y-component; therefore the CM's y-coordinate was zero before the blast and must stay zero after it. Gravity does act — the particle is above the ground — but the xy plane is horizontal, so g points along the perpendicular z direction and pulls both fragments down identically; it can never shift the CM sideways in y. That is why the phrase "at a certain height above ground" is a deliberate red herring, not data.
Formula ycm = (m1y1 + m2y2)/(m1 + m2) = 0 measured from the explosion point. Setting the origin at the point of explosion makes the CM's y-coordinate identically zero at t = 0, and internal forces keep it there.
Solution
1. Fix the origin at the point of explosion and note the pre-explosion y-velocity is zero, so ycm = 0 both before and "an instant later".Only an external y-force could change ycm; the explosion supplies none and gravity is perpendicular to the plane.
2. Apply the CM condition in y: (m/4)(+15) + (3m/4)(y2) = 0 ⇒ 15/4 + 3y2/4 = 0 ⇒ y2 = −5 cm.The mass m cancels throughout — only the mass ratio 1 : 3 matters, which is why no numerical mass is given.
3. Read the ratio directly: y2/y1 = −m1/m2 = −(1/4)/(3/4) = −1/3, so the heavier piece sits one-third as far out and on the opposite side: −15/3 = −5 cm.Heavier fragment → smaller displacement; if your answer puts the 3m/4 piece farther than the m/4 piece, the ratio has been inverted.
4. Momentum cross-check: for the CM to stay put in y the transverse momenta must cancel, (m/4)vy1 = −(3m/4)vy2, giving |vy2| = |vy1|/3; over the same short instant that reproduces the 15 cm : 5 cm split exactly.
5. The x-coordinate: the CM continues along x at the original (slow) speed, so both fragments straddle xcm = v0t. 6. The z (vertical) coordinates of both fragments are equal, each having fallen ½gt2, so gravity cancels out of any comparison between the two pieces — and independent working therefore confirms the key's y = −5 cm.

9A mass m1 is connected by a weightless cable passing over a frictionless pulley to a container of water, whose mass is m0 at t = 0. If the container ejects water in downward direction at a constant rate b kg/s with velocity v0 relative to the container, determine the acceleration of m1 as a function of time.Variable mass, thrust forceVariable Mass Systems Masterclass | All I.E. Irodov Problems Solved | JEE Advanced Physics🟡 Similara = [(m1 − m0 + bt)g + bv0] / (m1 + m0 − bt)
Concept This is an Atwood machine with one side leaking, so two things change at once: the container gets lighter, and the escaping water pushes back. Newton's second law in the form F = ma is invalid for the container because its mass is not constant; the correct statement adds a thrust term vrel(dm/dt). Water leaves downwards, so by reaction it shoves the container upwards with force bv0 — exactly a rocket, hung upside down on a string. The naive treatment that only replaces m0 by (m0 − bt) in the ordinary Atwood formula misses this thrust entirely and is the error the question is built to catch.
Formula m(t)·dv/dt = Fext + vrel(dm/dt), giving thrust Fthrust = bv0 upward on the container. With vrel = −v0 (down) and dm/dt = −b (mass falling), the product (−v0)(−b) = +bv0 is positive, i.e. directed upward.
Solution
1. Container mass at time t: mc(t) = m0 − bt, valid until the water runs out at t = m0/b.Constant rate b makes this linear; beyond that time the problem simply ends.
2. Take m1 descending with acceleration a; the inextensible cable over an ideal pulley forces the container upward with the same magnitude a.One string, one pulley, no slipping → the two accelerations are equal and opposite, so a single unknown a suffices.
3. Equation for m1 (down positive): m1g − T = m1a. Its mass is constant, so ordinary Newton applies unchanged.
4. Equation for the container (up positive), including thrust: T − mcg + bv0 = mca.The ejected water carries its own weight away with it, so only the water still inside contributes mcg — that is already handled by using mc(t).
5. Add the two equations to eliminate T: (m1 − mc)g + bv0 = (m1 + mc)a ⇒ a = [(m1 − mc)g + bv0]/(m1 + mc).T is internal to the pair once the cable is treated as massless, so adding always kills it — never solve for T first.
6. Substitute mc = m0 − bt: a(t) = [(m1 − m0 + bt)g + bv0]/(m1 + m0 − bt), which matches the printed key.
7. Behaviour check: at t = 0, a = [(m1 − m0)g + bv0]/(m1 + m0) — the ordinary Atwood value plus a thrust bonus. As t grows the numerator rises and the denominator falls, so a increases monotonically; setting b = 0 recovers exactly a = (m1 − m0)g/(m1 + m0).Any candidate answer that does not collapse to the plain Atwood result at b = 0 is wrong on inspection.
