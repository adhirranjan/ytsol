1Which of the following is an intensive property? (a) Volume (b) Mass (c) Density (d) Number of molesExtensive vs intensive propertyProperties of Thermodynamics Intensive and Extensive | Intensive & Extensive Property Thermodynamics🟡 SimilarcDensity
Concept Every measurable property of a system falls into one of two families, and the test is purely mechanical: imagine splitting the system into two identical halves. If the property also halves, it is extensive (it depends on how much stuff is present). If it stays exactly the same in each half, it is intensive (it does not depend on the amount). Volume, mass and mole count are the three most basic "how much" measurements of a sample, so they are the textbook extensive trio; density is built by dividing one extensive quantity by another.
Key relation density = mass / volume. Whenever a property is written as extensive ÷ extensive, the amount-dependence in the numerator and denominator cancel, so the ratio is intensive — this is exactly how molar volume, molarity and molality are all built.
Solution
1. Apply the halving test to each option in turn, splitting the sample into two identical halves.
2. (a) Volume: each half occupies half the space ⇒ V → V/2 ⇒ scales with amount ⇒ extensive.
3. (b) Mass: each half weighs half as much ⇒ m → m/2 ⇒ extensive.
4. (d) Number of moles: half the substance means half the moles ⇒ n → n/2 ⇒ extensive.
5. (c) Density: each half has both half the mass and half the volume, so ρ = (m/2)/(V/2) = m/V — completely unchanged.This is why you can dip a thermometer or a hydrometer into a spoonful or a bucketful of the same liquid and read the identical value — density does not care about sample size.
6. Only option (c) survives the halving test unchanged ⇒ density is the intensive property.
property     formula      whole system   each half     type
volume       V            V              V/2           extensive
mass         m            m              m/2           extensive
moles        n            n              n/2           extensive
density      m/V          m/V            (m/2)/(V/2)   intensive  <-- answer (c)
                                          = m/V (same)
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an option list mixes raw amounts (mass, volume, moles, energy) with a ratio built from them (density, molar volume, concentration), the ratio is almost always the odd one out and the intensive answer. Trap: students sometimes assume "more mass ⇒ more density" and pick (b); density is a property of the substance, not the sample size, and stays fixed as long as the composition and conditions don't change.
Answer: (c) Density.

Concept A thermodynamic system is classified purely by what is allowed to cross its boundary — never by what it looks like physically. There are exactly two things that can flow across a boundary: matter (mass) and energy (heat/work). Every system type is just one row of a 2×2 truth table of "can matter cross? can energy cross?". A system that says yes to both is the most permeable of the three named categories, and the theory calls it an open system — the classic example being the human body (it eats, breathes, sweats, and radiates heat, exchanging both mass and energy continuously).
Key relation Open: Δm ≠ 0, ΔE ≠ 0 · Closed: Δm = 0, ΔE ≠ 0 · Isolated: Δm = 0, ΔE = 0. "Rigid" is not a system-permeability category at all — it only describes whether the boundary can change volume (mechanical property), unrelated to matter/energy exchange.
Solution
1. Build the 2×2 grid: matter (yes/no) × energy (yes/no) gives four combinations, and the syllabus names three of them.
2. Matter YES + Energy YES ⇒ open system — e.g. an open cup of coffee losing vapour (matter) and heat (energy) to the room.
3. Matter NO + Energy YES ⇒ closed system — e.g. a tightly capped, uninsulated cup: no mass leaves, but heat still escapes through the walls.
4. Matter NO + Energy NO ⇒ isolated system — e.g. coffee in a perfectly insulated thermos: neither vapour nor heat gets out.
5. Match the question's requirement — "exchanges both matter and energy" — directly to row 2 of the grid ⇒ open system, option (b)."Rigid system" (d) is a distractor built from an unrelated axis (fixed boundary shape) and never appears in the matter/energy classification at all.

⚡ JEE Trick Read "open" as "open to everything" — both gates unlocked. Any question that says "exchanges both" is always open; "exchanges neither" is always isolated; anything in between (one gate open, one shut) is closed. Trap: "rigid system" and "adiabatic system" are real thermodynamic terms from later sections (rigid = fixed volume, adiabatic = no heat exchange) but they answer a different question than this matter/energy classification — don't let them get shortlisted here.

3Which of the following quantities cannot be used to specify the thermodynamic state of a system? (a) Pressure (b) Heat (c) Temperature (d) VolumeState variables vs path quantities2 STATE FUNCTION AND PATH FUNCTION | THERMODYNAMICS | IIT ADVANCED | JEE MAIN | CHEMISTRY CLASS 11🟡 SimilarbHeat
Concept The state of a system is the snapshot you get by reading off its macroscopic properties — pressure, volume, temperature and amount — at one instant, the way P, V, n, T, S, E, H, G are listed as the coordinates of a state in the theory's diagram. A quantity can specify the state only if it has one fixed value for that state, independent of how the system got there. Heat fails this test: it is not a property the system possesses at all — it is energy in transit across the boundary, and its amount depends entirely on the path taken between two states, not on the states themselves.
Key relation State variables: (P, V, T, n, S, E, H, G) — fixed once the state is fixed. q (heat) is a path function: the same initial and final state can be reached by an infinite number of different processes, each transferring a different amount of heat — so "the heat of the system" is not even a well-defined phrase; only "heat transferred during a specific process" makes sense.
Solution
1. List each option and ask: "does this quantity have one definite value once P, V, n, T are fixed?"
2. (a) Pressure — yes, a defining state variable, appears directly in the state-function tuple (P₁,V₁,n₁,T₁,...).
3. (c) Temperature and (d) Volume — same reasoning, both are listed macroscopic properties that fix the state.
4. (b) Heat — a system in a given state does not "contain" a fixed quantity of heat; heat only appears as energy crossing the boundary during a change of state, and its value changes with the path chosen (e.g. isothermal vs adiabatic route between the same two states).
5. Therefore heat cannot be used to specify a state — it describes a process, not a point — giving option (b).
quantity      fixed by the state alone?     category

volume V            YES                      state variable
heat q              NO (path-dependent)       path function  <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the short list of path functions — heat (q) and work (w) — everything else in the syllabus (P, V, T, U, H, S, G) is a state function. If a quantity is ever written with a lower-case letter (q, w) it is path-dependent; capital-letter thermodynamic quantities (U, H, S, G) are state functions. Trap: students sometimes think "heat" must be a state variable because temperature (which measures "hotness") clearly is — but heat is energy in transit, not a property held by the system.
Answer: (b) Heat.
4A system shows no change in any macroscopic property with time, the system is: (a) In thermal equilibrium only (b) In mechanical equilibrium only (c) In chemical equilibrium only (d) In thermodynamic equilibriumThermodynamic equilibriumThermodynamic Equilibrium - Thermal Equilibrium - Mechanical Equilibrium - Chemical Equilibrium🟢 ExactdIn thermodynamic equilibrium
Concept "No change in any macroscopic property with time" is, word for word, the syllabus definition of thermodynamic equilibrium — and the key word is any. Thermodynamic equilibrium is not a single condition but a bundle of three simultaneous equilibria: thermal (no net heat flow, uniform T), mechanical (no unbalanced forces, uniform P) and chemical (no net reaction or diffusion, uniform composition). A system that satisfies only one of the three still has properties drifting on the other two fronts, so it fails the "no change in any property" test.
Key relation Thermodynamic equilibrium = Thermal + Mechanical + Chemical equilibrium (simultaneously). Each partial equilibrium freezes a different property: thermal freezes T, mechanical freezes P, chemical freezes composition/concentration. Only when all three hold do all macroscopic properties stop changing at once.
Solution
1. Note the question's own wording: "no change in any macroscopic property" — this is an all-properties condition, not a single-property condition.
2. Test (a) thermal equilibrium only: T is fixed, but P could still be changing (piston still moving) or composition could still be reacting — other properties still drift.
3. Test (b) mechanical equilibrium only: P is fixed, but heat could still be flowing (T still changing) — fails the "any property" test.
4. Test (c) chemical equilibrium only: composition is fixed, but T or P could still be adjusting — again incomplete.
5. Only when thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium hold together does every macroscopic property (T, P, composition, and everything derived from them) stop changing — this composite state is called thermodynamic equilibrium, option (d).

thermal                  T                        NO
mechanical                P                        NO
chemical               composition                 NO
thermal+mech+chem   T, P, composition (all)     YES  <-- thermodynamic equilibrium (d)
⚡ JEE Trick Any question phrased as "no change in any/all macroscopic property" is asking for the composite state, thermodynamic equilibrium — never one of the three partial equilibria named individually as distractors. Trap: options (a)-(c) are each individually true statements about equilibrium, which makes them tempting; the question is testing whether you notice the word "any", which demands the full three-way bundle, not a single type.
Answer: (d) In thermodynamic equilibrium.
5Which of the following are state functions? Concept A state function is any macroscopic property whose value is completely fixed once the state (P, V, n, T, ...) of the system is fixed — it does not care how that state was reached. A path function is not a property of the system at all; it is energy in transit across the boundary, and its size depends on the specific route the process follows between two states. This question is a straight sorting exercise: check each of the four listed quantities against that one test.
Key relation State functions in this syllabus: P, V, T, n, U, H, S, G (capital-letter thermodynamic quantities). Path functions: q (heat), w (work) — lower-case, always tied to a specific process. Internal energy U and enthalpy H = U + PV are both built entirely from state variables (U itself, plus P and V), so any combination of state functions is automatically a state function too.
Solution
1. (a) Internal energy U — defined as the total energy stored in the system; for a given state (fixed T, V, n) it has one definite value ⇒ state function.
2. (b) Enthalpy H = U + PV — built from three state variables (U, P, V) combined algebraically ⇒ inherits state-function status.Any algebraic combination of state functions (sum, difference, product) is itself a state function — this is why G = H − TS and A = U − TS are also state functions.
3. (c) Temperature T — one of the four defining macroscopic coordinates (P, V, n, T) that fix the state itself ⇒ state function by definition.
4. (d) Heat q — NOT a property the system possesses; it is energy that crosses the boundary during a process, and the same start/end states can be joined by an isothermal path or an adiabatic-then-isochoric path releasing completely different amounts of heat ⇒ path function, fails the test.
5. Collect the survivors: (a), (b) and (c) pass the "fixed-by-state-alone" test; (d) fails ⇒ correct set = a, b, c.
quantity      built from            fixed by state alone?   verdict
U (a)         primary state coord.        YES                state fn
H=U+PV (b)    U, P, V (all state fns)     YES                state fn
T (c)         primary state coord.        YES                state fn

⚡ JEE Trick Keep the short blacklist in your head: only q and w are path functions in this syllabus — everything else offered as an option (U, H, S, G, T, P, V) is a state function, so "select all state functions" questions reduce to "select everything except heat and work". Trap: because q and w individually are path functions, students sometimes wrongly extend that to their sum — but q + w = ΔU by the first law, and ΔU is a state function even though its two ingredients are not.

Concept Every classification of a thermodynamic system is built on one question only: what is allowed to cross the boundary? There are exactly two things that can move across it — matter and energy — so the classification is a small truth table, not a list to memorise by rote. Once you fix "can matter cross? can energy cross?" the system's type falls out automatically, and each type has one concrete household example that fixes it in memory permanently.
Key relation Open: Δm ≠ 0, ΔE ≠ 0 · Closed: Δm = 0, ΔE ≠ 0 · Isolated: Δm = 0, ΔE = 0. The fourth combination (Δm ≠ 0, ΔE = 0) is not physically realistic — matter always carries some energy with it — so the syllabus names only three types.
Solution
1. Open system — exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings. Example: an open cup of hot coffee (loses water vapour = matter, and heat = energy, to the room); the human body is another standard example (eats, breathes, sweats and radiates heat continuously).
2. Closed system — exchanges energy but not matter. Example: a tightly capped cup of coffee — no liquid or vapour escapes (matter sealed), but heat still passes through the cup walls to the surroundings (energy free to move).
3. Isolated system — exchanges neither matter nor energy. Example: coffee in a well-insulated, sealed thermos flask — the seal blocks matter, the insulation blocks heat, so nothing crosses the boundary at all.A perfectly isolated system is an idealisation; the universe itself is treated as the one true isolated system, since by definition nothing exists outside it to exchange with.
4. Present the three as a single ascending scale of "how sealed is the boundary": open (nothing blocked) → closed (only matter blocked) → isolated (everything blocked).

Concept "System + surroundings = universe" is a definition, not a discovery — the syllabus splits everything that exists into exactly two buckets: the small portion under study (the system) and literally everything else (the surroundings). An isolated system, by definition, has a boundary that lets neither matter nor energy cross. The question asks you to combine these two facts and see why the label "isolated" is forced onto the universe as a whole.
Key relation Universe = System + Surroundings. Isolated system: Δm = 0, ΔE = 0 across the boundary. "Exchange with surroundings" only makes sense if something exists outside the boundary to exchange with. The universe, by its own definition, has nothing left outside it.
Solution
1. Start from the definition: the universe is defined as the system plus its surroundings — i.e. absolutely everything, with nothing left over outside it.
2. For any exchange (of matter or of energy) to occur, there must be a "there" for the exchanged quantity to go to — some region outside the boundary that receives it.
3. Since the universe already contains everything, there is no "outside" left for matter or energy to be transferred into or received from.
4. Therefore, by the plain meaning of the words, no matter can leave or enter the universe, and no energy can leave or enter it either — which is exactly the defining condition of an isolated system (Δm = 0, ΔE = 0).
5. Note the subtlety: energy and matter can still move within the universe — between a system and its surroundings, both of which are parts of the universe — this internal shuffling is exactly what every other thermodynamic problem studies; it is only the universe as a whole that cannot exchange anything, because there is nowhere left to exchange with.

Concept "Homogeneous" and "heterogeneous" split systems by a completely different axis than open/closed/isolated — this axis is about phase, not about what crosses the boundary. A phase is a physically distinct, mechanically separable, uniform region of matter (you could in principle scoop it out with a spoon and it would still be the same stuff throughout). A homogeneous system is the simple case: the whole system is one single phase, so its composition and properties (density, colour, concentration) are identical at every point you sample.
Key relation Homogeneous system = 1 phase, uniform properties throughout. "Uniform" here means composition-uniform at the macroscopic level — a true solution looks and behaves identically whether you sample it from the top, middle or bottom of the container, because there is no internal physical boundary anywhere inside it.
Solution
1. Definition: a homogeneous system is one that consists of only a single phase, with the same physical state and uniform chemical composition throughout its entire volume.
2. Test for homogeneity: pick a sample from any point in the system — if every sample has identical composition and properties, no internal boundary exists, and the system is homogeneous.
3. Classic examples: a mixture of gases (e.g. air), a true solution such as sugar dissolved completely in water, or a pure liquid such as ethanol — in each case you cannot visually or mechanically separate the system into distinguishable parts.Gases are always homogeneous when mixed, because gas molecules intermix completely at the molecular level, leaving no phase boundary anywhere.
4. Contrast in one line (to sharpen the definition): the moment you can point to two regions inside the system with a visible interface between them (e.g. oil floating on water), the system is no longer homogeneous — that case is called heterogeneous instead.

Concept A heterogeneous system is the mirror image of a homogeneous one: instead of a single uniform phase, it is made up of two or more distinct phases, each with its own uniform properties, separated from each other by a real, physically identifiable interface (a surface you could, in principle, mechanically skim or filter along). Crucially, uniform overall temperature and pressure across the whole system do not make it homogeneous — what matters is whether the composition/phase is uniform, not whether T and P happen to be equal everywhere.

Solution
1. Definition: a heterogeneous system consists of two or more physically distinct phases, each with its own set of uniform properties, with a sharp boundary (interface) separating one phase from another.
2. Test for heterogeneity: look for a visible or mechanically detectable interface inside the system — a place where properties change abruptly rather than smoothly. If such an interface exists anywhere, the system is heterogeneous, however uniform its overall T or P may be.
3. 4Explain: (A) Extensive property (B) Intensive property of a system. OR What is the difference between extensive and intensive properties?Extensive vs intensive propertiesThermodynamics - Extensive and Intensive Properties - Class 11 Chemistry By Arvind Arora Sir🟢 Exact—
Concept Every macroscopic property of a system belongs to one of exactly two families, and the split is decided by a single mental test: imagine cutting the system into two identical halves and ask what happens to the property's value. If it also halves, the amount of substance matters, so it is extensive. If it stays exactly the same in each half, the amount doesn't matter, so it is intensive. This test is the entire content of the definition — no property needs to be memorised individually once the test is applied.
Key relation Extensive: X(2×amount) = 2·X(amount) — depends on quantity of matter. Intensive: Y(2×amount) = Y(amount) — independent of quantity of matter. Any intensive property built as extensive ÷ extensive automatically cancels the amount-dependence — this is exactly how density (mass/volume) and molar volume (volume/moles) become intensive even though their ingredients are extensive.
Solution
1. (A) Extensive property — definition: a property of a system that depends upon the amount (quantity) of substance present in the system.
2. Extensive examples with the halving test: mass (m → m/2), volume (V → V/2), number of moles (n → n/2), internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, heat capacity, free energy — each one shrinks proportionally when the sample is halved.
3. (B) Intensive property — definition: a property of a system that is independent of the amount of substance present in the system.
4. Intensive examples with the halving test: temperature, pressure, density, molar volume, molarity, molality, mole fraction, viscosity, surface tension, refractive index — each one is completely unchanged when the sample is halved, because it is a per-unit-amount (ratio) quantity.Density = mass/volume: halving the sample halves both numerator and denominator, so the ratio m/V stays fixed — this is the general mechanism behind every intensive ratio-property in the syllabus.
5. Difference in one line: extensive properties add up when two identical systems are combined (2 litres + 2 litres = 4 litres); intensive properties stay the same when two identical systems are combined (25 °C + 25 °C = still 25 °C, not 50 °C).
property family     halving test result     examples
EXTENSIVE            X → X/2                mass, volume, moles, U, H, S, heat capacity
INTENSIVE             Y → Y (unchanged)      T, P, density, molarity, viscosity, mole fraction
rule: intensive = extensive / extensive  (amount cancels top and bottom)
⚡ JEE Trick Do not memorise two separate lists — memorise the halving test and derive the list live under exam pressure; it also instantly resolves "is X extensive or intensive?" for properties you haven't seen before. Whenever an option list mixes a raw amount (mass, volume, moles) with a ratio built from amounts (density, molarity, molar volume), the ratio is almost always the intensive one. Trap: heat capacity (extensive, total J/K for the whole sample) is easily confused with specific heat (intensive, J/g/K per unit mass) — the word "specific" or "molar" in front of a property is the giveaway that it has been converted to intensive.
Key point: extensive properties (mass, volume, moles, U, H, S) scale with the amount of substance and add on combining systems; intensive properties (T, P, density, molarity) are independent of amount and stay unchanged on combining identical systems.

Concept The universe is the special, all-encompassing case at the very top of the system/surroundings hierarchy: by definition it is System + Surroundings combined, and since there is nothing left outside it for it to exchange matter or energy with, it satisfies the isolated-system condition by simple exhaustion — not because some physical wall blocks the exchange, but because there is nowhere else for anything to go. This makes every one of the four statements true simultaneously, echoing the "all four correct" pattern already seen in L6-Q7.
Key relation Universe = System + Surroundings (by definition, nothing exists outside this sum). Because "outside the universe" is not a physically meaningful place, the universe trivially satisfies Δm = 0, ΔE = 0 across its own boundary — it is the one isolated system that isn't isolated by an actual wall, but by definition having nothing beyond it.

4. Test (d) Its boundary is conceptual, not physical: unlike a system boundary (which can be a real wall, membrane or piston), the "boundary" of the universe is not a physical surface anyone could touch — it is a conceptual device used purely to mark "everything that exists" ⇒ TRUE.
