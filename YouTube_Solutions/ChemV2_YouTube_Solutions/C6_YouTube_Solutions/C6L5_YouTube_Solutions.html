1 (i)Fill in the blank with appropriate term.
Maximum work is done in ……… , ……… conditions.		Maximum work — reversible, isothermal conditions Wmax = 2.303 nRT log(V2/V1) Derivation | Max Work Done Isothermal Reversible Expansion🟡 Similarreversible, isothermal
Concept Work is a path function — between the same initial and final states a gas can deliver very different amounts of work depending on how you take it there. Two separate conditions have to be stacked to squeeze out the absolute maximum. First, the expansion must be reversible: at every instant the external pressure is held only infinitesimally below the gas pressure, so the gas is always pushing against the largest opposing pressure that still permits expansion. Second, it must be isothermal: heat flows in continuously to stop the gas cooling, which keeps P high all the way along, so the area under the P–V curve stays as large as possible.
Key relation wmax = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1), obtained by putting Pext = Pgas = nRT/V in w = −∫Pext dV. Only when Pext tracks Pgas can the integrand be replaced by nRT/V; any real (irreversible) expansion runs against a lower fixed Pext and therefore sweeps a smaller area.
Solution
1. Write the general work integral for any expansion: w = −∫Pext dV. The only quantity you control is Pext, so maximising |w| means making Pext as large as it can possibly be.
2. The ceiling on Pext is Pgas itself: the moment Pext equals or exceeds Pgas the expansion stops or reverses. So the best case is Pext = Pgas − dP, which is exactly the definition of a reversible change.This is why reversible work is the theoretical upper bound that no real process can beat.
3. That fixes the first blank: reversible.
4. Now decide the thermal condition. If the gas were allowed to cool (adiabatic), P would fall faster than 1/V and the curve would drop steeply, shrinking the area under it.
5. Holding T constant by letting heat leak in keeps the gas on the highest hyperbola available, P = nRT/V, so the area under the path — and hence the work — is the largest obtainable. That fixes the second blank: isothermal.
6. Both together give the standard maximum-work formula wmax = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1), the expression every numerical in this lecture is built on.
 path (V1 -> V2)        P_ext during expansion    |work|
 vacuum                 0                         0   (least)
 fixed low P_ext        constant, below P_gas     small
 reversible ADIABATIC   tracks P_gas, T falls     larger
 reversible ISOTHERMAL  tracks P_gas, T held up   MAXIMUM
⚡ JEE Trick "Maximum work" is a code word: the instant you read it, switch to the log formula w = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1) rather than PextΔV. Writing only "reversible" is the standard half-mark loss here — the isothermal half of the answer is what guarantees T (and hence P) does not sag during the expansion.
Answer: maximum work is done under reversible, isothermal conditions.
1 (ii)In a reversible isothermal expansion of one mole of an ideal gas from volume V1 to V2 the work done by the system is equal to ……… .Reversible isothermal work formulaWorkdone in Isothermal Reversible Expansion of an Ideal Gas |Chemical Thermodynamics|proChem Academy🟡 Similar−2.303 RT log(V<sub>2</sub>/V<sub>1</sub>)⚠
Concept For a reversible isothermal change the external pressure is matched to the gas pressure at every instant, so Pext can be replaced by the ideal-gas expression nRT/V inside the work integral. With n = 1 mole and T constant, the integral is a pure logarithm. The only thing that then needs care is the sign convention: this lecture writes lower-case w for the work done on the system (so expansion gives a negative w), whereas the phrase "work done by the system" flips that sign.
Key relation won = −∫V1V2 (nRT/V) dV = −nRT ln(V2/V1) = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1). The factor 2.303 is just ln 10, converting the natural log into the base-10 log the book prefers; with n = 1 the n drops out.
Solution
1. Start from w = −∫Pext dV, the general definition with the on-system sign convention.
2. Reversible means Pext = Pgas at every step, and for one mole of an ideal gas Pgas = RT/V.
3. Substitute and integrate at constant T: w = −∫V1V2 (RT/V) dV = −RT [ln V]V1V2 = −RT ln(V2/V1).T comes out of the integral only because the process is isothermal — this single step is what the word "isothermal" buys you.
4. Convert to base-10: w = −2.303 RT log(V2/V1). This is the printed answer, and it is the work done on the gas.
5. Sanity-check the sign: expansion means V2 > V1, so log(V2/V1) > 0 and won is negative — correct, since a gas that expands loses energy to its surroundings rather than receiving it.
6. ⚠ The blank as printed asks for the work done by the system, which is the opposite of won: Wby = +2.303 RT log(V2/V1). Write the magnitude 2.303 RT log(V2/V1) and attach whichever sign the convention in force demands.

Then set the sign by asking one question only: "on the gas, or by the gas?" — the two differ by nothing but a minus sign.

Concept "Expansion in vacuum" (free expansion) means there is nothing on the far side of the boundary pushing back, so Pext = 0. The work integral then collapses no matter how large the volume change is. The container is taken to be isolated, so no heat crosses the wall either, and for an ideal gas internal energy depends only on temperature — with neither heat nor work exchanged, there is nothing left that could change T or U.
Key relation w = −PextΔV = −(0)ΔV = 0 and ΔU = q + w. The three zeros in the options are not three independent facts — Pext = 0 forces w = 0, isolation forces q = 0, and the first law then forces ΔU = 0 automatically.
Solution
1. Identify the external pressure. Expansion into a vacuum has no atmosphere, no piston and no opposing gas, so Pext = 0 exactly.
2. Apply the work formula: w = −PextΔV = 0 × ΔV = 0. The volume change is irrelevant — the gas has nothing to push against, so it spends no energy. Option (b) is therefore true.
3. The standard assumption for this MCQ is a thermally isolated vessel, so no heat enters or leaves: q = 0. Option (c) is true.This is the Joule free-expansion experiment; the vessel is jacketed precisely so that q can be set to zero.
4. Feed both into the first law: ΔU = q + w = 0 + 0 = 0. Option (a) is true.
5. Cross-check with ideal-gas behaviour: U depends only on T, so ΔU = 0 means ΔT = 0 — the gas neither cools nor warms, which is exactly what Joule observed.
6. All three statements hold simultaneously, so the answer that covers them is (d).

Concept An "open vessel" means the reaction runs at the constant pressure of the atmosphere. Iron dissolving in dilute HCl liberates hydrogen gas, and that new gas has to shove the atmosphere out of the way to make room for itself. The work is therefore fixed entirely by how many moles of gas appear, not by the masses of the liquids or solids involved.
Key relation w = −PΔV = −ΔngRT, using PV = nRT for the gas produced. Δng counts only gaseous species: moles of gaseous product minus moles of gaseous reactant. Fe(s), HCl(aq) and FeCl2(aq) all contribute nothing.
Solution
1. Write the reaction: Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + H2(g). One mole of Fe releases one mole of H2.
2. Convert the mass to moles: 112 g Fe ÷ 56 g mol−1 = 2 mol Fe, so 2 mol of H2 are formed.

5. Convert: −1200 cal = −1.2 kcal. The negative sign says the system spends energy pushing the atmosphere back, as expansion always must.

1What are the characteristics of maximum work?Reversible work is the maximum work — descriptiveHow to Calculate Maximum Work Done in Thermodynamics | Step-by-Step Derivation🟡 Similar—
Concept "Maximum work" is not a bigger number pulled out of nowhere — it is the ceiling that the work integral itself imposes. Because w = −∫Pext dV, the work a gas can deliver over a fixed volume change is set entirely by how hard it is pushing back against. Raise Pext as high as physically possible while still allowing expansion, and you reach the maximum. That limiting case is the reversible, isothermal expansion.
Key relation wmax = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1) = −2.303 nRT log(P1/P2). The two forms are interchangeable through Boyle's law at constant T (P1V1 = P2V2), so use whichever pair of data the question gives you.
Solution The characteristics expected in a Board answer are:
1. It is obtained only in a reversible process. Pext is kept infinitesimally below Pgas throughout, so the gas works against the greatest possible opposing pressure at every instant.
2. It is isothermal. Heat flows in to hold T constant, keeping the gas on the highest P–V hyperbola and so maximising the area under the curve.An adiabatic expansion between the same volumes cools the gas, P sags, and the area — the work — is smaller.
3. It is the upper limit of work. For the same initial and final states, |wrev| > |wirrev| always; no real process can exceed it.
4. It is carried out infinitely slowly, in an infinite number of infinitesimal steps, so the system stays in equilibrium with its surroundings at all times.
5. It is a path function, not a state function. wmax depends on the route taken, which is exactly why a "maximum" exists at all — a state function would have one fixed value.
6. Its magnitude is given by wmax = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1), and the negative sign shows the work is done by the system on the surroundings during expansion.
 condition        why it maximises the work
 reversible       P_ext is as high as expansion allows
 isothermal       T held up, so P stays high throughout
 infinitely slow  system never leaves equilibrium

 w_max = -2.303 nRT log(V2/V1)   (upper bound on |w|)
⚡ JEE Trick Board examiners award marks per named characteristic, so list them as bullet points and always close with the formula plus a one-line sign statement. The single most-marked point is that reversible work is the maximum work — state it explicitly rather than leaving it implied.
Answer: maximum work is the reversible, isothermal, infinitely-slow expansion work, wmax = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1) — the upper limit no real process can beat.

Concept Four paths leave a common starting point on a P–V diagram, and each must be identified from its shape alone. The ordering by steepness is fixed and worth knowing outright: from flattest to steepest, isobaric, isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric. Every one of the four labels in this question fits that sequence.
Key relation isobaric: P = constant; isothermal: PV = constant; adiabatic: PVγ = constant with γ > 1; isochoric: V = constant. The exponent on V controls the steepness: 0 for the isobar, 1 for the isotherm, γ for the adiabat, and effectively infinite for the vertical isochore.
Solution
1. Curve A is drawn vertical — the volume never changes while the pressure varies. That is an isochoric process, so (a) is correct.
2. Curve D is horizontal — the pressure stays fixed while the volume increases. That is an isobaric process, so (d) is correct.
3. Curves B and C both fall away as V increases, so both are hyperbola-like; they must be the isotherm and the adiabat, and only their relative steepness distinguishes them.Slope on a P–V plot is dP/dV = −P/V for an isotherm but −γP/V for an adiabat — steeper by exactly the factor γ.
4. Curve B is the steeper of the two, falling fastest, so it is the adiabatic path — the gas cools as it expands, losing pressure faster. Option (b) is correct.
5. Curve C is the gentler curve, so it is the isothermal path, where heat flows in to hold the temperature and pressure up. Option (c) is correct.
6. All four statements hold, so the answer is (a), (b), (c) and (d).
