1 (i)State whether the following are true or false.
The amount of heat evolved or absorbed when one gram of the substance is dissolved in excess of water is called heat of solution.		Definition of enthalpy of solution6 Enthalpy of hydration | Enthalpy of solution | IIT JEE main advanced | chemistry class 11🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept The enthalpy of solution is the heat change that accompanies dissolving a substance in so much solvent that adding more solvent produces no further heat effect ("infinite dilution"). The physical content of the statement — heat evolved or absorbed, dissolution in excess of water, and the name "heat of solution" — is exactly right; only the amount is quoted loosely.
Key relation X(s) + aq → X(aq); ΔHsol measured per mole of X. Enthalpy is extensive, so a heat of solution is meaningless until you say how much substance dissolved.
Solution
1. Read the three parts of the claim separately: (a) heat may be evolved or absorbed — correct, KOH warms water while KNO3 cools it; (b) the solvent must be in excess — correct, that is the infinite-dilution condition; (c) the amount is "one gram".
2. Parts (a) and (b) are the defining features, so the statement is marked True.
3. On part (c), the chapter's own theory (p. 250) says one mole of substance: KCl + aq → KClaq, ΔH = 4.4 kcal is a per-mole figure.
4. Strictly, then, "one gram" should read "one mole" — per-gram values are called specific heats of solution and are the molar value divided by M.
5. Every worked problem in this lecture (BaCl2, CuSO4, CaCl2, NaCl) quotes ΔHsol in kJ mol−1, confirming the molar convention.
6. Answer as keyed: True, with the amount understood to be one mole.

1 (ii)Whenever an acid is neutralised by a base, the net reaction is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l); ΔH = −13.7 kcalNet ionic reaction of neutralisationEnthalpy of Neutralization - Thermodynamics | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6 | IIT JEE/NEET/CBSE/NCERT🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept A strong acid and a strong base are both fully ionised in dilute solution. Na+ and Cl− stay hydrated and untouched on both sides — they are spectators. The only chemical change is H+ combining with OH− to make water, so every strong acid–strong base neutralisation has the same net ionic equation and therefore the same enthalpy.
Key relation H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l); ΔH = −13.7 kcal = −57.1 kJ This is just the enthalpy of formation of liquid water from its aqueous ions.
Solution
1. Write HCl + NaOH in full ionic form: H+ + Cl− + Na+ + OH− → Na+ + Cl− + H2O.
2. Cancel the spectators Na+ and Cl−; what survives is H+ + OH− → H2O.
3. Repeat with HCl + KOH, HNO3 + NaOH, HClO4 + KOH — the same net equation appears every time, so ΔH is the same −13.70 kcal.
4. The book's own table confirms it: HCl + NaOH and HCl + KOH both give −13.70 kcal.
5. Caveat worth knowing: this holds for strong acid with strong base. With a weak partner some of the released heat is spent ionising it, so the apparent value falls (12.3 kcal for NH4OH, 13.4 for CH3COOH).
6. Read as the book intends it — the standard strong/strong case — the statement is True.

1 (iii)The value −13.7 kcal is constant for all neutralization reaction between strong acid and strong base.Why ΔHneut is constant for SA–SBHeat of neutralization of strong acid by a strong base is a constant value due to:🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Constancy follows directly from the previous statement. Because every strong acid–strong base pair reduces to the identical net reaction, the measured heat cannot depend on which acid or which base was used. This is one of the cleanest experimental confirmations that ions in dilute solution behave independently of one another.
Key relation ΔHneut(SA + SB) = ΔHf[H2O(l)] from aqueous ions = −13.7 kcal eq−1 A single number, not a family of numbers.
Solution
1. Strong acids (HCl, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4) are ≈100% ionised in dilute solution, so no energy is needed to liberate H+.
2. Strong bases (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2) are likewise fully ionised, so OH− is already free.
3. Therefore the only bond-making step is H+ + OH− → H2O for every such pair.
4. Cations and anions merely exchange partners; their hydration shells are unchanged, so they contribute no enthalpy.
5. Experiment agrees: HCl/NaOH, HCl/KOH, HNO3/NaOH all give −13.70 ± 0.05 kcal per equivalent.
6. Hence the statement is True. (Note "per equivalent": 1 mol H2SO4 releases 2 × 13.7 kcal because it supplies two equivalents.)

1 (v)Bond enthalpies of diatomic molecules are equal to the bond enthalpies of dissociation.Bond enthalpy vs bond dissociation enthalpyWhat is the Difference Between Bond Energy & Bond Dissociation Energy | Chemical Bonding🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept For a molecule with only one bond there is nothing to average. Breaking H—H gives two H atoms in one step, so the "bond enthalpy" and the "bond dissociation enthalpy" describe the very same process and carry the same magnitude. The distinction between the two words only becomes meaningful for polyatomic molecules.
Key relation A–B(g) → A(g) + B(g); ΔH = bond dissociation enthalpy One bond, one step, one number — no averaging is possible.
Solution
1. Take H2: H2(g) → 2H(g), ΔH = +435.14 kJ mol−1. That single figure is both the bond enthalpy and the bond dissociation enthalpy of H—H.
2. Contrast H2O, which has two O—H bonds broken in sequence: +501.87 then +423.38 kJ mol−1.
3. Those two stepwise dissociation enthalpies differ because the fragment left behind (•OH vs a bare O atom) is different, so only their average, 462.6, is quoted as the O—H bond enthalpy.
4. A diatomic offers no second step, so no such discrepancy can arise.
5. Sign convention: bond dissociation is endothermic (+), bond formation exothermic (−); equal in magnitude, opposite in sign.
6. The statement is therefore True for diatomic molecules.
 diatomic  H2 -> 2H            +435.14  (only step)
   bond enthalpy = BDE = 435.14 kJ/mol
 -----------------------------------------------
 polyatomic  H-O-H -> H + OH   +501.87
             O-H   -> O + H    +423.38
   average O-H bond enthalpy   = 462.6 kJ/mol
 -----------------------------------------------
 averaging is needed ONLY when bonds > 1
⚡ JEE Trick If a question quotes one number for a diatomic, use it directly. If it quotes several numbers for a polyatomic, they are stepwise values and you must average them before using them as "the" bond energy.
Answer: True — for a diatomic molecule bond enthalpy and bond dissociation enthalpy are the same quantity.
1 (vi)Bond formation is always exothermicSign of bond-formation enthalpyWhat is Bond Enthalpy? Organic Chemistry - Class 11 & 12🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Two free atoms sit at high potential energy. As they approach, attraction between the nucleus of each and the electrons of the other lowers the potential energy until a minimum is reached at the equilibrium bond length. That drop in energy has to leave the system as heat, so forming a bond is necessarily exothermic. Breaking the bond is the uphill reverse and is always endothermic.
Key relation H•(g) + H•(g) → H2(g); ΔH = −435.14 kJ mol−1 The negative sign is not a convention — it is the depth of the potential-energy well.
Solution
1. Sketch potential energy against internuclear distance: it falls from zero at infinite separation to a minimum, then rises steeply as the nuclei repel.
2. Energy released on going from infinity to the minimum equals the well depth, which is the bond enthalpy.
3. Since the products (the molecule) sit lower than the reactants (free atoms), ΔH for bond formation is negative — exothermic.
4. The book states it directly: bond enthalpies are exoergic (ΔH = −ve), bond dissociation enthalpies endoergic (ΔH = +ve).
5. No counter-example exists: if a "bond" cost energy to form, the atoms would simply fly apart and the molecule would not be stable.
6. Hence the statement is True.

    |     .
    |        .____.____ formed bond (minimum)
    +----------------------------> r
 -----------------------------------------------
 formation : atoms -> molecule   dH < 0  (exo)

⚡ JEE Trick Use this as a sign check on every bond-energy sum. If your calculated "bond formation" enthalpy comes out positive, you have added the products instead of subtracting them.
Answer: True — bond formation lowers the potential energy of the atoms, so it is always exothermic.
1 (vii)Heat of reaction = Σ Bond energies of product − Σ Bond energies of reactants.Correct direction of the bond-energy formulaBond Energy Calculations & Enthalpy Change Problems, Basic Introduction, Chemistry🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept A reaction proceeds conceptually in two stages: every bond in the reactants is broken (costing energy) and every bond in the products is formed (releasing energy). The net enthalpy is therefore energy spent minus energy regained — reactants first, products subtracted. The statement has the two terms the wrong way round, which flips the sign of every answer.
Key relation ΔrH = ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products) Note this is the opposite order to the ΔHf formula, which is products minus reactants — the classic trap.
Solution
1. Break all reactant bonds: this absorbs ΣBE(reactants), a positive contribution.
2. Assemble the products from the free atoms: this releases ΣBE(products), a negative contribution.
3. Add them: ΔrH = +ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products).
4. The statement writes products minus reactants, which is the negative of the correct expression.
5. Test it on H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl with BE = 435, 243, 431: correct gives (435 + 243) − 862 = −184 kJ, exothermic as observed. The reversed version would predict +184 kJ, which is wrong.
6. The statement is therefore False.
 step 1  break reactant bonds   + sum BE(react)
 step 2  form product bonds     - sum BE(prod)
 -----------------------------------------------
 dH_rxn = sum BE(reactants) - sum BE(products)
 -----------------------------------------------
 check  H2 + Cl2 -> 2HCl
        (435 + 243) - (2 x 431) = -184 kJ  (exo)
 reversed order would give +184 kJ : WRONG
⚡ JEE Trick Two formulae, opposite orders — keep them apart: bond energies = reactants − products; formation enthalpies = products − reactants. Remember it as "bonds must break first".
Answer: False — heat of reaction = ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products), not the other way round.

1What is phase transformation?Phase transformation definedEnthalpy Change during Phase Transformation 🔴 Chemistry for Class 11🟡 Similar—
Concept A phase transformation is a change of a substance from one physical state (or allotropic form) to another at constant T and P, with no change in chemical composition. Only the arrangement and freedom of the particles differ, and the enthalpy change measures the energy needed to rearrange the intermolecular forces.
Key relation solid ⇆ liquid ⇆ gas, each step at a fixed T and P with its own ΔH Temperature stays constant throughout a phase change; all supplied heat goes into potential energy, not kinetic energy.
Solution
1. Definition. Phase transformation is the conversion of a substance from one phase to another at the characteristic temperature for that change, the chemical identity remaining unaltered.
2. The changes. Fusion (s → l) at the melting point, vaporisation (l → g) at the boiling point, sublimation (s → g) below the melting point, and their reverses — freezing, condensation, deposition.
3. Allotropic transition counts too: Cdiamond → Camorphous, ΔH = −1.9 kJ, at the transition temperature.
4. Energetics. Changes that increase disorder absorb heat (ΔH +ve); their reverses release the same amount. H2O(l) → H2O(g) needs +40.6 kJ mol−1, condensation returns −40.6.
5. Constant temperature. While ice melts at 0°C the thermometer does not move: the heat is latent heat, spent breaking attractions, not raising kinetic energy.
6. Why it matters. ΔH is a state function, so these enthalpies add freely in Hess cycles — that is how ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap arises.

Concept Freezing is the reverse of fusion. When a liquid solidifies, its particles lose the freedom to move past one another and settle into a fixed lattice, so intermolecular attractions are made rather than broken. Energy is therefore released and the enthalpy of freezing is negative, equal in magnitude to the enthalpy of fusion.
Key relation ΔHfreezing = −ΔHfusion Reversing any process reverses the sign of its enthalpy change, because H is a state function.
Solution
1. Definition. The enthalpy of freezing (heat of solidification) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a liquid is completely converted into solid at its freezing point and constant pressure.
2. Sign. It is exothermic, ΔH negative, because ordered bonding in the lattice releases the energy that melting had to supply.
3. Example. H2O(l) → H2O(s) at 273 K, ΔH = −6.01 kJ mol−1, whereas fusion H2O(s) → H2O(l) has ΔH = +6.01 kJ mol−1.

5. Freezing point = melting point. The two phases are in equilibrium at the same temperature, so one number serves both directions.
6. Everyday use. Orchards are sprayed with water before a frost: as the water freezes it releases 6 kJ mol−1, keeping the fruit near 0°C instead of colder.

Solution
1. Lattice break-up: NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g), ΔH = +787 kJ mol−1.
2. Hydration: Na+(g) + Cl−(g) + aq → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq), ΔHhyd = ?
3. Sum of the two steps is the overall dissolution, ΔHsol = +3.9 kJ mol−1.
4. So 3.9 = 787 + ΔHhyd, giving ΔHhyd = 3.9 − 787 = −783.1 kJ mol−1 for the ion pair.
5. Sign check: hydration is exothermic and its magnitude must be just under the lattice energy, otherwise the salt would not dissolve endothermically. 783.1 < 787 by 3.9 — exactly the observed heat of solution.
6. This near-cancellation is the general rule for soluble 1:1 salts: |U| and |ΔHhyd| are both large and nearly equal, so ΔHsol is a small difference of two big numbers.
