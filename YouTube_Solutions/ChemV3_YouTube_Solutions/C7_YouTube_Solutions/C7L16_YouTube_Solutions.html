1 (i)Fill in the blank: An ______ is a substance that changes color in response to a chemical change.Definition of indicatorWhat Are Indicators & How Do We Use Them? | Chemical Tests | Chemistry | FuseSchool🟡 Similarindicator
Concept An acid–base indicator is a weak organic acid (HIn) or weak base whose un-ionised and ionised forms have different colours. A change in pH shifts its ionisation equilibrium, so the colour of the solution reports the chemical change. Trap: “titrant” or “buffer” do not change colour.
Key relation HIn ⇌ H+ + In−, pH = pKIn + log([In−]/[HIn]) The colour seen is fixed by the ratio [In−]/[HIn], and that ratio is fixed by the pH.
Solution
1. The blank asks for the substance whose colour responds to a chemical change; in acid–base work that change is a change in [H+].
2. Such substances are weak acids or weak bases: phenolphthalein (HIn colourless, In− pink) and methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base).
3. Adding H+ pushes HIn ⇌ H+ + In− to the left (acid colour); adding OH− removes H+ and pushes it right (base colour). Le Chatelier
4. The eye sees a single colour once one form is about 10 times the other (book, p.142): [In−]/[HIn] ≥ 10 gives pH ≥ pKIn + 1; ≤ 1/10 gives pH ≤ pKIn − 1.
5. So the colour-change range is about pKIn ± 1, i.e. roughly 2 pH units wide.
6. Check with phenolphthalein, pKIn = 9.6 (book table): range ≈ 8.6–10.6, close to the table’s 8.3–10.5.
7. Hence the blank is indicator (“An indicator” also fits the article).
 HIn        <=>   H+   +   In-
 (acid colour)          (base colour)

 pH = pKIn + log([In-]/[HIn])
 [In-]/[HIn] = 1/10 -> pH = pKIn - 1   acid colour
 [In-]/[HIn] = 10   -> pH = pKIn + 1   base colour
 range = pKIn +/- 1  (about 2 pH units)
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = indicator                    <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Keep the three titration words apart: titrant is in the burette, analyte is in the flask, and the indicator is the few drops that change colour. An indicator must be weak: a strong acid would be fully ionised at every pH and show one colour only.
Answer: indicator.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: The point in a titration at which an equal amount of titrant has been added to the analyte is called the ______.Definition of equivalence pointWhat is Equivalence Point (acid-base titrations)?🟡 Similarequivalence point
Concept The equivalence point is the theoretical point at which the titrant added is chemically equivalent to the analyte, i.e. equal milliequivalents have reacted. “Equal amount” means equal equivalents, not equal volumes and not always equal moles. Trap: the point where the indicator changes colour is the end point, not this one.
Key relation meq of titrant = meq of analyte, i.e. N1V1 = N2V2 Complete reaction (book, p.142) means every equivalent of acid has met one equivalent of base.
Solution
1. The blank describes the stoichiometric point of the titration, which is fixed by the chemistry alone and not by any indicator.
2. Example: 50 mL of 0.1 M CH3COOH needs V mL of 0.1 M NaOH, with 50 × 0.1 = 0.1 × V = 5 meq, so V = 50 mL.
3. For H2SO4 against NaOH, equal moles is wrong: 1 mol H2SO4 (2 eq) needs 2 mol NaOH, so “equal amount” must be read as equal equivalents.
4. The pH at this point depends on the salt formed: 7 for strong acid–strong base, above 7 for weak acid–strong base, below 7 for weak base–strong acid.
5. Check for CH3COOH/NaOH: [CH3COO−] = 5/100 = 0.05 M, pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa + log C) = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 − 1.30) = 8.72.
6. An indicator is then chosen so that its colour change (the end point) falls as close as possible to this pH.
7. Hence the blank is equivalence point.
 meq acid = meq base   (N1V1 = N2V2)
 0.1 x 50 = 0.1 x V    ->  V = 50 mL

 H2SO4 + 2NaOH : 1 mol acid = 2 eq
   -> equal EQUIVALENTS, not equal moles

 at eq. pt, [CH3COO-] = 5/100 = 0.05 M
 pH = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 + log 0.05) = 8.72

 blank = equivalence point            <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick The equivalence point is not always at pH 7; only a strong acid–strong base titration gives 7. Equivalence point = theoretical (stoichiometry); end point = experimental (colour change). A well-chosen indicator makes them differ by less than one drop.
Answer: equivalence point.
1 (iii)Fill in the blank: The point in a titration when the indicator changes color is known as the ______.Definition of end pointDifference between End Point and Equivalence Point- Titrations🟡 Similarend point
Concept The end point is the experimentally observed point at which the indicator changes colour and the titration is stopped. Ideally it coincides with the equivalence point; the small difference is the titration error. Trap: do not write “equivalence point” here, since that one is defined by stoichiometry, not by colour.
Key relation end point: pH ≈ pKIn ± 1 The indicator flips colour over its own pH range, so the end point is wherever the titration curve crosses that range.
Solution
1. The blank defines the point detected by a visible colour change, so it belongs to the indicator, not to the stoichiometry.
2. An indicator changes colour around pH = pKIn ± 1, where [In−]/[HIn] goes from 1/10 to 10.
3. If that range lies inside the steep (vertical) part of the titration curve, one drop of titrant crosses it, and the end point and equivalence point practically coincide.
4. HCl against NaOH: the jump runs from about pH 3.5 to 10 (book, p.142), so methyl orange and phenolphthalein both give a good end point.
5. CH3COOH against NaOH: the jump runs from about pH 7 to 10.6, so phenolphthalein works, but methyl orange (pH ≈ 3–4.5) would change colour long before equivalence, giving a large error.
6. Weak acid against weak base: there is no sharp jump, so no indicator gives a sharp end point.
7. Hence the blank is end point.
 indicator range  = pKIn +/- 1
 end point        = where curve crosses that range

 HCl / NaOH       jump 3.5 - 10   MO, phph both OK
 CH3COOH / NaOH   jump 7 - 10.6   phph OK, MO not
 weak / weak      no sharp jump   no indicator

 titration error  = end point - equivalence point
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = end point                    <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the pair: equivalence point is theoretical and fixed by moles/equivalents; end point is practical and fixed by the indicator. A question saying “indicator changes colour” always wants end point.
Answer: end point.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: Phenolphthalein changes color in a pH range from approximately ______ to ______.Phenolphthalein pH rangeWhy does phenolphthalein change colors?🟡 Similar8.2, 10.0
Concept Phenolphthalein is a weak acid indicator (HIn colourless, In− pink) with pKIn near 9.3–9.6. Its colour change therefore sits on the basic side, at about pH 8.2 to 10. Exact limits vary slightly between sources; the book’s own table on p.142 lists 8.3–10.5.
Key relation range ≈ pKIn ± 1 One colour dominates when one form is about 10 times the other.
Solution
1. Phenolphthalein: HIn (colourless) ⇌ H+ + In− (pink).
2. Below about pH 8.2 the HIn form dominates and the solution is colourless.
3. Take pKIn ≈ 9.3: at pH 8.2, [In−]/[HIn] = 108.2 − 9.3 = 10−1.1 ≈ 0.08, so it is still colourless.
4. At pH 10.0, [In−]/[HIn] = 1010.0 − 9.3 = 100.7 = 5, so a clear pink is seen.
5. So the range is approximately 8.2 to 10.0. The book table (8.3–10.5, pKa 9.6) is the same range within source-to-source variation.
6. Use: this range lies inside the jump of strong acid–strong base (3.5–10) and weak acid–strong base (7–10.6) titrations, so phenolphthalein suits both.
7. It does not suit weak base–strong acid titrations, whose jump (3.5–7) is entirely below 8.2.

1 (vi)Fill in the blank: The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is used to calculate the ______ of a buffer solution.Henderson equation useHenderson–Hasselbalch equation | Acids and bases | AP Chemistry | Khan Academy🟡 SimilarpH
Concept The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation gives the pH of a buffer, a mixture of a weak acid and its salt (or a weak base and its salt), directly from pKa and the salt-to-acid ratio. It follows from the Ka expression by taking logarithms. Trap: it gives pH, not Ka or the buffer capacity.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]); pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) Valid when both partners are present in comparable amounts (ratio roughly 0.1 to 10).
Solution
1. Start from Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA], so [H+] = Ka[HA]/[A−].
2. Take −log of both sides: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]), the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.
3. Acetate buffer, 0.2 M CH3COONa + 0.1 M CH3COOH: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) = 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04.
4. Book example (p.144, part b): after 25 mL NaOH, H3PO4 and NaH2PO4 are 2.5 mmol each, so pH = pKa1 + log(2.5/2.5) = 3 + 0 = 3.
5. At half-neutralisation [salt] = [acid], so pH = pKa; this is how pKa is read off a titration curve.
6. For a basic buffer (NH3 + NH4Cl) the same equation is written for pOH, then pH = 14 − pOH (Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C).
7. Hence the blank is pH.
 Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA]
 pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA])

 0.2 M salt / 0.1 M acid :
 pH = 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04

 half-neutralised : [A-] = [HA] -> pH = pKa
 basic buffer : pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base])
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = pH                           <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Quick check on any buffer answer: pH must lie within pKa ± 1. If the salt term is larger, pH > pKa; if the acid term is larger, pH < pKa. Using the ratio of moles instead of concentrations is fine, since the total volume cancels.
Answer: pH.
1 (vii)Fill in the blank: At the endpoint of a titration, the amount of titrant is ______ to the amount of analyte.Titrant equals analyteTitrations - Equivalence Point, End Point and Neutralisation Point (A-level Chemistry)🟡 Similarequal
Concept At a correctly located end point the titrant added is chemically equivalent to the analyte, which is why the book writes N1V1 = N2V2 at the end point (p.142). “Equal” means equal milliequivalents. Strictly this defines the equivalence point; the end point only approximates it, but with a suitable indicator the two differ by less than a drop.
Key relation meq of titrant = meq of analyte, N1V1 = N2V2 This equality is the basis of every titration calculation.
Solution
1. The blank compares the amount of titrant with the amount of analyte at the end of the titration; the stoichiometric relation is equal equivalents.
2. It is equal in equivalents, not necessarily in moles or volume: 1 mol Na2CO3 needs 2 mol HCl to reach the methyl-orange end point.
3. Book Example-1 (p.144): 20 mL of x M HCl neutralises 10 mL of 0.1 M NaHCO3 plus 5 mL of 0.2 M Na2CO3 to the methyl-orange end point.
4. meq HCl = meq NaHCO3 + meq Na2CO3: 20x = 10 × 0.1 + 5 × 0.2 × 2 = 1 + 2 = 3.
5. So x = 3/20 = 0.15 M, exactly the book’s value; the equality of equivalents is what makes the calculation work.
6. Fine point: at the end point the equality is only approximate (titration error); it is exact at the equivalence point. The book’s intended word is still equal.
7. Hence the blank is equal (equivalent).
 at end point: meq titrant = meq analyte

                 not equal moles

2 (i)True or false: An indicator can be a weak acid or a weak base.Nature of indicators23 Ionic Equilibrium | Theory of Indicators | Equivalence Point | JEE Main Adv | Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Acid–base indicators are weak organic acids or weak organic bases whose un-ionised and ionised forms have different colours (book, p.142, point ii). Weakness is essential: only a weak electrolyte switches between its two forms over a narrow pH range. So the statement is true.
Key relation HIn ⇌ H+ + In− (weak acid) or InOH ⇌ In+ + OH− (weak base) Ostwald’s theory: the two forms have different colours.
Solution
1. Phenolphthalein is a weak acid: HIn (colourless) ⇌ H+ + In− (pink), pKIn ≈ 9.6 (book table).
2. Methyl orange is treated in Ostwald’s theory as a weak base: MeOH (yellow) ⇌ Me+ (red) + OH−, red in acid where OH− is removed.
3. For a weak indicator, pH = pKIn + log([In−]/[HIn]) moves the ratio from 1/10 to 10 as pH moves by 2 units, giving a sharp colour change.
4. If an indicator were a strong acid, it would be fully ionised at every pH and show only one colour, so it could not signal anything.
5. A strong-acid indicator would also consume titrant; only a few drops of a weak one are used so the error is negligible.
6. So an indicator can be a weak acid or a weak base: the statement is True.
 weak acid :  HIn  <=> H+  + In-
              (X)          (Y)
 weak base :  InOH <=> In+ + OH-

⚡ JEE Trick The key word is weak. A statement such as “indicators are strong acids or strong bases” is false for exactly the reason above. Colour change happens because pH shifts the indicator’s own ionisation equilibrium, not because of temperature.
Answer: True.

Concept The equivalence point is theoretical (fixed by stoichiometry); the end point is experimental (fixed by the indicator’s colour change). They coincide only when the indicator’s range lies on the steep part of the curve around the equivalence pH. The word “always” makes the statement false.
Key relation end point pH ≈ pKIn ± 1; equivalence pH set by the salt formed Two different physical criteria, so they need not match.

1. Equivalence point: meq titrant = meq analyte. End point: the indicator changes colour.

2 (iii)True or false: Phenolphthalein is colorless in acidic solution.Phenolphthalein colour in acidWhy does phenolphthalein change colors?🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Phenolphthalein is a weak acid indicator whose un-ionised form HIn is colourless and whose anion In− is pink. In acidic solution the high [H+] suppresses its ionisation, so almost all of it is HIn and the solution is colourless. The statement is true.
Key relation [In−]/[HIn] = 10(pH − pKIn) Rearranged from pH = pKIn + log([In−]/[HIn]).
Solution
1. Phenolphthalein: HIn (colourless) ⇌ H+ + In− (pink), pKIn ≈ 9.6 (book table, p.142).
2. In acid, extra H+ pushes the equilibrium left (common-ion effect), so HIn dominates.
3. At pH 7: [In−]/[HIn] = 10(pH − pKIn) = 10(7 − 9.6) = 10−2.6 ≈ 0.0025, i.e. about 0.25% pink form.
4. At pH 4 the ratio is 10−5.6, smaller still, so any acidic solution is clearly colourless.
5. Pink appears only above about pH 8.2–8.3, where the ratio passes about 1/10; it is clearly pink by pH 10.
6. That is why in an acid-in-flask titration the solution stays colourless until the first excess drop of NaOH turns it pink.
7. At very high pH (above about 13) the pink colour fades slowly again, but that is irrelevant to acidic solutions.
8. So the statement is True.

2 (v)True or false: At the end point, the Henderson-Hasslebalch equation can be used to calculate the pH of the solution.Henderson equation at end pointDetermine pH for Acid/Base Reaction at Equivalence point: 3 Steps!🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation needs a buffer: both the weak acid and its salt present. At the end point (≈ equivalence point) the weak acid has been completely converted to salt, so [HA] ≈ 0 and the log term blows up. The pH there comes from salt hydrolysis instead. HH is used in the buffer region, best at the half-equivalence point.
Key relation at equivalence: pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa + log C) Only the salt A− is left, so the pH is set by its hydrolysis, not by a [A−]/[HA] ratio.
Solution
1. Take 50 mL of 0.1 M CH3COOH titrated with 0.1 M NaOH (5 mmol acid).
2. At 25 mL (half-way): 2.5 mmol acid and 2.5 mmol salt, so pH = pKa + log(2.5/2.5) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74. HH works here.
3. At 50 mL (end point): 0 mmol acid and 5 mmol salt, so HH would give 4.74 + log(5/0), which is undefined.
4. Correct route: [CH3COO−] = 5/100 = 0.05 M, pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa + log C) = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 − 1.30) = 8.72 (Kw = 10−14, 25 °C).
5. The book does the same on p.144: at each equivalence point it uses the amphoteric or hydrolysis formula (parts c, e, g), and uses pH = pKa + log ratio only at the half-way points (b, d, f).
6. For strong acid–strong base there is no weak acid at all, so HH never applies; pH at the end point is simply 7.
7. So, read normally, the statement is false. The key prints True, perhaps meaning the indicator relation pH = pKIn + log([In−]/[HIn]); but that needs the unknown colour ratio and does not calculate the solution’s pH.
