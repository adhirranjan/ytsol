1A cyclist speeding at 18 km h−1 on a level road takes a sharp circular turn of radius 3 m without reducing the speed and without bending towards the centre of the circular path. The coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the road is 0.1. Will the cyclist slip while taking the turn? — (Level-1)Slipping on a level turnA cyclist speeding at 18 km/h on a level road takes a sharp circular turn of radius 3 m without redu🟢 ExactYes — the cyclist slips (v² = 25 > μrg = 3 m²s−2)
Concept On a level road the road can push the cyclist only straight up (normal) and horizontally backwards/sideways (friction). Gravity and the normal force are both vertical, so the entire centripetal requirement mv²/r has to be met by static friction alone. The phrase “without bending towards the centre” is there to shut off the other option: a leaning cyclist tilts the reaction so its horizontal component helps. Upright, the tyre must deliver everything, and it can deliver at most μsmg.
Formula Required force Fc = mv²/r, available friction fmax = μsmg, so the no-slip test is v² ≤ μsrg. Mass cancels from both sides, which is why the cyclist's weight never appears in the answer.
Solution
1. Convert: v = 18 km h−1 = 18 × (5/18) = 5 m/s; r = 3 m, μs = 0.1, g = 10 m/s².
2. Needed: ac = v²/r = 25/3 = 8.33 m/s².
3. Available: μsg = 0.1 × 10 = 1.0 m/s² (0.98 m/s² if you use g = 9.8).
4. Compare: 8.33 > 1.0, i.e. v² = 25 m²s−2 is far larger than μsrg = 3 m²s−2. The condition fails, so the cyclist slips.Once the demand exceeds fmax the contact breaks into sliding; static friction cannot “try harder”.
5. How badly? Safe speed here is vmax = √(μsrg) = √3 = 1.73 m/s = 6.2 km/h — the cyclist is riding at nearly 3× the limit.
6. Fix it either way: drop below 6.2 km/h, or widen the turn to r ≥ v²/(μsg) = 25 m.3 m is a footpath corner; 25 m is a real road bend — hence the lopsided numbers.

Also note that the answer is unchanged whether you take g = 9.8 or 10, so the examiner is testing the inequality, not arithmetic.

2Find the maximum speed with which an unpowered car of mass 1000 kg can take a corner of radius 20 m if the coefficient of friction between the tyres and the road is 0.5. Take g = 10 ms−2. — (Level-2)Maximum speed on a level cornerA car of mass 1000 kg moves on a circular track of radius 20 . If the coefficient of friction is...🟡 Similar10 m s−1
Concept “Unpowered” simply means no engine torque, so there is no tangential friction to worry about — the whole friction budget is free to point towards the centre. On a flat corner that budget is μN = μmg, and it must cover the centripetal demand mv²/r. The fastest safe speed is the one at which friction is exactly saturated.
Formula At the limit μmg = mvmax²/r ⇒ vmax = √(μrg). The 1000 kg is a red herring: m appears on both sides and cancels, so a truck and a bicycle skid at the same speed on the same corner.
Solution
1. Data: r = 20 m, μ = 0.5, g = 10 ms−2, m = 1000 kg (not needed).
2. Substitute: vmax = √(μrg) = √(0.5 × 20 × 10) = √100 = 10 m s−1.Choosing the product order 0.5×20 = 10 first turns the radicand into a perfect square with no calculator.
3. Cross-check with forces: fmax = μmg = 0.5 × 1000 × 10 = 5000 N, and mv²/r = 1000 × 100/20 = 5000 N. They match exactly, confirming saturation.
4. In everyday units 10 m/s = 36 km/h — deliberately slow, because r = 20 m is a tight corner.vmax ∝ √r, so doubling the corner radius only raises the limit by √2, not by 2.
5. Beyond 10 m/s the required force exceeds what the tyres can grip and the car understeers outwards along a wider arc — it does not fly off tangentially at once.
6. Sensitivity: halving μ to 0.25 (wet road) gives vmax = √50 = 7.07 m/s, a 29% drop for a 50% loss of grip.Again √, not linear — the standard exam follow-up.

3A coin is pushed down tangentially from an angular position θ on a cylindrical surface, with a velocity v as shown in the figure. If the coefficient of friction between the coin and the surface is μ, find the tangential acceleration of the coin. — (Level-3)Tangential acceleration on a curved surfaceA coin is pushed down tangentially from an angular position `theta ` on a cylindrical surface🟢 Exactat = g(sinθ − μcosθ) − μv²/R
Concept The coin sits on the inside of a cylindrical valley, at angular position θ measured from the lowest point, and is given a push down the surface. Two directions must be handled separately: along the radius the net force is the centripetal one (it does not accelerate the coin along its path), and along the tangent gravity drives it while friction resists. The subtlety: friction depends on N, and N depends on the speed — so v enters the tangential answer.
Formula Radial: N − mg cosθ = mv²/R. Tangential: mg sinθ − μN = mat. N is measured from the surface towards the centre, and the coin needs a net inward mv²/R, so N must exceed the radial weight component mg cosθ.
Solution
1. Resolve the weight at angular position θ: radial component mg cosθ (outward, away from the centre), tangential component mg sinθ (down the slope).
2. Radial equation: N − mg cosθ = mv²/R ⇒ N = m(g cosθ + v²/R).This is the step most students skip — taking N = mg cosθ (the static-incline value) drops the whole μv²/R term.
3. Friction magnitude: f = μN = μm(g cosθ + v²/R), directed up the surface because the coin is moving down it.
4. Tangential equation: mat = mg sinθ − μm(g cosθ + v²/R).Only the tangential components can change the speed; the radial ones merely bend the path.
5. Cancel m: at = g sinθ − μg cosθ − μv²/R = g(sinθ − μcosθ) − μv²/R.
6. Limits: at v = 0 it reduces to the incline result g(sinθ − μcosθ); at θ = 0 it gives at = −μv²/R, pure retardation.Both are standard results, confirming the sign convention.

⚡ JEE Trick On any curved track the normal force is never just mg cosθ — add (or subtract) mv²/R depending on whether the centre is above or below the block. Concave surface: N = m(g cosθ + v²/R). Convex surface (a dome): N = m(g cosθ − v²/R). Getting that one sign right is the whole question; the tangential algebra afterwards is two lines.
Answer: at = g(sinθ − μcosθ) − μv²/R.
4A pendulum consists of a light string of length 25 cm and a small bob of mass 150 g. It is set into motion so that it makes 90 rpm as a conical pendulum. Calculate the ratio of increase in kinetic energy to the increase in gravitational potential energy of the bob. (Take π = 22/7) — (Level-4)Conical pendulum — ΔKE : ΔPEConical Pendulum | Derivation & Numericals | Physics | HSC(12th) Science🟡 Similar1.6  [ratio = (1 + cosθ)/(2cosθ), cosθ = 0.45]
Concept Setting a hanging bob into conical motion does two things at once: it lifts the bob by L(1 − cosθ) and it gives it speed v = ωL sinθ. Both are governed by the same cone angle θ, which the rotation rate fixes through cosθ = g/(Lω²). So the ratio of the two energy changes must come out as a pure function of cosθ — mass and length cancel completely.
Formula Cone angle cosθ = g/(Lω²); energies ΔK = ½mω²L²sin²θ, ΔU = mgL(1 − cosθ). ΔK is measured from rest (the bob hanging still), so the “increase” in KE is the whole KE.
Solution
1. Rate: n = 90 rpm = 1.5 rev/s, so ω = 2πn = 3π = 3(22/7) = 66/7 = 9.4286 rad/s and ω² = 88.90 s−2.
2. Cone angle: cosθ = g/(Lω²) = 10/(0.25 × 88.90) = 10/22.22 = 0.45, so θ = 63.3° and sinθ = 0.893.This comes from dividing T sinθ = mω²L sinθ by T cosθ = mg — the tension drops out.
3. Build the ratio symbolically first: ΔK/ΔU = ω²L sin²θ / [2g(1 − cosθ)].
4. Replace ω²L by g/cosθ using step 2: ΔK/ΔU = (1 − cos²θ)/[2cosθ(1 − cosθ)] = (1 + cosθ)/(2cosθ).The factor (1 − cosθ) cancels top and bottom — that is what makes the answer clean.
5. Evaluate: (1 + 0.45)/(2 × 0.45) = 1.45/0.90 = 1.611 ≈ 1.6.
6. Note m = 150 g and L = 25 cm never appear in step 4 — only the spin rate matters. A faster spin lowers cosθ and pushes the ratio up without limit; as ω → √(g/L) the cone collapses and the ratio → 1.

Everything collapses to (1 + cosθ)/(2cosθ), a one-line formula worth memorising for conical-pendulum energy questions. The π = 22/7 instruction exists only to make cosθ land on the round 0.45; with π² = 9.87 you would get 0.4556 and the same 1.6.

If the coefficient of friction between the wheels of a race car and the road is 0.2, what is the (i) optimum speed of the race car (tan15° = 0.768) to avoid wear and tear on its tyres, and the (ii) maximum permissible speed to avoid slipping? — (Level-5)Banked track — optimum & maximum speedA circular racetrack of radius 300 m is banked at an angle of 15° If the coefficient of friction bet🟢 Exact(i) 48 m/s   (ii) 58.6 m/s ⚠
Concept A banked track has two special speeds. At the optimum speed the normal reaction alone bends the car, friction is zero and the tyres suffer no sideways scrub. Above it the car tends to slide outwards and up the bank, so friction turns on pointing down the slope; the speed at which that friction saturates is the maximum permissible speed. The two formulas differ only in how μ enters.
Formula v0 = √(rg tanθ) and vmax = √[rg(tanθ + μ)/(1 − μtanθ)]. (1 − μtanθ) in the denominator, not (1 + μtanθ) — the plus sign belongs to vmin = √[rg(tanθ − μ)/(1 + μtanθ)].
Solution
1. Data as printed: r = 300 m, tanθ = 0.768, μ = 0.2, g = 10 m/s², so rg = 3000.
2. (i) v0 = √(3000 × 0.768) = √2304 = 48 m/s.2304 = 48² exactly, which is how you know 0.768 was reverse-engineered to give a round answer.
3. (ii) Numerator: tanθ + μ = 0.768 + 0.2 = 0.968. Denominator: 1 − μtanθ = 1 − 0.1536 = 0.8464.
4. vmax = √(3000 × 0.968/0.8464) = √(3000 × 1.1437) = √3431 = 58.6 m/s.
5. ⚠ The printed key gives 50.17 m/s. Reverse it: 50.17² = 2517 = 3000 × 0.968/1.1536, i.e. the key divided by (1 + μtanθ). That is the minimum-speed denominator used in the maximum-speed formula — a sign slip, not a rounding difference.
6. Second defect: tan15° is 0.2679, not 0.768 (0.768 is tan 37.5°). Using the true value: v0 = √(3000 × 0.2679) = 28.3 m/s and vmax = √(3000 × 0.4679/0.9464) = 38.5 m/s — the familiar NCERT pair.

1Railway tracks are banked at the curves so that: (a) the train may not fall down inwards (b) the weight of the train may be reduced (c) the necessary centripetal force may be obtained from the horizontal component of the reaction of the train (d) no frictional force may be produced between the wheel and the trackWhy railway tracks are bankedRailway tracks are banked at the curves so that :🟢 Exactcthe necessary centripetal force may be obtained from the horizontal component of the reaction
Concept A train rounding a curve needs an inward force mv²/r. On level track that force can only come from the rail pressing sideways on the wheel flanges — a brutal, wearing contact. Raising the outer rail tilts the bed, and with it the normal reaction, which then acquires a horizontal component pointing towards the centre — enough on its own at the design speed.
Formula On a banked bed N sinθ = mv²/r and N cosθ = mg, giving tanθ = v²/(rg). N sinθ is the horizontal component of the reaction — exactly the wording of option (c).
Solution
1. Identify the forces available on a banked track: weight mg (vertical), normal reaction N (perpendicular to the bed), and friction (which the design tries to avoid using).
2. Resolve N: vertical part N cosθ carries the weight, horizontal part N sinθ points towards the centre of the curve and is the centripetal force.Nothing else in the free-body diagram has an inward horizontal component, so the reaction must supply it.
3. Hence (c) is the correct statement of the purpose of banking.
4. (a) “may not fall down inwards” is wrong twice over: a train on a curve tends to be thrown outwards, and banking is not an anti-toppling device but a force-supply device.
5. (b) “weight may be reduced” is impossible — mg depends on mass and g only. Banking redistributes the reaction; it does not change the weight.Note N = mg/cosθ is actually larger than mg on a bank, not smaller.
6. (d) is the tempting near-miss: at the design speed friction happens to be zero, but that is a consequence, not the purpose, and at any other speed friction reappears.Away from v0 = √(rg tanθ) friction is very much produced — so (d) is not true in general.

2A car sometimes overturns while taking a turn. Concept Overturning is a torque question, not a force question. As the car corners, the road must push it inwards with mv²/r, but that push acts at the tyres while the weight acts at the centre of gravity, a height h above the road. The offset creates a moment that tries to tip the car about the outer wheels, and the way the two wheels share the weight shifts to resist it. Overturning begins the instant one wheel's share reaches zero.
Formula Taking moments about the outer contact, Ninner = mg/2 − mv²h/(ra) where a is the track width. Nouter = mg/2 + mv²h/(ra). The two must still add to mg, so whatever the inner wheel loses the outer wheel gains.
Solution
1. Work in the car's frame: a centrifugal force mv²/r acts outwards through the centre of gravity, at height h.
2. That force has a moment mv²h/r about the road, tending to lift the wheels on the inside of the bend and press down the outside ones.Picture leaning outwards on a roundabout — the load transfers away from the inner side.
3. Balancing moments about the outer contact point gives Ninner = mg/2 − mv²h/(ra), which falls as v grows.
4. Setting Ninner = 0 gives the critical speed v² = rga/(2h); above it the inner wheel has already left the road.A normal reaction cannot go negative — the ground can push but not pull — so zero is the physical limit.
5. Therefore the inner wheel leaves the ground first: option (a).
6. (b) reverses the load transfer; (c) would require the whole car to lift straight up, which no horizontal force can do; (d) is wrong because the geometry is not symmetric — the bend has a definite inside.
