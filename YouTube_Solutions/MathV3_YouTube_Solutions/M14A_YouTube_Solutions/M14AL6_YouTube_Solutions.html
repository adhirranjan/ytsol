1 (i)Fill in the blanks: The point which is equidistant from all three vertices of a triangle, lying in the plane of the triangle, is the ______.CircumcentreThe co-ordinates of the point which is equidistance from the three vertices of the triangle AOB as🟢 Exactcircumcentre
Concept Every point on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equally far from the segment’s two end-points. Push that idea onto all three sides of a triangle at once: the single point that is the same distance from the three vertices must sit on the perpendicular bisector of every side simultaneously.
Formula SA = SB = SC ⇒ S is the circumcentre. S is the common point of the three side perpendicular bisectors and the centre of the circle through A, B and C, whose radius is R = SA = SB = SC.
Solution
1. Let the triangle be ABC and suppose a point S satisfies SA = SB = SC.
2. SA = SB means S is equidistant from A and B, so S lies on the perpendicular bisector of side AB. The locus of all points equidistant from two fixed points is precisely the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them.
3. In the same way SB = SC forces S onto the perpendicular bisector of side BC.
4. Two non-parallel bisectors cross in exactly one point, so S is fixed; the third side’s bisector then passes through the same S, making SA = SB = SC hold together.
5. This common point S is named the circumcentre, and the circle centred at S with radius R = SA passes through all three vertices. That circle is the circumscribed circle — S literally “circum”-scribes the triangle.
6. S need not fall inside the triangle: for an obtuse triangle the circumcentre lies outside it, and for a right triangle it lands on a side.
 side AB  -> perp bisector  \
 side BC  -> perp bisector   >  meet at S
 side CA  -> perp bisector  /
 SA = SB = SC = R  (circumradius)
⚡ JEE Trick “Equidistant from the three vertices” is a trigger phrase — the answer is always the circumcentre, and you only ever need two perpendicular bisectors to pin it down; the third is a free check. In coordinates just set SA2 = SB2 = SC2 and solve the two resulting linear equations.
Answer: circumcentre.
1 (ii)Fill in the blanks: For a right-angled triangle the circumcentre lies at the mid-point of the ______.Circumcentre of a right triangleTheorem Proof: Circumcenter of Right Triangle lies on the midpoint of hypotenuse (Circumcircle)🟢 Exacthypotenuse
Concept The circumcentre is the centre of the circle through all three vertices. In a right-angled triangle the angle in a semicircle is 90° (Thales’ theorem), so the side opposite the right angle must be a diameter of that circle — and the centre of a circle is the mid-point of any diameter.
Formula ∠C = 90° ⇒ circumcentre S = mid-point of AB (the hypotenuse), R = AB/2. The hypotenuse subtends the right angle, so by the converse of Thales it is the diameter; its mid-point is therefore equidistant from A, B and C.
Solution
1. Take triangle ABC right-angled at C, so AB is the hypotenuse.
2. Draw the circle on AB as diameter. By Thales’ theorem every point of the semicircle sees AB at 90°, and C sees it at exactly 90°, so C lies on this circle. Converse of the angle-in-a-semicircle result: a 90° angle means the opposite side is a diameter.
3. Hence all three vertices A, B, C lie on one circle whose diameter is AB; that circle is the circumcircle.
4. The circumcentre S is the centre of this circle, i.e. the mid-point of the diameter AB. The centre of a circle is the mid-point of each of its diameters, so S = mid-point of the hypotenuse.
5. Therefore the circumradius is R = AB/2, and S is equidistant (= AB/2) from all three vertices, including the right-angle vertex C.
6. So for a right triangle the circumcentre lies on the triangle itself, exactly at the middle of the hypotenuse.
      C
     / \
    /   \        S = mid-point of AB
   A--S--B        R = SA = SB = SC = AB/2
   hypotenuse AB  is the diameter
⚡ JEE Trick For any right triangle with legs on the axes, the circumcentre is instantly the mid-point of the hypotenuse — no perpendicular-bisector algebra needed. Example: vertices O(0,0), A(a,0), B(0,b) give circumcentre (a/2, b/2) and circumradius √(a2+b2)/2.
Answer: hypotenuse (circumcentre = its mid-point).
1 (iii)Fill in the blanks: The point of intersection of the altitudes of a triangle is the ______.OrthocentreAltitude of a Triangle - Finding The Orthocenter🟢 Exactorthocentre
Concept An altitude of a triangle is the perpendicular dropped from a vertex to the opposite side (or its extension). A triangle has three altitudes, and a classical theorem guarantees that these three lines are concurrent — they all pass through one common point.
Formula altitude from A ⊥ BC, from B ⊥ CA, from C ⊥ AB; the three meet at H = orthocentre. Concurrency of the altitudes is proved by drawing the medial parallel triangle, where the altitudes become its perpendicular bisectors, which are known to meet.
Solution
1. In triangle ABC drop AD ⊥ BC from vertex A; this is one altitude.
2. Similarly drop BE ⊥ CA and CF ⊥ AB, giving the other two altitudes. Each altitude pairs a vertex with the side facing it and meets that side at a right angle.
3. Take any two altitudes, say AD and BE; being non-parallel they cross at a single point H.
4. The altitude-concurrency theorem then shows the third altitude CF also passes through H, so all three are concurrent. Through the parallel-triangle construction the three altitudes reduce to three perpendicular bisectors, which always meet in one point.
5. This common point H of the altitudes is called the orthocentre of the triangle.
6. Like the circumcentre, H can lie inside (acute triangle), on (right triangle), or outside (obtuse triangle) the triangle.
 AD _|_ BC  \
 BE _|_ CA   >  concurrent at H
 CF _|_ AB  /
 H = orthocentre
⚡ JEE Trick To find H in coordinates, you never need all three altitudes: write just two altitude lines (slope = −1/slope of the opposite side, through the opposite vertex) and solve them. The orthocentre, centroid and circumcentre are also collinear on the Euler line, with G dividing HO in ratio 2:1.
Answer: orthocentre.
1 (iv)Fill in the blanks: For a right-angled triangle the orthocentre lies at the vertex having the ______.Orthocentre of a right triangleFinding the Orthocenter of a Right Triangle🟢 Exactright angle (90°)
Concept The orthocentre is where the three altitudes meet. In a right-angled triangle two of the sides — the legs — are already perpendicular to each other, so each leg is itself an altitude. Both of these altitudes pass through the vertex where the legs meet, which is the right-angle vertex.
Formula ∠C = 90° ⇒ CA ⊥ CB ⇒ the two legs are altitudes ⇒ orthocentre H = C. An altitude from a vertex is perpendicular to the opposite side; since each leg is already perpendicular to the other leg, the leg doubles as the altitude to that side.
Solution
1. Let triangle ABC be right-angled at C, so the legs CA and CB meet at 90°.
2. The altitude from A must be perpendicular to the opposite side BC. But CA is already perpendicular to CB, so side CA is the altitude from A. The altitude to BC through A is unique, and CA already satisfies CA ⊥ CB.
3. Likewise CB is the altitude from B, since CB ⊥ CA.
4. Both of these altitudes, CA and CB, pass through the vertex C where they intersect.
5. The third altitude (from C, perpendicular to the hypotenuse AB) also passes through C. So all three altitudes meet at C. Every altitude of the triangle therefore contains the point C, making C the orthocentre.
6. Hence in a right triangle the orthocentre coincides with the vertex holding the right angle (90°).
      A
      |\
      | \   CA and CB are the legs
      |  \  CA _|_ CB  at  C
      C---B  => orthocentre H = C
⚡ JEE Trick Pair this with 1(ii): in a right triangle the orthocentre sits at the 90° vertex while the circumcentre sits at the mid-point of the hypotenuse. If a question gives a right triangle and asks for its orthocentre, just read off the right-angle vertex — no calculation at all.
Answer: right angle (90°) — the orthocentre is that vertex.

Concept A triangle has four classical centres, each defined by a different set of three concurrent cevians or bisectors: the medians, the internal angle bisectors, the perpendicular bisectors of the sides, and the altitudes. Matching each construction to its centre is pure definition recall.
Formula medians → centroid; angle bisectors → incentre; perpendicular bisectors of sides → circumcentre; altitudes → orthocentre. A median joins a vertex to the mid-point of the opposite side; an angle bisector splits a vertex angle; a side perpendicular bisector is equidistant from two vertices; an altitude is perpendicular from a vertex.
Solution
1. (A) The three medians meet at the centroid G, which divides each median 2:1 from the vertex. So A → (s). G is also the triangle’s centre of mass and the average of the three vertices.
2. (B) The three internal angle bisectors meet at the incentre I, the centre of the inscribed circle that touches all three sides. So B → (r).
3. (C) The three perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet at the circumcentre, equidistant from the vertices. So C → (q). Each side’s perpendicular bisector is the locus equidistant from that side’s two end-vertices.
4. (D) The three altitudes meet at the orthocentre. So D → (p).
5. Collecting the pairs: A→s, B→r, C→q, D→p.
 (A) medians        -> (s) centroid
 (B) angle bisectors-> (r) incentre
 (C) perp bisectors -> (q) circumcentre
 (D) altitudes      -> (p) orthocentre
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the two “equidistant” centres: incentre is equidistant from the three sides (so bisectors), circumcentre is equidistant from the three vertices (so perpendicular bisectors). The centroid, circumcentre and orthocentre are collinear on the Euler line; the incentre generally is not.
Answer: A→s, B→r, C→q, D→p.

Formula (a1x+b1y+c1)/√(a12+b12) = ±(a2x+b2y+c2)/√(a22+b22). With c1,c2>0, the “+” branch is the acute bisector when a1a2+b1b2<0 and the obtuse one when it is >0.
Solution
1. Lines: 2x − y + 4 = 0 and x − 2y − 1 = 0 (writing x − 2y = 1 as x − 2y − 1 = 0).
2. Make both constants positive. Line 1 already has +4. Multiply line 2 by −1: −x + 2y + 1 = 0, constant +1. The sign test for acute/obtuse is only valid after both c terms are made positive.
3. Now a1=2, b1=−1, a2=−1, b2=2. Compute a1a2+b1b2 = (2)(−1)+(−1)(2) = −4 < 0. Negative value ⇒ take the “+” sign for the acute bisector.

Concept When a ray hits the x-axis and reflects, the reflected line is the mirror image of the incident line in the x-axis. Reflection in the x-axis sends every point (x, y) to (x, −y), so to get the image of any curve or line you simply replace y by −y in its equation.
Formula reflection in x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y); so f(x, y)=0 → f(x, −y)=0. The x-axis is the mirror, x-coordinates are unchanged and y-coordinates flip sign, which is exactly the substitution y → −y.
Solution

2. The mirror is the x-axis, so apply the map (x, y) → (x, −y): replace y by −y. A point on the reflected ray is the mirror image of a point on the incident ray, and mirroring in the x-axis only negates the y-coordinate.
3. Substitute: x + (−y) = 2, which is x − y = 2.
4. Write in standard form: x − y − 2 = 0.
5. Sanity check: incident line x+y=2 meets the x-axis (y=0) at (2, 0); the reflected line x−y−2=0 also passes through (2, 0), as a reflected ray must share the point of incidence. Both lines hit the mirror at the same point, confirming the reflection is correct.

Concept A triangle carries four classical centres, and each is the meeting point of one special family of lines. 5. Slope of BC = (−2/5 + 28/13)/(9/5 + 23/13) = (114/65)/(232/65) = 57/116. Both images lie on BC, so their join IS BC.
6. Through (9/5, −2/5): 57x − 116y = 149. Option (a) reads 57x + 116y = 149 (wrong y-sign), so the answer is (d).

8The lines y = m1x, y = m2x and y = m3x make equal intercepts on the line x + y = 1. Then: (a) 2(1 + m1)(1 + m3) = (1 + m2)(2 + m1 + m3) (b) (1 + m1)(1 + m3) = (1 + m2)(1 + m1 + m3) (c) (1 + m1)(1 + m2) = (1 + m3)(2 + m1 + m3) (d) 2(1 + m1)(1 + m3) = (1 + m2)(1 + m1 + m3)Equal intercepts on a transversalLine making equal intercepts and passing through a given point - coordinate geometry🟡 Similara2(1 + m1)(1 + m3) = (1 + m2)(2 + m1 + m3)
Concept Each line y = mix cuts the transversal x + y = 1 at one point. “Equal intercepts” means the three crossing points are equally spaced along x + y = 1, so the middle one (from m2) is the midpoint of the outer two (from m1 and m3). On a straight line, equal spacing of x-coordinates is enough to express this as a midpoint condition.
Formula x-coordinate of crossing: xi = 1/(1 + mi). Substituting y = mix into x + y = 1 gives x(1 + mi) = 1, so xi = 1/(1 + mi).
Solution
1. Crossing abscissae: x1 = 1/(1 + m1), x2 = 1/(1 + m2), x3 = 1/(1 + m3).
2. Equal intercepts ⇒ the middle point is the midpoint of the other two: 2x2 = x1 + x3. Equally spaced points on a line satisfy the midpoint relation in their x-coordinates.
3. So 2/(1 + m2) = 1/(1 + m1) + 1/(1 + m3).
4. Combine the right side: 1/(1 + m1) + 1/(1 + m3) = (2 + m1 + m3)/[(1 + m1)(1 + m3)].
5. Cross-multiply: 2(1 + m1)(1 + m3) = (1 + m2)(2 + m1 + m3). Clearing both denominators keeps the relation symmetric in m1 and m3.
6. This is exactly option (a).

9A triangle is formed by the lines x + y = 0, x − y = 0 and lx + my = 1. If l and m vary subject to the condition l2 + m2 = 1, then the locus of its circumcentre is: (a) (x2 − y2)2 = x2 + y2 (b) (x2 + y2)2 = (x2 − y2) (c) (x2 + y2) = 4x2y2 (d) (x2 − y2)2 = (x2 + y2)2Locus of a circumcentreLocus of the circumcentre of a variable triangle with a side lx+my=1🟡 Similara(x2 − y2)2 = x2 + y2
Concept The lines x + y = 0 and x − y = 0 have slopes −1 and 1, so they are perpendicular and meet at the origin. The triangle they make with lx + my = 1 is therefore right-angled at O, and the circumcentre of a right triangle is the midpoint of its hypotenuse — here the segment of lx + my = 1 cut off between the two fixed lines.
Formula circumcentre = midpoint of the hypotenuse. A right angle sits at O, so O lies on the circle with the hypotenuse as diameter; its centre is the hypotenuse midpoint.
Solution
1. Meet lx + my = 1 with y = −x: (l − m)x = 1, giving A = (1/(l − m), −1/(l − m)).
2. Meet lx + my = 1 with y = x: (l + m)x = 1, giving B = (1/(l + m), 1/(l + m)).
3. Midpoint (h, k): h = (1/2)[1/(l − m) + 1/(l + m)] = l/(l2 − m2). The two fractions add to 2l/(l2 − m2), halved gives l/(l2 − m2).
4. Similarly k = (1/2)[−1/(l − m) + 1/(l + m)] = −m/(l2 − m2).
5. With l2 + m2 = 1: h2 + k2 = (l2 + m2)/(l2 − m2)2 = 1/(l2 − m2)2, and h2 − k2 = (l2 − m2)/(l2 − m2)2 = 1/(l2 − m2). The constraint l2 + m2 = 1 collapses the numerator of h2 + k2 to 1.
6. Therefore (h2 − k2)2 = 1/(l2 − m2)2 = h2 + k2, i.e. (x2 − y2)2 = x2 + y2, option (a).

10The line 3x − 2y = 24 meets the x-axis at A and the y-axis at B. The perpendicular bisector of AB meets the line through (0, −1) and parallel to the x-axis at C. Then C is: (a) (−7/2, −1) (b) (−15/2, −1) (c) (−11/2, −1) (d) (−13/2, −1)Intersection of a perpendicular bisector with a horizontal lineLine 3x + 2y = 24 meets x-axis at A and y-axis at B, perpendicular bisector of AB🟢 Exacta(−7/2, −1)
Concept The line 3x − 2y = 24 hits the axes at A and B. The perpendicular bisector of AB passes through the midpoint of AB with slope the negative reciprocal of AB's slope. Point C is where that bisector crosses the horizontal line y = −1 (through (0, −1), parallel to the x-axis), so we just substitute y = −1.
Formula perpendicular bisector: y − ym = −(1/slopeAB)(x − xm). It runs through the midpoint (xm, ym) of AB and is perpendicular to AB.
Solution
1. On the x-axis (y = 0): 3x = 24 ⇒ x = 8, so A = (8, 0).
2. On the y-axis (x = 0): −2y = 24 ⇒ y = −12, so B = (0, −12).
3. Midpoint M = ((8 + 0)/2, (0 − 12)/2) = (4, −6); slope AB = (−12 − 0)/(0 − 8) = 3/2. Rise over run between A and B gives the slope 3/2.
4. Perpendicular slope = −2/3, so the bisector is y + 6 = −(2/3)(x − 4).
5. C lies on y = −1: −1 + 6 = −(2/3)(x − 4) ⇒ 5 = −(2/3)(x − 4). The target line through (0, −1) parallel to the x-axis is simply y = −1.
6. Solve: x − 4 = −15/2 ⇒ x = 4 − 15/2 = −7/2, so C = (−7/2, −1), option (a).
