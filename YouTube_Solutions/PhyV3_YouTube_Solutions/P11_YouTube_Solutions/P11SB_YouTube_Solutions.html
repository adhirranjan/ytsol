1Two particles, 1 and 2, each of mass m, are connected by a massless spring, and are on a horizontal frictionless plane, as shown in the figure. Initially, the two particles, with their center of mass at x0, are oscillating with amplitude ‘a’ and angular frequency ω. Thus, their positions at time t are given by x1(t) = (x0 + d) + a sinωt and x2(t) = (x0 − d) − a sinωt, respectively, where d > 2a. Particle 3 of mass m moves towards this system with speed u0 = aω/2, and undergoes instantaneous elastic collision with particle 2, at time t0. Finally, particles 1 and 2 acquire a center of mass speed vcm and oscillate with amplitude b and the same angular frequency ω. (JEE Advanced 2024 Paper 2) If the collision occurs at time t0 = π/(2ω), then the value of 4b2/a2 will be ____. (a) 4.25 (b) 3.25 (c) 4.50 (d) 3.50Two-body spring oscillator, elastic hitSHM + Elastic Collision Problem | JEE Advanced Level Mechanics | Two particles 1 and 2, each of mass m🟢 Exacta4.25
Concept A spring-linked pair is a two-body oscillator: its entire internal state is carried by the relative coordinate s = x1 − x2 − 2d, measured from the natural length 2d. The collision is instantaneous, so it changes velocities but not positions — s survives the hit untouched while vrel jumps. The tempting shortcut, “new amplitude = old amplitude + kick amplitude”, is wrong because amplitude is not additive; only the phase-space invariant s2 + (vrel/ω)2 is conserved through free SHM.
Formula S2 = s2 + (vrel/ω)2 for the relative coordinate, plus the equal-mass elastic rule v2′ = u0, v3′ = v2. Both particles have mass m, so the reduced mass is μ = m/2 and k = μω2; the invariant then follows from energy without ever needing k itself.
Solution
1. Relative coordinate: s(t) = x1 − x2 − 2d = 2a sinωt, so the relative motion has amplitude 2a while each particle swings by a about the centre of mass.Working in s removes the centre-of-mass drift for free — that drift never affects amplitude.
2. Velocities: v1 = aωcosωt, v2 = −aωcosωt. At t0 = π/(2ω), ωt0 = π/2 → cos = 0, so v1 = v2 = 0.This is the turning point: the pair is momentarily frozen with all energy in the spring.
3. Position at that instant: s = 2a sin(π/2) = 2a — maximum stretch, and it is unchanged by the instantaneous collision.
4. Equal masses in a head-on elastic collision exchange velocities: particle 2 leaves with u0 = aω/2 (towards +x), particle 3 is left at rest.No need for the two-line elastic formula — equal masses always swap, which is why the problem chose mass m for particle 3.
5. New relative velocity: vrel = v1 − v2 = 0 − aω/2 = −aω/2, so (vrel/ω)2 = a2/4.
6. New relative amplitude: S2 = s2 + (vrel/ω)2 = (2a)2 + a2/4 = 4a2 + 0.25a2 = 4.25a2.
7. Each particle’s amplitude is b, so the relative amplitude is S = 2b: 4b2 = 4.25a2 ⇒ 4b2/a2 = 4.25 — matching the key.The answer is a pure number, so the unknown spring constant k never had to be found.
 ledger at t0 = pi/(2w)   (before -> after the hit)
 ------------------------------------------------
 s   (stretch)   2a        ->   2a    (unchanged)
 v1              0         ->   0
 v2              0         ->   +aw/2 (swap with 3)
 v3             +aw/2      ->   0
 s_dot = v1-v2   0         ->   -aw/2
 S^2 = s^2 + (s_dot/w)^2 = 4a^2 + a^2/4 = 4.25 a^2
 S = 2b  ->  4b^2/a^2 = 4.25
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is thinking a collision must always add speed to the spring — at t0 = π/(2ω) the pair is at rest at maximum stretch, so the kick is delivered cleanly and simply adds a2/4 to the 4a2 already stored. Option (c) 4.50 is the classic reduced-mass slip: using the full mass m instead of μ = m/2 in the relative kinetic energy doubles the kick to a2/2. Options (b) 3.25 and (d) 3.50 are the same two numbers with the sign flipped — they assume the collision drains the oscillation, which an incoming particle never does.
Answer: (a) 4.25.
2Two particles, 1 and 2, each of mass m, are connected by a massless spring, and are on a horizontal frictionless plane, as shown in the figure. Initially, the two particles, with their center of mass at x0, are oscillating with amplitude ‘a’ and angular frequency ω. Thus, their positions at time t are given by x1(t) = (x0 + d) + a sinωt and x2(t) = (x0 − d) − a sinωt, respectively, where d > 2a. Particle 3 of mass m moves towards this system with speed u0 = aω/2, and undergoes instantaneous elastic collision with particle 2, at time t0. Finally, particles 1 and 2 acquire a center of mass speed vcm and oscillate with amplitude b and the same angular frequency ω. (JEE Advanced 2024 Paper 2) If the collision occurs at time t0 = 0, then the value of vcm/(aω) will be ____. (a) 0.75 (b) 0.71 (c) 0.79 (d) 0.77COM velocity after elastic collisionJEE ADVANCED 2024 : Two particles, 1 and 2, each of mass m, are connected by a massless spring🟢 Exacta0.75
Concept The centre-of-mass speed of the pair (1, 2) after the hit depends only on their two velocities at that instant — the spring, however stretched, is an internal agent and cannot shift vcm. The whole question therefore reduces to: what is v2 just before t0 = 0, and what does the elastic collision replace it with? The obvious wrong move is to assume particle 2 was at rest (as it was in the π/(2ω) version); at t0 = 0 the pair is passing through the natural length, so both particles are at their maximum speed aω.
Formula vcm = (mv1 + mv2)/(m + m) = (v1 + v2)/2, with the equal-mass elastic rule v2′ = u0, v3′ = v2. Momentum of the pair is redefined by the collision, so vcm must be recomputed from the post-collision velocities, not carried over.
Solution
1. Differentiate the given positions: v1 = aωcosωt and v2 = −aωcosωt; at t0 = 0 this gives v1 = +aω and v2 = −aω.At t = 0 the separation is x1 − x2 = 2d, the natural length — zero spring energy, so the speeds are maximal. That is exactly the opposite of the previous part.
2. Geometry check: particle 3 comes from the left with u0 = +aω/2 while particle 2 is moving left with −aω, so they are closing head-on — the collision is legitimate.
3. Equal masses, head-on and elastic → the two swap velocities: v2′ = +aω/2 and v3′ = −aω.Check it against the two conservation laws: momentum −aω + aω/2 is unchanged, and the kinetic energies (aω)2 + (aω/2)2 merely trade owners.
4. Particle 1 is untouched by the instantaneous collision, so v1′ = +aω still.The spring transmits force only over finite time; an instantaneous impulse on 2 cannot reach 1 through it.
5. New centre-of-mass speed: vcm = (v1′ + v2′)/2 = (aω + aω/2)/2 = (3aω/2)/2 = 3aω/4.
6. Required ratio: vcm/(aω) = (3aω/4)/(aω) = 3/4 = 0.75, agreeing with the key.The answer is a clean fraction; the three decimal-looking distractors 0.71, 0.77, 0.79 exist purely to make you doubt an exact 3/4.
7. Sanity: from now on the pair drifts right at 3aω/4 while oscillating about that drifting centre — no external horizontal force acts, so this vcm is permanent.
 ledger at t0 = 0     (before  ->  after the hit)
 -----------------------------------------------
 stretch x1-x2-2d      0      ->   0
 v1                  +aw      ->  +aw      (untouched)
 v2                  -aw      ->  +aw/2    (swap)
 v3                  +aw/2    ->  -aw
 v_cm = (v1+v2)/2  = (aw + aw/2)/2 = 3aw/4
 v_cm/(aw) = 0.75
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is carrying over the previous part’s picture, where the pair was frozen at maximum stretch; here at t0 = 0 particle 2 is racing towards particle 3 at aω, and swapping that away is what makes vcm so large. Answer 0.25 (not offered) is what you get if you wrongly leave v2 = −aω and only add the incoming momentum. The three ugly decimals decode as near-misses built on √2 arithmetic: 0.71 ≈ 1/√2 and 0.79 ≈ 1/√2 + 0.08 tempt anyone who inserts a cos(π/4) that this problem never contains.
Answer: (a) 0.75.
3Multiple Correct Answer Question. A block of mass M has a circular cut with a frictionless surface as shown. The block rests on the horizontal frictionless surface of a fixed table. Initially the right edge of the block is at x = 0, in a coordinate system fixed to the table. A point mass m is released from rest at the topmost point of the path as shown and it slides down. When the mass loses contact with the block its position is x and velocity is v. At that instant which of the following options is correct? (JEE Advanced 2017 Paper 1) (a) The velocity of the point mass m is v = √[2gR / (1 + m/M)] (b) The velocity of the block M is v = −(m/M)√(2gR) (c) The position of the point mass is x = −√2 · mR/(m + M) (d) The x component of displacement of the centre of mass of block M is −mR/(m + M)Momentum & energy on a movable trackJEE Advanced 2017 Physics Paper 1 (Q 2) || A block of mass M has a circular cut with a frictionless🟢 Exacta,d
Concept Table and cut are both frictionless, so no horizontal external force ever acts on the (M + m) system: horizontal momentum stays zero and the system’s centre of mass never moves sideways. Those two statements do all the work — energy conservation then fixes the speeds and the fixed-COM condition fixes the displacements. The classic error is to use v = √(2gR) as if the block were bolted down; because M recoils, part of the released potential energy goes into M and m ends up slower than the textbook √(2gR).
Formula mv + MV = 0, mgR = ½mv2 + ½MV2, and mΔxm + MΔxM = 0. The mass leaves the block at the bottom of the arc where the surface is horizontal, so at that instant v is purely horizontal and both relations apply with scalars.
Solution
1. Momentum: 0 = mv + MV ⇒ V = −mv/M — the block slides the opposite way, which is why the answer for M carries a minus sign.
2. Energy, substituting V: mgR = ½mv2 + ½M(mv/M)2 = ½mv2(1 + m/M) ⇒ v = √[2gR/(1 + m/M)]. Option (a) is correct.Here v is the velocity relative to the ground; the drop is R because the mass falls from the top of the quarter-circle to its bottom.
3. Test (b): V = −(m/M)v = −(m/M)√[2gR/(1 + m/M)], which is smaller in magnitude than the printed −(m/M)√(2gR) — the option silently drops the recoil factor. (b) is wrong.The two agree only in the limit M ≫ m, the very case the question is built to exclude.
4. Displacements: the mass slides from the top of the arc to the bottom, i.e. a horizontal distance R relative to the block, so Δxm = ΔxM + R.
5. Fixed centre of mass: m(ΔxM + R) + MΔxM = 0 ⇒ ΔxM = −mR/(m + M). Option (d) is correct.Zero horizontal momentum from rest means the system COM stays put — no integration needed.
6. Test (c): Δxm = ΔxM + R = MR/(m + M), and the mass started at x = −R, so its final position is x = −R + MR/(m+M) = −mR/(m + M). No √2. (c) is wrong.
7. Only (a) and (d) survive — this matches the printed key (a, d).

⚡ JEE Trick In every “block free to recoil” problem, write the two zero-momentum lines before touching energy; the factor (1 + m/M) then appears by itself. Option (b) is the standard fixed-wedge answer smuggled in — it is what you get if you use the ground-frame energy equation for m alone. Option (c) dresses the right structure mR/(m+M) in a bogus √2, betting that you will accept any expression that “looks like” the derived one; the horizontal run of a quarter-circle is R, never R√2.

4Statement I If there is no external torque on a body about its centre of mass, then the velocity of the centre of mass remains constant. Statement II The linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant. (JEE Advanced 2007 Paper 2) (a) Statement I is true, Statement II is true; Statement II is a correct explanation for Statement I (b) Statement I is true, Statement II is true; Statement II is NOT a correct explanation for Statement I (c) Statement I is true, Statement II is false (d) Statement I is false, Statement II is trueAssertion–reason: torque vs forceCentre of Mass PYQs Part-2 | JEE Advanced Problem Solving | Master Rigid Body Concepts🟡 SimilardStatement I false, Statement II true
Concept Translation and rotation are governed by two independent equations: net external force controls the centre of mass, net external torque controls angular momentum. Statement I quietly swaps one for the other. The alternative reading, “no torque means nothing is pushing it”, is wrong: a force acting through the centre of mass has zero moment arm about that point, so it gives zero torque while still accelerating the body.
Formula ΣFext = M acm and, separately, Στcm = dLcm/dt. These are two different theorems with two different left-hand sides; neither implies the other, which is exactly the gap Statement I falls into.
Solution
1. Test Statement I with the cheapest counterexample — a ball in free fall: gravity acts at the COM, so τcm = r × F = 0 × F = 0, yet acm = g ≠ 0 and vcm grows every second.One valid counterexample kills a universal claim outright; no further analysis is needed.
2. Read it structurally: vcm = constant requires ΣFext = M acm = 0, a statement about force. τcm = 0 is a statement about moment. τcm = 0 does not force ΣFext = 0. Statement I is false.
3. The converse direction is true and is what students half-remember: ΣFext = 0 ⇒ vcm constant. Statement I is that implication run backwards through the wrong quantity.Assertion–reason papers reward spotting a reversed or substituted implication far more often than a numerical slip.
4. Test Statement II: “isolated” means ΣFext = 0, so dP/dt = ΣFext = 0 and P = Mvcm is constant. Statement II is true — it is Newton’s second law for a system, stated correctly.
5. Combination: false + true → only choice (d) fits. Options (a), (b), (c) all require Statement I to be true, and (c) additionally mislabels the standard momentum law as false.Once Statement I is falsified, three of the four options die at once — do the falsification first, it is the fastest route.
6. Note the explanation question never even arises: Statement II would explain “no external force ⇒ vcm constant”, but it cannot explain a false claim about torque.
7. This reproduces the printed key (d), so no discrepancy is flagged here.

⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an assertion–reason item pairs a torque premise with a translation conclusion (or the reverse), suspect the swap immediately — it is the single most reused trap in this format. Options (a) and (b) bait you into arguing “is II a correct explanation?” before checking whether I is even true. Option (c) is for the candidate who rejects Statement I but then over-corrects and doubts the momentum law itself.
Answer: (d) Statement I is false, Statement II is true.

At t = 1 s, |v⃗A| = |v⃗B| and velocities v⃗A and v⃗B of the particles are orthogonal to each other. At t = 1 s, the magnitude of angular momentum of particle (A) with respect to the position of particle (B) is √L kg m2 s−1. The value of L is ____. (JEE Main 2025 Shift 1)Angular momentum about a moving pointJEE Main 2025: The position vectors of two 1kg particles, A and B, are given by rA = (α1t²i + α2tj + α3tk)🟢 Exact90
Concept Two unknown constants n and p are hidden inside the data, and the problem hands you exactly two scalar conditions — equal speeds and perpendicular velocities — to pin them down. Only after n and p are fixed does the angular-momentum part become arithmetic. The trap is to reach for the cross product first with symbols still floating; the other trap is taking the angular momentum about the origin, whereas “with respect to the position of particle B” means the moment arm is r⃗A − r⃗B.
Formula L⃗ = (r⃗A − r⃗B) × m v⃗A, with v⃗ = dr⃗/dt. Angular momentum is always defined about a chosen reference point; shifting that point from O to B changes the moment arm and therefore the answer.
Solution
1. Differentiate: v⃗A = (2α1t, α2, α3) → at t = 1 s, v⃗A = (2, 3n, 2); v⃗B = (β1, 2β2t, β3) → v⃗B = (2, −2, 4p).
2. Equal speeds: 4 + 9n2 + 4 = 4 + 4 + 16p2 ⇒ 9n2 = 16p2.Squaring keeps it linear-in-squares; do not take square roots yet or you lose a sign you will need.
3. Orthogonality: v⃗A·v⃗B = 4 − 6n + 8p = 0 ⇒ 8p = 6n − 4, i.e. 4p = 3n − 2.
4. Substitute into step 2 as (4p)2 = (3n)2: (3n − 2)2 = 9n2 ⇒ 9n2 − 12n + 4 = 9n2 ⇒ n = 1/3, hence 4p = 1 − 2 = −1 and p = −1/4.The 9n2 cancels, so the system is linear in disguise — that cancellation is the whole design of the question.
5. Therefore α2 = 3n = 1 and β3 = 4p = −1. At t = 1 s: r⃗A = (1, 1, 2), r⃗B = (2, −1, −1), v⃗A = (2, 1, 2), v⃗B = (2, −2, −1). Check: |v⃗A| = |v⃗B| = 3 and v⃗A·v⃗B = 4 − 2 − 2 = 0. ✓
6. Moment arm: r⃗A − r⃗B = (−1, 2, 3). With m = 1 kg, L⃗ = (−1, 2, 3) × (2, 1, 2) = (2·2 − 3·1, 3·2 − (−1)·2, (−1)·1 − 2·2) = (1, 8, −5).
7. Magnitude: |L⃗| = √(12 + 82 + 52) = √(1 + 64 + 25) = √90, so √L = √90 and L = 90 — agreeing with the key.The answer asked for is L, not |L⃗|; stopping at √90 ≈ 9.49 is the most common lost mark on this question.

6The center of mass of a thin rectangular plate (fig − x) with sides of length a and b, whose mass per unit area (σ) varies as σ = σ0x/(ab) (where σ0 is a constant), would be — the plate occupies 0 ≤ x ≤ a, 0 ≤ y ≤ b with one corner at the origin O. (JEE Main 2025 Shift 1) (a) (2a/3, b/2) (b) (a/2, b/2) (c) (a/3, b/2) (d) (2a/3, 2b/3)COM of a non-uniform laminaJEE MAIN 2025 (28 Jan Shift 1) The centre of mass of a thin rectangular plate with sides of length a and b🟢 Exacta(2a/3, b/2)
Concept The density σ = σ0x/(ab) grows linearly with x and is completely independent of y. That single observation splits the problem in two: along y the plate is uniform, so ycm is the plain geometric midpoint b/2 with no integration at all; along x the mass is biased towards the far edge, so xcm must lie beyond a/2. Assuming the geometric centre (a/2, b/2) is the standard error — that answer only holds for constant σ.
Formula xcm = ∫xσ dA / ∫σ dA, with strip element dA = b dx. Because σ depends only on x, a full-height vertical strip of width dx has uniform density, so the 2-D integral collapses to a 1-D one and the constant b cancels top and bottom.
Solution
1. Take a vertical strip at distance x, width dx, height b: its mass is dm = σ·b dx = (σ0x/ab)·b dx = (σ0/a)x dx.Choosing the strip along the direction of constant density is what makes this a one-line integral instead of a double integral.
2. Total mass: M = ∫0a(σ0/a)x dx = (σ0/a)(a2/2) = σ0a/2.
3. First moment about the y-axis: ∫x dm = ∫0a(σ0/a)x2 dx = (σ0/a)(a3/3) = σ0a2/3.
4. Divide: xcm = (σ0a2/3)/(σ0a/2) = (a2/3)·(2/a) = 2a/3.σ0 and b both cancel, as they must — a centre of mass cannot depend on the overall mass scale.
5. y-direction: σ contains no y, so every horizontal layer carries the same mass and ycm = b/2 by symmetry — verified formally by ∫0by dy / ∫0bdy = (b2/2)/b = b/2.
6. So the centre of mass is at (2a/3, b/2), which is option (a) and matches the printed key.
7. Sanity: 2a/3 > a/2, i.e. shifted towards the heavier (large-x) edge — exactly what a density rising with x demands.This one inequality check rejects options (b) and (c) instantly, before any integration.

7Two blocks of masses 10 kg and 30 kg are placed on the same straight line with coordinates (0, 0) cm and (x, 0) cm respectively. The distance through which the block of 30 kg must be moved to keep the position of centre of mass of the system unchanged is (JEE Main 2022 Shift 1) (a) 4 cm towards the 10 kg block (b) 2 cm away from the 10 kg block (c) 2 cm towards the 10 kg block (d) 4 cm away from the 10 kg blockZero net shift of centre of massTwo blocks of masses 10 kg and 30 kg are placed on the same straight line with coordinates (0,0) cm🟢 Exactc2 cm towards the 10 kg block
Concept Holding the centre of mass fixed means the total first moment m1Δx1 + m2Δx2 must vanish, so the two displacements are opposite in sign and inversely proportional to the masses. Notice what the question does not need: the unknown separation x never enters, because only changes in position matter. The seductive wrong move is to compute the original xcm and the new one and solve — correct, but three times the work, and it invites an arithmetic slip.
Formula Δxcm = (m1Δx1 + m2Δx2)/(m1 + m2) = 0. Differencing the definition xcm = Σmixi/Σmi kills every constant term, which is why x and the total mass 40 kg both drop out.
Solution
1. Set the axis: the 10 kg block sits at the origin and the 30 kg block at x > 0, so “towards the other block” means the 10 kg block moves Δx1 = +6 cm.Fixing the sign convention before substituting is the whole battle here — every wrong option is a sign or ratio error.
2. Impose the condition and solve in one line: 0 = 10(+6) + 30(Δx2) ⇒ 30Δx2 = −60 ⇒ Δx2 = −2 cm.
3. Interpret the minus sign: the 30 kg block moves 2 cm in the −x direction, i.e. towards the 10 kg block — option (c), agreeing with the printed key.Both blocks moving towards each other is the physical picture: their approach keeps the balance point pinned.
4. Ratio check: |Δx2|/|Δx1| = m1/m2 = 10/30 = 1/3, so 6 cm × 1/3 = 2 cm — the heavier block always moves less, by exactly the mass ratio.
5. Cross-check the long way with x = 10 cm say: initially xcm = (10·0 + 30·10)/40 = 7.5 cm; finally (10·6 + 30·8)/40 = (60 + 240)/40 = 7.5 cm. ✓ Unchanged, and the assumed value of x cancelled out as promised.
6. Reject (b) and (d): moving the 30 kg block away from the 10 kg block would shift both first moments the same way and drive xcm forward, not hold it.
7. Reject (a): 4 cm would demand a mass ratio 6:4 = 3:2, i.e. blocks of 30 kg and 20 kg — it uses the wrong pair of masses.

   10(+6) + 30(dx2) = 0  ->  dx2 = -2 cm

⚡ JEE Trick For a fixed centre of mass just read off the inverse mass ratio — here 10:30 means the 30 kg block moves one third of 6 cm — and then spend your effort on the direction word, which is where the marks are lost. Option (b) has the right 2 cm with the direction flipped and traps anyone who ignores the minus sign; options (a) and (d) both pair a wrong 4 cm (from the ratio 10:(30−10) or from halving 6+2) with the two directions, so that every careless route lands on a printed option.
Answer: (c) 2 cm towards the 10 kg block.
