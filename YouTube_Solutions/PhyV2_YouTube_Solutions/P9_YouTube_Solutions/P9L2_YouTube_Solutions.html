1In a collision, the total momentum of the system: (a) Always increases (b) Is not conserved (c) Is always conserved (d) Depends on the type of collision — (Level-1)Momentum conservation (concept)Conservation of Momentum in Collisions🟡 SimilarcIs always conserved
Concept Two different conservation laws get mixed up in collision problems. Momentum is a vector and is protected by Newton's third law itself — it is conserved in every collision, elastic or not. Kinetic energy is a scalar with no such automatic protection; it is conserved only in the special case of an elastic collision. This question tests whether you know which one is unconditional.
Formula p⃗total,before = m₁u⃗₁ + m₂u⃗₂ = m₁v⃗₁ + m₂v⃗₂ = p⃗total,after This holds regardless of whether KE is conserved — it comes from Newton's third law, not from the elastic/inelastic assumption.
Solution
1. During the collision, body 1 exerts a force F⃗ on body 2, and by Newton's third law body 2 exerts −F⃗ on body 1, at every instant of contact.
2. Integrate both forces over the (same) contact time Δt: the impulse on body 2 is J⃗ and the impulse on body 1 is −J⃗.Equal contact time because the two bodies are in contact with each other, not with anything external.
3. Impulse = change in momentum, so Δp⃗₁ = −J⃗ and Δp⃗₂ = +J⃗ ⇒ Δp⃗₁ + Δp⃗₂ = 0.
4. Hence total momentum after = total momentum before, with no assumption anywhere about elastic or inelastic.This derivation never used the word "elastic" — that's the whole point.
5. Option (a) fails — momentum can increase for one body but never for the total on its own. Option (b) is the opposite of what's proven above. Option (d) is the common trap: students confuse this with KE conservation, which genuinely does depend on collision type.
6. Only option (c) survives: total momentum is always conserved, independent of collision type.

                                  at every instant of
 after:   m1→v1    m2→v2         contact ⇒ Δp1 = −Δp2
          ●────▶   ●────▶        ⇒ Δp_total = 0 always

Answer: (c) Is always conserved.
2Two bodies of masses 2 kg and 3 kg are moving with velocities 4 m/s and -2 m/s respectively. What is the total momentum before collision? (a) 2 kg·m/s (b) 8 kg·m/s (c) 10 kg·m/s (d) 1 kg·m/s — (Level-2)Total momentum, sign conventionLaw of Conservation of Momentum (How to get the total momentum before and after collisions)🟡 Similara2 kg·m/s
Concept Momentum is a vector, so before adding two momenta you must first fix a positive direction and assign every velocity its correct sign along that axis. The minus sign on −2 m/s is not decoration — it means that body moves opposite to the first body, so its momentum subtracts from the total rather than adding to it.
Formula p_total = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ — masses in kg, velocities in m/s, p in kg·m/s. kg × m/s = kg·m/s on both sides, so the units check out, and the algebraic sum (not the sum of magnitudes) is the physically meaningful total.
Solution
1. Take the direction of the first body's velocity (4 m/s) as positive. Then m₁ = 2 kg, v₁ = +4 m/s; m₂ = 3 kg, v₂ = −2 m/s (given negative, so opposite direction).
2. Momentum of body 1: p₁ = m₁v₁ = 2 × 4 = +8 kg·m/s.
3. Momentum of body 2: p₂ = m₂v₂ = 3 × (−2) = −6 kg·m/s.The negative sign is carried straight through from the given velocity — never drop it or treat it as "just subtract magnitudes at the end".
4. Add algebraically: p_total = p₁ + p₂ = 8 + (−6) = 2 kg·m/s.
5. Because the result is positive, the total momentum points in the same direction as body 1's original velocity — the system's net motion "tendency" is dominated by the faster, lighter body's momentum, even though body 2 is heavier.
6. This is the total momentum before collision only — the question does not ask what happens after, so no conservation equation is needed yet, just the vector sum.

  m1=2kg  v1=+4 m/s        m2=3kg  v2=-2 m/s
     ●───────▶                 ◀───●

  p1 = 2(4)  = +8 kg·m/s
  p2 = 3(-2) = -6 kg·m/s
  ---------------------------------
  p_total    = +8 - 6 = +2 kg·m/s
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question gives one velocity with an explicit minus sign, that is your cue that the two bodies move in opposite directions — always add momenta algebraically (with sign), never add the two given magnitudes (8 + 6 = 14 is a classic wrong-answer trap, not even in the option list here but common in similar problems). Fix +ve direction first, write every p with its sign, then add.
Answer: (a) 2 kg·m/s.
3A 2 kg object at rest is hit elastically by a 3 kg object moving at 6 m/s. What is the final velocity of the 2 kg object? (a) 5.2 m/s (b) 7.2 m/s (c) 6 m/s (d) 2 m/s — (Level-3)1D elastic collision, target formulaFinal Velocity of bodies after one dimensional elastic collision for JEE and NEET Physics🟡 Similarb7.2 m/s
Concept This is the standard "one moving body strikes a stationary body, elastically" set-up. Rather than re-deriving momentum + KE conservation from scratch, JEE problems expect you to plug straight into the two standard elastic-collision results for v₁' and v₂' — memorising them is what turns a 5-minute derivation into a 30-second substitution.
Formula v₁' = [(m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ + [2m₂/(m₁+m₂)]v₂, v₂' = [(m₂−m₁)/(m₁+m₂)]v₂ + [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ — all masses in kg, velocities in m/s. Both are dimensionally velocity (a pure mass-ratio times a velocity), and both reduce correctly to v₁'=v₂'=0 type checks in the special cases printed in the lecture. Here body 1 = the 3 kg striker (v₁=6, moving), body 2 = the 2 kg object at rest (v₂=0) — the question calls body 2 "the 2 kg object", so we need v₂'.
Solution
1. Assign: m₁ = 3 kg (striker), v₁ = 6 m/s; m₂ = 2 kg (target, at rest), v₂ = 0.
2. The target's final velocity uses the second formula: v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ + [(m₂−m₁)/(m₁+m₂)](0).The v₂ term vanishes automatically since v₂ = 0 — this is why "one body at rest" problems simplify so much.
3. Compute the coefficient: 2m₁/(m₁+m₂) = 2(3)/(3+2) = 6/5 = 1.2.
4. Substitute: v₂' = 1.2 × 6 = 7.2 m/s.
5. Cross-check with the striker: v₁' = [(3−2)/5](6) = (1/5)(6) = 1.2 m/s (it keeps moving forward, slower, since m₁ > m₂).
6. Momentum check: p_before = 3×6 = 18 kg·m/s; p_after = 3(1.2) + 2(7.2) = 3.6 + 14.4 = 18 kg·m/s ✓. KE check: ½(3)(36)=54 J before; ½(3)(1.44)+½(2)(51.84)=2.16+51.84=54 J after ✓ — both conserved, confirming the arithmetic.

  after:   m1=3kg,v1'=1.2       m2=2kg,v2'=7.2
              ●──▶                  ○────────▶

  v2' = 2m1/(m1+m2) · v1 = 2(3)/5 · 6 = 7.2 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the target-at-rest shortcut directly: v_target' = [2m_striker/(m_striker+m_target)] × v_striker — no need to write the full two-formula system when one body starts at rest, since every term with v₂ drops out. Trap: option (c) "6 m/s" tempts students who think the lighter body just takes the striker's full speed (true only if m₁≫m₂); option (a) 5.2 m/s comes from a sign slip.

4What percentage of kinetic energy of a moving particle is transferred to a stationary particle when it strikes the stationary particle of twice its mass elastically? — (Level-4)Fraction of KE transferred, elastic collisionFraction of Kinetic Energy Transferred in Elastic Collision🟡 Similar8/9 ≈ 88.9%
Concept This is a standalone, reusable result: in an elastic collision of a moving mass m₁ with a stationary mass m₂, the fraction of KE handed over depends only on the mass ratio, never on the speed. That's worth memorising outright, since it saves re-deriving v₁' and v₂' from scratch every time this question type appears.
Formula Fraction transferred, f = 4m₁m₂ / (m₁+m₂)² — dimensionless (mass²/mass² cancels). Symmetric in m₁, m₂, and capped at f=1 when m₁=m₂ (AM–GM: (m₁+m₂)² ≥ 4m₁m₂ always), which matches the lecture's own special case v₁'=0 for equal masses — 100% transfer.
Solution
1. Let the moving particle have mass m₁ = m, and the stationary target have m₂ = 2m ("twice its mass").
2. Substitute into f = 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)²: f = 4(m)(2m) / (m+2m)² = 8m² / (3m)².
3. Simplify: f = 8m² / 9m² = 8/9.The m² cancels top and bottom, confirming the promised speed-independence — this ratio is the same whether the particle moves at 1 m/s or 100 m/s.
4. Convert to percentage: 8/9 × 100 = 88.9% (recurring 88.888...%).
5. Sanity check via v₂': v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ = (2m/3m)v₁ = (2/3)v₁. KE transferred / KE initial = [½m₂v₂'²]/[½m₁v₁²] = [2m × (4/9)v₁²] / [m v₁²] = (8/9)v₁²/v₁² = 8/9 ✓.Re-deriving from v₂' independently confirms the shortcut formula rather than trusting it blindly.
  before:  m1=m, v1          m2=2m, at rest
              ●──────▶            ○

  f = 4 m1 m2 / (m1+m2)^2
    = 4(m)(2m) / (3m)^2
    = 8m^2 / 9m^2  = 8/9  = 88.9% transferred
                            (11.1% retained by striker)
⚡ JEE Trick Keep f = 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)² on instant recall — for "n times the mass" questions it collapses to f = 4n/(1+n)² (matches the general Suggested-Problems Level-5 result with n replaced by 1/n depending on which body is "n times"). Here n = 2 ⇒ f = 4(2)/9 = 8/9 directly, no need to re-plug the raw masses.
Answer: 8/9 ≈ 88.9% of the kinetic energy is transferred.
5Show that when a moving particle collides elastically with a stationary particle of mass 1/n times the mass of moving particle, then the moving particle transfers a fraction 4n/(1+n)² of its kinetic energy to the stationary particle. — (Level-5)Fraction of KE transferred — general proofFraction of Kinetic Energy Transferred in Elastic Collision🟡 SimilarProved: f = 4n/(1+n)²
Concept This "show that" question wants the general mass-ratio derivation, not a numeric plug-in — it is the parent result behind every specific "n times the mass" question in this chapter (including the Level-4 Q4 case above, which is just n = 1/2 of this one). Build it from the elastic-collision v₂' formula, then convert to a KE ratio.
Formula v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ (target starts at rest) and f = KE₂'/KE₁ = (m₂v₂'²)/(m₁v₁²). Both are standard elastic-collision results already established in this lecture's theory section — reusing them keeps the proof short instead of re-deriving from momentum + energy conservation each time.
Solution
1. Let the moving particle have mass m₁ = m and initial speed v₁ = u. The stationary target has mass m₂ = m/n ("1/n times the mass of the moving particle").
2. Target's velocity after collision: v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]u = [2m/(m + m/n)]u = [2m / (m(1+1/n))]u = [2/(1+1/n)]u = [2n/(n+1)]u.Multiplying numerator and denominator by n clears the fraction inside the fraction — a common algebra slip is stopping one step early and leaving 1/n terms unsimplified.
3. Initial KE of moving particle: KE₁ = ½mu².
4. Final KE of the target: KE₂' = ½m₂v₂'² = ½(m/n)[2n/(n+1)]²u² = ½(m/n) × 4n²/(n+1)² × u² = ½mu² × 4n/(n+1)².The n in the denominator of m₂ cancels one power of n from the n² in the numerator, leaving a clean single power of n.
5. Form the fraction: f = KE₂'/KE₁ = [½mu² × 4n/(n+1)²] / [½mu²] = 4n/(n+1)².
6. This matches the required result exactly, with (1+n)² and (n+1)² being the same thing written in either order — proved.
  before:  m1=m, v1=u          m2=m/n, at rest
              ●───────▶            ○

  v2' = 2m1/(m1+m2) u = 2n/(n+1) · u

  f = KE2'/KE1 = (m2 v2'^2)/(m1 u^2)
    = (m/n)(2n/(n+1))^2 u^2 / (m u^2)
    = 4n/(n+1)^2        <-- required result
⚡ JEE Trick Note f(n) = 4n/(n+1)² is symmetric under n → 1/n (check: f(1/n) = 4(1/n)/(1/n+1)² = same value after simplifying) — physically that's just relabelling which body you call "moving" vs "target" in the mass-ratio, and it peaks at f=1 when n=1 (equal masses). Use this general formula to shortcut every specific-ratio question instead of re-deriving v₁', v₂' each time.
Answer: Proved — the fraction of kinetic energy transferred is f = 4n/(1+n)².

1In an elastic collision of two particles the following is conserved: (a) Momentum of each particle (b) Speed of each particle (c) Kinetic energy of each particle (d) Total kinetic energy of both the particles — (Level-1)What is conserved — elastic collisionIn an elastic collision, what is conserved? (a) Kinetic energy (b) Momentum (c) Both (a) and (b)...🟡 SimilardTotal kinetic energy of both the particles
Concept The trap in this question is the word "each". Elastic collision guarantees two system-level (total) conservation laws — total momentum and total kinetic energy — it says nothing about any individual particle's momentum, speed, or KE staying fixed. In fact the whole point of a collision is that each particle's own velocity, momentum and KE typically change.
Formula m₁v₁+m₂v₂ = m₁v₁'+m₂v₂' (total momentum) and ½m₁v₁²+½m₂v₂² = ½m₁v₁'²+½m₂v₂'² (total KE) — both sums over the whole system, never term-by-term equalities for each particle individually.
Solution
1. Option (a) "momentum of each particle": false in general — a light particle striking a heavy one clearly changes its own momentum (it may even reverse direction), only the sum m₁v₁+m₂v₂ is protected.
2. Option (b) "speed of each particle": false — e.g. the target-at-rest formulas show v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁, which is generally not equal to v₁ or to 0, so speeds change for both bodies except in special mass ratios.
3. Option (c) "kinetic energy of each particle": false for the same reason — energy is exchanged between the two particles during contact; only their sum is fixed.
4. Option (d) "total kinetic energy of both the particles": this is the literal definition of an elastic collision from this lecture's own theory — "kinetic energy and linear momentum will remain conserved before and after the collision". True.
5. Cross-check with the m₁=m₂ special case derived earlier: v₁'=v₂ (a completely different value from v₁), and v₂'=v₁ — individual values swap entirely, yet ½m₁v₁'²+½m₂v₂'² still equals ½m₁v₁²+½m₂v₂² because the two masses are equal and the values just exchanged places.
   before:  m1,v1        m2,v2
              ●───▶         ●───▶

   after:   m1,v1'       m2,v2'   (each v CHANGES)
              ●──▶          ●────▶

   momentum:  m1v1+m2v2 = m1v1'+m2v2'   (TOTAL, fixed)
   KE:        ½m1v1²+½m2v2² = ½m1v1'²+½m2v2'²  (TOTAL, fixed)
   -- neither holds term-by-term for one particle alone --
⚡ JEE Trick Any option containing the phrase "of each particle" in a conservation-law MCQ is almost always a distractor — conservation laws in collisions are system-wide (total) statements, not per-body ones. Scan straight for the option that says "total".
Answer: (d) Total kinetic energy of both the particles.
2In an inelastic collision: (a) Only momentum is conserved (b) Only kinetic energy is conserved (c) Neither momentum nor kinetic energy is conserved (d) Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved — (Level-1)What is conserved — inelastic collisionMomentum (6 of 16) Inelastic Collisions, An Explanation🟡 SimilaraOnly momentum is conserved
Concept Momentum conservation follows purely from Newton's third law — it never cares whether shapes are permanently deformed. Kinetic-energy conservation is the special extra condition that defines "elastic"; an inelastic collision, by definition, is exactly the case where that extra condition fails and some KE converts irreversibly into heat, sound, or deformation (potential) energy.
Formula m₁v₁+m₂v₂ = m₁v₁'+m₂v₂' — holds in every collision. ΔKE = KE_initial − KE_final > 0 for inelastic — this lost energy equals the deformation potential energy created, as stated directly in this lecture's own definitions section.
Solution
1. Recall the lecture's own definitions: "Elastic collision — kinetic energy and linear momentum remain conserved." "Inelastic collision — the colliding bodies do not return to their original shape; some mechanical energy goes into deformation potential energy. Linear momentum remains conserved in case of inelastic collision."
2. This directly rules out options (b) "only KE conserved" and (c) "neither conserved" — momentum survives regardless of elasticity.
3. It also rules out (d) "both conserved" — that description applies only to the elastic case, not the inelastic one asked about here.
4. Physical reasoning for why momentum still holds: during contact, body 1 pushes body 2 with force F(t) and by Newton's third law body 2 pushes body 1 with −F(t) at every instant — this pairing is completely independent of whether the bodies dent, deform, or stick.Newton's third law is a statement about forces between two bodies, not about their material properties, so it survives any kind of collision.
5. Why KE fails: some of the ordered kinetic energy of the two bodies converts into disordered forms — heat from internal friction during deformation, sound, and permanent deformation (dent) potential energy — none of which is "kinetic energy of the two bodies" any more.
  Elastic:    p conserved  ✓     KE conserved  ✓
  Inelastic:  p conserved  ✓     KE conserved  ✗ (lost to heat/deformation)

  Newton's 3rd law (F on 2 = -F on 1) guarantees p always;
  only the SPECIAL elastic assumption guarantees KE too.
⚡ JEE Trick Momentum conservation is the one law you can invoke in literally every collision problem — elastic, inelastic, perfectly inelastic, explosion — without checking any condition first. KE conservation is the one extra assumption you must verify ("elastic" stated explicitly) before using it.
Answer: (a) Only momentum is conserved.

Concept A wall is effectively an infinitely massive, immovable collision partner, so this is the m₁≪m₂ special case from the theory section: the light body simply reverses its velocity while keeping the same speed. Change of momentum is a vector subtraction — final minus initial — and reversing direction means you are subtracting a negative, which is the same as adding.
Formula Δp⃗ = p⃗_final − p⃗_initial = mv⃗_final − mv⃗_initial — m in kg, v in m/s, Δp in kg·m/s. This is just the definition of change; no collision-specific formula is even needed once you know the before/after velocities.
Solution
1. Take the direction of approach (towards the wall) as positive. Before impact: v_initial = +v, so p_initial = +mv.
2. "Rebounds with the same speed" means the body reverses direction but |v| is unchanged: v_final = −v, so p_final = −mv."Same speed" (a scalar) after a rebound always means the velocity vector flips sign — this is the standard reading in every JEE wall-collision question.
3. Change in momentum: Δp = p_final − p_initial = (−mv) − (mv) = −2mv.
4. The question asks for the change of momentum generically (its "change"), and the option list gives magnitudes without a stated sign convention — so report the magnitude: |Δp| = 2mv.
5. Reject (b) mv and (d) zero: those would apply only if the body stuck to the wall (mv) or passed straight through unaffected (zero) — neither matches "rebounds". Reject (c) −mv: that under-counts by a factor of 2, forgetting that the velocity fully reverses, not just stops.

            ●─────────────────█

   after:   m ◀── v           wall
            ●─────────────────█

   p_i = +mv
   p_f = -mv

4In elastic collision between two bodies, complete energy is transferred when: (a) both bodies have equal mass (b) both bodies are moving (c) heavy body is moving and lighter one is at rest (d) lighter body is moving and heavier one is at rest — (Level-1)Complete energy transfer conditionPerfectly Elastic Collision, Conservation of Momentum and Kinetic Energy🟡 SimilaraBoth bodies have equal mass
Concept "Complete energy transfer" means the fraction f = 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)² must hit its maximum possible value, 1 (i.e. 100%). This is exactly the equal-mass special case already worked out in the lecture's theory: v₁'=v₂, v₂'=v₁ — the moving body stops dead and the stationary one leaves with all of its speed (and hence all of its KE).
Formula f = 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)², dimensionless. By AM–GM, (m₁+m₂)² ≥ 4m₁m₂ always, with equality exactly when m₁=m₂ — so f ≤ 1 always, and f = 1 (complete transfer) is achieved only at equal mass, never exceeded.
Solution
1. Maximise f(m₁,m₂) = 4m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)² over the mass ratio. Write r = m₂/m₁, so f(r) = 4r/(1+r)².
2. Differentiate and set to zero: df/dr = [4(1+r)² − 4r·2(1+r)] / (1+r)⁴ = [4(1+r) − 8r]/(1+r)³ = [4 − 4r]/(1+r)³ = 0 ⇒ r = 1.r=1 means m₂=m₁ — equal masses is a genuine maximum, not a coincidence, confirmed by calculus rather than just quoting the special-case result.
3. At r = 1: f = 4(1)/(2)² = 4/4 = 1 — 100% of the KE is transferred.
4. Reject (c) and (d): both describe an unequal-mass scenario, where f < 1 always (some KE necessarily stays with the striker), regardless of which body happens to be moving.
5. Reject (b): "both bodies moving" is not even a condition compatible with this chapter's target-at-rest formulas, and moving-vs-moving doesn't by itself guarantee f = 1 either.
6. Physical picture: equal-mass elastic collision along a line is literally a velocity swap — all the kinetic energy the mover had walks straight across to the target, none is left behind.

5What happens to kinetic energy in a perfectly inelastic collision? (a) Remains the same (b) Increases (c) Decreases (d) Becomes zero — (Level-1)KE loss — perfectly inelastic collision3 Loss In Kinetic Energy For Perfectly Inelastic Collision🟡 SimilarcDecreases
Concept "Perfectly inelastic" means the two bodies stick together and move with one common final velocity — the maximum possible deformation, hence the maximum possible KE loss for a given pair of masses/speeds. But "maximum loss" is not the same as "total loss": unless the total momentum is zero to begin with, the stuck-together system still carries some KE away, so the energy strictly decreases, it does not vanish.
Formula Common velocity from momentum conservation: v = (m₁v₁+m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂). ΔKE = ½m₁v₁²+½m₂v₂² − ½(m₁+m₂)v², which algebraically simplifies to ΔKE = [m₁m₂/2(m₁+m₂)](v₁−v₂)². This reduced form is always ≥ 0 (a square times positive masses), proving energy loss can never be negative — KE can only decrease or, in the trivial no-relative-motion case, stay the same.
Solution
1. Write the KE loss in the simplified reduced-mass form: ΔKE = [m₁m₂/2(m₁+m₂)] × (v₁−v₂)², where (v₁−v₂) is the relative velocity of approach.
2. Every factor on the right — m₁, m₂, (m₁+m₂), and the square (v₁−v₂)² — is non-negative. So ΔKE ≥ 0 always: KE after ≤ KE before.
3. ΔKE = 0 only in the degenerate case v₁ = v₂ (no relative approach speed, so there is technically no "collision" happening at all) — not a real collision scenario.

2. Apply the equal-mass exchange rule: v_A' = u_B = −12 m/s (i.e. 12 m/s WEST). v_B' = u_A = +12 m/s (i.e. 12 m/s EAST).
3. Physically: it's exactly as if each ball bounced off a mirror-image wall — because the two balls are identical in mass and their combined momentum is zero (12−12=0), the collision is equivalent to each ball independently reversing its own velocity.
4. Verify momentum: initial p = 2(12)+2(−12) = 0. Final p = 2(−12)+2(12) = 0 ✓ (trivially conserved, since it's zero throughout).
5. Verify KE: initial KE = ½(2)(12²)+½(2)(12²) = 144+144 = 288 J. Final KE = ½(2)(−12)²+½(2)(12²) = 144+144 = 288 J ✓ — confirms elastic, no energy lost.
6. Note this is the SAME numeric outcome as if both balls simply bounced straight back off each other unchanged in speed — a useful pattern to recognise: symmetric equal-and-opposite equal-mass collisions always just reverse both velocities.

              ◀════●          ●════▶

  after:   A(2kg,12W)          (2kg,12E)B
              ●════▶          ◀════●

⚡ JEE Trick Whenever two EQUAL masses collide elastically — regardless of whether either starts at rest — the answer is always a straight velocity SWAP: v_A'=u_B and v_B'=u_A. Memorise this as the single most useful shortcut in the whole equal-mass collision family, since it never requires solving simultaneous equations.
Answer: Ball A moves at 12 m/s westward; ball B moves at 12 m/s eastward (they simply swap velocities).

Concept This row directly contrasts the two collision types on the IDENTICAL setup (equal masses m, sphere strikes pendulum bob at v₀): part (i) is elastic (equal masses ⇒ full velocity hand-off, sphere stops, bob moves off with v₀), part (ii) is perfectly inelastic (already solved as an MCQ earlier in Level-4 of this same file, with a different numeric label but the same physics).

Solution
1. (i) Elastic, equal masses (m striking m at rest): full velocity hand-off — sphere stops completely, pendulum bob moves off with the sphere's full original speed v₀.
2. (i) Energy conservation for the swing: ½mv₀² = mgh ⇒ h = v₀²/2g.
3. (ii) Perfectly inelastic (stick together): momentum conservation gives mv₀ = (2m)v' ⇒ v' = v₀/2.
4. (ii) Energy conservation for the swing (using the COMBINED mass 2m, which cancels): ½(2m)v'² = (2m)gh' ⇒ h' = v'²/2g = (v₀/2)²/2g = v₀²/8g.
5. 6. This exact inelastic result (h=v₀²/8g) already appeared earlier in this file as a standalone MCQ (option a there) — recognising the shared physics between these two rows saves re-deriving the momentum step from scratch.

Solution
1. Data: m₁=10 kg, u₁=6 m/s; m₂=1 kg, u₂=3 m/s; m₁+m₂=11 kg.
2. v₁' = [(10−1)(6) + 2(1)(3)]/11 = [9(6)+6]/11 = [54+6]/11 = 60/11 ≈ 5.45 m/s.
3. v₂' = [(1−10)(3) + 2(10)(6)]/11 = [−9(3)+120]/11 = [−27+120]/11 = 93/11 ≈ 8.45 m/s.

6. Concept Three-stage problem: (1) energy conservation for the swing down from horizontal to the lowest point, (2) elastic collision (unequal masses, m vs 5m) at that point, (3) two separate follow-up questions — the STRING TENSION right after impact (needs circular-motion / centripetal analysis, not just linear collision formulas) and the bob's own rebound height (energy conservation again, using its post-collision speed).
Formula Swing down: v₀=√(2gl). Elastic collision (bob mass m vs block 5m): v_bob'=[(m−5m)/(6m)]v₀, v_block'=[2m/(6m)]v₀. Centripetal tension at the lowest point: T−mg=mv_bob'²/l. Tension needs the SPEED-SQUARED (magnitude only) in the centripetal formula, regardless of which direction the bob is moving right after the bounce.
Solution
1. Swing from horizontal to lowest point (height drop = l): ½mv₀² = mgl ⇒ v₀ = √(2gl).
2. Elastic collision, bob (m) strikes block (5m) at rest: v_block' = [2m/(m+5m)]v₀ = (1/3)v₀ = (1/3)√(2gl) = √(2gl)/3 — confirms option (c).
3. Bob's own velocity after collision: v_bob' = [(m−5m)/6m]v₀ = (−4/6)v₀ = −(2/3)v₀ (the bob bounces BACK, since it's lighter than the block it struck).
4. Tension just after impact (centripetal, at the lowest point, using |v_bob'|): T − mg = m·v_bob'²/l. Compute v_bob'² = (4/9)v₀² = (4/9)(2gl) = 8gl/9.
5. T = mg + m(8gl/9)/l = mg + 8mg/9 = 9mg/9 + 8mg/9 = 17mg/9 — confirms option (a) and rules out (b) 3mg.
6. Maximum height the bob rebounds to: using energy conservation on the bob alone (½mv_bob'² = mgh'): h' = v_bob'²/2g = (8gl/9)/2g = 4l/9 — confirms option (d).
