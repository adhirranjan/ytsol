1A cricket bat is cut at the location of its centre of mass as shown. Then (a) The two pieces will have the same mass (b) The bottom piece will have larger mass (c) The handle piece will have larger mass (d) Mass of handle piece is double the mass of bottom piece (Level-1)CM position vs mass distributionA cricket bat is cut at the location of its center of mass as shown. Then 1 The two pieces will have🟢 ExactbThe bottom piece will have larger mass
Concept Cutting a body at its centre of mass does not cut it into two equal masses — that is the single most common misread of this question. The centre of mass is the balance point of moments, not the midpoint of mass along the length. Because the CM sits where the two sides' mass×distance products match, a piece that lies far from the CM needs less mass to balance a piece that hugs the CM. A cricket bat is a long thin handle joined to a short fat blade, so its CM lies deep inside the blade end, leaving a long light handle piece and a short heavy bottom piece.
Formula m1r1 = m2r2, i.e. m1/m2 = r2/r1. Treat each cut piece as a point mass at its own CM; the whole bat's CM must be the two-particle CM of those two pieces, which is exactly the condition Σmiri = 0 about the cut.
Solution
1. Replace the two pieces by point masses: handle piece mh at distance rh from the cut, bottom piece mb at distance rb from the cut, on opposite sides.Legitimate because the CM of a composite equals the CM of its parts' CMs — the internal shape of each piece no longer matters.
2. The cut is at the bat's CM, so moments about it cancel: mhrh − mbrb = 0 ⇒ mh/mb = rb/rh.
3. Geometry of a bat: the handle is a long slender rod, so its own CM sits far from the cut; the blade is short and stubby, so its CM sits close ⇒ rh > rb.This is the only physical input; everything else is algebra. Students who skip it end up guessing between (b) and (c).
4. Substituting the inequality: mh/mb = rb/rh < 1 ⇒ mh < mb. The bottom (blade) piece is the heavier one.
5. Cross-check with the standard rule r ∝ 1/m: the CM always lies nearer the heavier body. Here the CM is visibly nearer the blade end, so the blade must be the heavier side — same conclusion, one line, no algebra.Use this as the exam-hall shortcut; the moment equation is only the proof behind it.
6. Option (d) claims a fixed 2:1 ratio. Nothing in the problem fixes rb/rh numerically, so no exact ratio can be asserted — and it points the wrong way besides.
   handle piece (long, light)   |   bottom piece (short, heavy)
   ===========================  X  #####
        CM_h  <---- r_h ---->   |  <-r_b->  CM_b
   X = cut = CM of whole bat
   balance: m_h * r_h  =  m_b * r_b
   r_h > r_b   ==>   m_h < m_b
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the phrase "cut at the centre of mass", which whispers "equal halves" — that would be true of the centre of length for a uniform rod only. (a) is that trap. (c) reverses the r ∝ 1/m rule, putting the CM near the light end. (d) invents a numeric ratio the data cannot support. Whenever a body is cut at its CM, ask which piece's own CM is farther from the cut — that piece is the lighter one.
Answer: (b) The bottom piece will have larger mass.
2Particle of mass 2 kg and 3 kg are separated by 3 m. Locate the centre of mass w.r.t. the 2 kg particle. (Level-2)Two-particle CMCalculating the Center of Mass of a System of Particles🟡 Similar1.8 m from the 2 kg particle, towards the 3 kg particle
Concept For two particles the centre of mass always lies on the line joining them and inside the segment, never outside — so the answer must be between 0 and 3 m. Its distance from each particle is inversely proportional to that particle's own mass, which means the CM leans towards the heavier 3 kg body. The naive answer "halfway, 1.5 m" is wrong precisely because the masses are unequal; equal split happens only when m1 = m2. The question asks "w.r.t. the 2 kg particle", so the origin is fixed at the lighter mass and the answer must exceed 1.5 m.
Formula r1 = m2d / (m1 + m2), equivalently xcm = (m1x1 + m2x2)/(m1 + m2). The first form is the two-particle shortcut derived from m1r1 = m2r2 with r1 + r2 = d; it skips the coordinate set-up entirely.
Solution
1. Put the 2 kg particle at x1 = 0 and the 3 kg particle at x2 = 3 m, so distances measured from the origin are automatically "w.r.t. the 2 kg particle".Choosing the origin at the body the question asks about removes the last subtraction step — a free simplification.
2. Direct formula: r1 = m2d/(m1 + m2) = (3 × 3)/(2 + 3) = 9/5 = 1.8 m.
3. Cross-check by the weighted average: xcm = (2×0 + 3×3)/(2+3) = 9/5 = 1.8 m — identical, as it must be.Two independent routes to the same number is the cheapest error-check available in a numerical CM problem.
4. Distance from the 3 kg particle: r2 = 3 − 1.8 = 1.2 m, and the ratio check r1:r2 = 1.8:1.2 = 3:2 = m2:m1.The ratio must come out inverted relative to the masses; if you get 2:3 you have swapped m1 and m2 in the numerator.
5. Sanity: 1.8 m > 1.5 m, i.e. the CM sits on the 3 kg side of the midpoint — consistent with "CM leans towards the heavier mass".
6. Statement of the answer: the centre of mass lies 1.8 m from the 2 kg particle, measured along the line joining the two, in the direction of the 3 kg particle.
   2 kg                 CM              3 kg
    O-------------------X---------------O
   x=0                x=1.8            x=3
    |<---- r1 = 1.8 --->|<-- r2=1.2 --->|
   check:  m1*r1 = 2*1.8 = 3.6
           m2*r2 = 3*1.2 = 3.6   (balanced)
⚡ JEE Trick The classic slip is writing r1 = m1d/(m1+m2) = 1.2 m — the reciprocal error — which quietly puts the CM nearer the light mass. Remember the numerator carries the other mass. Also watch the phrase "w.r.t. the 2 kg particle": answering 1.2 m gives the distance from the 3 kg particle instead. A one-second check (is the answer past the midpoint on the heavy side?) catches both mistakes.

Concept "Centre of mass at the origin" is not a statement about distances — it is the statement that the total first moment of mass about the origin vanishes, Σmiri = 0. That single vector equation is far cheaper than writing the full xcm, ycm, zcm fractions, because the denominator (total mass) never enters: any non-zero total mass divides out. The two given particles supply a fixed moment; the third particle must supply exactly the negative of it, so its position is forced to lie in the third quadrant of the x–y plane, diagonally opposite the given pair.
Formula Σmiri = 0 ⇒ r3 = −(m1r1 + m2r2)/m3. Setting rcm = 0 in rcm = Σmiri/Σmi kills the denominator, so the unknown mass appears only once — in m3r3.
Solution
1. Moment of the fixed pair: m1r1 + m2r2 = 2(3, 0, 0) + 3(0, 4, 0) = (6, 0, 0) + (0, 12, 0) = (6, 12, 0) kg·m.Multiply mass into each coordinate before adding; adding the position vectors first and then weighting is the standard wrong route.
2. Balance condition: m3r3 = −(6, 12, 0) ⇒ r3 = −(6, 12, 0)/m3. The direction of r3 is fixed (opposite to (6, 12, 0)); only its length depends on m3.
3. Case (i), m3 = 5 kg: r3 = −(6, 12, 0)/5 = (−6/5, −12/5, 0) = (−1.2, −2.4, 0) m.
4. Case (ii), m3 = 10 kg: r3 = −(6, 12, 0)/10 = (−0.6, −1.2, 0) m — exactly half the previous distance, since doubling the mass halves the lever arm needed.This inverse-proportionality is the whole point of part (ii): the answer need not be recomputed from scratch, just scaled by 5/10.
5. Verify (i) by direct substitution: xcm = (2×3 + 3×0 + 5×(−1.2))/(2+3+5) = (6 + 0 − 6)/10 = 0; ycm = (0 + 12 + 5×(−2.4))/10 = (12 − 12)/10 = 0. Origin confirmed.
6. Both answers have z = 0: neither given particle has a z-component, so any non-zero z3 would push the CM off the plane. The third particle must stay in the x–y plane.Marks are routinely lost by quoting only (x, y) and forgetting to justify z = 0 in a 3-D problem.

⚡ JEE Trick Do not compute a "centre of mass of the first two" and then try to balance about it — that adds a step and a chance to slip. Go straight to Σmr = 0. The trap in part (ii) is recomputing everything; the moment (6, 12, 0) is unchanged, so only the division changes. 4Particles of mass 1 kg, 1 kg & 2 kg are placed at (a, 0, 0), (−a, 0, 0) and (0, 2, 0) m. Where should a 4 kg particle be placed so that the centre of mass lies at (0, 0, 2) m? Concept Here the target centre of mass is not the origin, so the shortcut Σmr = 0 does not apply directly; the correct statement is Σmiri = Mtotalrcm. Note that the total mass now includes the 4 kg particle being placed, so Mtotal is known before the position is. The symbolic mass "a" in the first two coordinates is a deliberate distraction: the two 1 kg particles sit symmetrically at ±a, so their x-moments cancel exactly and "a" can never appear in the answer.
Formula Σmiri = (Σmi) rcm, so r4 = [Mtotalrcm − Σknownmiri] / m4. Clearing the denominator turns the CM definition into a linear moment balance, which is the only form that solves cleanly for an unknown position.
Solution
1. Total mass: M = 1 + 1 + 2 + 4 = 8 kg, so the required total moment is Mrcm = 8(0, 0, 2) = (0, 0, 16) kg·m.The 4 kg must be counted in M even though its position is unknown — leaving it out is the single most frequent error in this question.
2. Moment of the three known particles: 1(a, 0, 0) + 1(−a, 0, 0) + 2(0, 2, 0) = (a − a, 4, 0) = (0, 4, 0) kg·m — the ±a pair cancels and "a" disappears.
3. Moment the 4 kg must supply: 4r4 = (0, 0, 16) − (0, 4, 0) = (0, −4, 16) kg·m.Subtract in the right order: required total minus what is already there. Reversing it flips every sign of the answer.
4. Divide by the mass: r4 = (0, −4, 16)/4 = (0, −1, 4) m.
5. Check y: ycm = (1×0 + 1×0 + 2×2 + 4×(−1))/8 = (4 − 4)/8 = 0 ✓. Check z: zcm = (0 + 0 + 0 + 4×4)/8 = 16/8 = 2 ✓. Check x: (a − a + 0 + 0)/8 = 0 ✓.
6. Physical reading: the 4 kg must sit below the x-axis (y = −1) to pull the CM back down past the 2 kg particle at y = +2, and well above the target plane (z = 4) to lift the CM from z = 0 up to z = 2 — both displacements halved relative to their pulls because 4 kg is half the 8 kg total.This "half the total mass moves twice the required displacement" reading is a good final sanity filter on any placement problem.

   4 kg must supply                           ( 0, -4, 16)
   divide by 4                    r4 =        ( 0, -1,  4)

⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit in this one question. First, the symbol "a": candidates waste time carrying it, when the symmetric ±a pair guarantees it cancels — spot the symmetry before computing. Second, the total mass: writing M = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 kg (forgetting the particle being placed) gives the wrong moment (0, 0, 8) and the wrong answer (0, −1, 2). Whenever a mass is being added to reach a stated CM, it always belongs in the denominator too.
Answer: (0, −1, 4) m.
5Three identical spheres each of radius 'R' are placed touching each other so that their centres A, B and C lie on a straight line. Find the position of their centre of mass from A. (Level-5)CM of touching spheresthree identical spheres each of mass m and radius R placed touching each other so that their centres🟢 Exact2R from A (i.e. at the centre B of the middle sphere)
Concept A uniform solid sphere may be replaced, for centre-of-mass purposes, by a single point mass sitting at its geometric centre — that is the whole reason the problem gives the centres A, B, C rather than the surfaces. So a three-sphere assembly collapses to three equal point masses on a line, and the CM of equal masses is simply their arithmetic mean position. The only geometry needed is the spacing: two spheres of radius R that touch have their centres separated by R + R = 2R, not by R and not by 4R.
Formula xcm = Σmixi / Σmi, which for identical masses reduces to xcm = (x1 + x2 + x3)/3. Equal masses factor out of numerator and denominator, so the mass value m is never needed — which is why the question does not bother to state it.
Solution
1. Set the origin at A and the x-axis along the line of centres, so xA = 0 by construction and the final answer is read off directly as xcm.Placing the origin at the point the question measures from removes the last subtraction and one chance to slip.
2. Touching condition gives centre-to-centre spacing 2R, so xB = 2R and xC = 2R + 2R = 4R.Adjacent spheres share one contact point; the distance across it is one radius from each sphere. Using R instead of 2R is the classic geometry error here and yields R instead of 2R.
3. Apply the formula: xcm = [m(0) + m(2R) + m(4R)]/(m + m + m) = 6mR/3m = 2R.
4. Symmetry cross-check: three equal masses evenly spaced on a line must have their CM at the middle one, i.e. exactly at B, whose coordinate is 2R. Same answer with no arithmetic.Any time equal masses sit at equal intervals, the CM is the midpoint of the array — worth one second before starting a calculation.
5. Note that 2R = 4R/2, the midpoint of the segment AC — consistent, since a symmetric arrangement about B forces the CM onto B.
6. Convert to a physical statement: the centre of mass lies at a distance 2R from A along AC, which is inside the middle sphere, at its very centre B.
        A            B            C
       (_)          (_)          (_)
        0           2R           4R      <- x (from A)
        |<--- 2R -->|<--- 2R --->|
   x_cm = (0 + 2R + 4R)/3 = 6R/3 = 2R
   equal masses, equal spacing -> CM at middle sphere B
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the word "touching": it fixes the spacing at 2R (one radius from each sphere), yet a hurried reading uses R and produces R, or measures A-to-C as 2R and produces R again. Also resist "radius R" implying the CM must be at R — the sphere's radius sets the spacing, not the answer. Whenever identical bodies are equally spaced, jump straight to the symmetry answer and use the summation only as a check.
Answer: 2R from A — that is, at the centre B of the middle sphere.

Concept Earth and Moon form a two-particle system whose common centre of mass — the barycentre — is the point both bodies actually orbit. Because each body is spherically symmetric, each may be treated as a point mass at its own centre, so the problem reduces to two points 3.84 × 108 m apart. The individual masses are never needed: only their ratio enters, which is why the data is given as "0.0123 times". The answer must come out much smaller than the Earth's radius scale would suggest — in fact 4.67 × 106 m is inside the Earth (radius 6.4 × 106 m), which is why the Earth wobbles rather than orbits a distant point.
Formula rE = mmd / (mE + mm), the distance from the first body carrying the other body's mass in the numerator. Follows from mErE = mmrm together with rE + rm = d; it is the two-particle form of xcm = Σmixi/Σmi.
Solution
1. Write masses in units of the Earth's mass: mE = M, mm = 0.0123M, d = 3.84 × 108 m. Put the Earth's centre at x = 0 and the Moon's at x = d.Working in units of M lets the unknown M cancel; carrying 6 × 1024 kg through the arithmetic is extra work for the same number.
2. Substitute into the formula: rE = (0.0123M)(3.84 × 108)/(M + 0.0123M) = (0.0123 × 3.84 × 108)/1.0123 m.
3. Evaluate the numerator, then divide: 0.0123 × 3.84 × 108 = 4.7232 × 106; dividing, 4.7232 × 106/1.0123 = 4.666 × 106 ≈ 4.67 × 106 m.Do not drop the 1.0123: skipping it gives 4.72 × 106 m, a 1.2 % error that changes the second significant figure the answer is quoted to.
4. Cross-check with the weighted average: xcm = [M(0) + 0.0123M(3.84 × 108)]/(1.0123M) = 4.67 × 106 m — identical, as expected.
5. Distance from the Moon: rm = d − rE = 3.84 × 108 − 0.0467 × 108 = 3.793 × 108 m, and rE/rm = 0.0123 = mm/mE ✓.The ratio of distances must reproduce the inverse mass ratio exactly; it is the fastest full check on this class of problem.
6. Interpretation: 4.67 × 106 m < Rearth = 6.4 × 106 m, so the barycentre lies about 1700 km beneath the Earth's surface — the Earth does not orbit an external point, it wobbles about an internal one.
   Earth (M)                                    Moon (0.0123M)
     (O)------X-------------------------------------(o)
      0    4.67e6                                  3.84e8   [m]
      |<-rE->|<---------- rm = 3.793e8 ------------->|
   rE = 0.0123*3.84e8 / 1.0123 = 4.67e6 m

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the denominator. Because 0.0123 is tiny, many students silently approximate mE + mm ≈ mE and report 4.72 × 106 m — wrong at the printed precision. The second trap is putting the Earth's mass in the numerator, which would place the barycentre almost at the Moon. Remember: the numerator always carries the far body's mass. Finally, compare the answer with the Earth's radius; a barycentre inside the Earth is the expected, and physically famous, result.

2Locate the centre of mass of three particles of mass m1 = 1 kg, m2 = 2 kg and m3 = 3 kg at the corners of an equilateral triangle of each side of 1 m, as shown (1 kg at the bottom-left corner, 2 kg at the bottom-right corner, 3 kg at the apex).CM of an equilateral triangleThree particles of masses `1kg`, `2kg` and `3kg` are placed at the corners A, B and C respective...🟢 Exact(7/12 m, √3/4 m) ≈ (0.583 m, 0.433 m) from the 1 kg corner
Concept The centre of mass of a triangle of unequal point masses is not the centroid; the centroid (side/2, side/(2√3)) is only the special case of three equal masses. Here the 3 kg at the apex is half the total mass, so the CM is dragged strongly upward, and the 2 kg outweighs the 1 kg, so it also slides right of the mid-line. Because the answer is a point in a plane, the whole job is choosing convenient coordinates first — a bad axis choice turns clean fractions into surds in both coordinates.
Formula xcm = Σmixi/Σmi, ycm = Σmiyi/Σmi. The CM equation is a vector equation, so it splits into one independent scalar equation per axis; x and y never mix.
Solution
1. Choose axes along the base: 1 kg at (0, 0), 2 kg at (1, 0). Then the apex of an equilateral triangle of side 1 m is at (1/2, √3/2), since its height is (√3/2) × side.Origin at a mass and the x-axis along a side puts two of the three particles at y = 0, which halves the y-arithmetic.
2. Total mass: Σm = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 kg — the common denominator for both coordinates.
3. x-coordinate: xcm = [1(0) + 2(1) + 3(1/2)]/6 = (0 + 2 + 1.5)/6 = 3.5/6 = 7/12 m ≈ 0.583 m.
4. y-coordinate: ycm = [1(0) + 2(0) + 3(√3/2)]/6 = (3√3/2)/6 = 3√3/12 = √3/4 m ≈ 0.433 m.Only the apex has a non-zero y, so ycm is just its mass fraction (3/6 = 1/2) times the height √3/2 — exactly √3/4, worth spotting as a one-line route.
5. Sanity vs. the centroid: for equal masses the CM would be (1/2, √3/6) ≈ (0.500, 0.289). Our point (0.583, 0.433) is right of it and higher — correct, because the heavier 2 kg pulls right and the heaviest 3 kg pulls up.Comparing against the centroid catches almost every arithmetic slip without redoing the sums.
6. The CM must also lie inside the triangle: 0 < 0.583 < 1 and 0 < 0.433 < 0.866 ✓. For point masses at the vertices of a convex figure, the CM can never fall outside it.

Concept A body balances on a knife edge exactly when the edge is under its centre of mass, because gravity is uniform here, so the resultant weight acts through the CM and produces zero torque about that point. So "find the balance point" and "find xcm" are the same computation — no torque equation is needed. The one piece of physics that must not be forgotten is that the rod itself is not massless: its 10 kg acts at its own centre, the 50 cm mark, and it must appear as a fifth particle in the sum.
Formula xcm = Σmixi / Σmi, with the uniform rod entered as a point mass Mrod at L/2. A uniform rod's own mass distribution is symmetric about its midpoint, so replacing it by a point mass at 50 cm changes no moment about any axis.
Solution
1. Concept The centre of mass is defined purely as a mass-weighted average of positions, Σmiri/Σmi. An average of points inside a body need not itself be a point of the body — it only has to lie inside the convex hull of the mass. For a solid convex object (sphere, cube, brick) the hull is the object, so the CM sits inside; but hollow it out or bend it, and the hull contains empty space where the CM can fall. Nothing in the definition ever requires material at the CM.
Formula rcm = Σmiri / Σmi (for a continuous body, ∫r dm / ∫dm). The definition constrains rcm to the convex hull of the mass distribution and nothing tighter — that single fact decides this question.
Solution
1. Test (a): a uniform sphere has its CM at the geometric centre, but a rod whose density rises from one end to the other has its CM shifted towards the dense end, away from the geometric centre. So "always at the geometrical centre" fails.Geometric centre and centre of mass coincide only for uniform density plus symmetry — two conditions, not one.
2. Test (b): a uniform ring of radius R has its CM at the centre of the circle by symmetry — a point occupied by air, not by ring material. So "always inside" fails.
3. More examples of outside-the-body CM: a hollow spherical shell (CM at the empty centre), a horseshoe or a bent wire (CM in the gap), a boomerang.The book itself states this in the definition section: the CM "does not necessarily have to be located within the physical boundaries of the object".
4. Test (c): a solid cube or sphere plainly has its CM inside the material, so "always outside" fails immediately — it is the mirror-image error of (b).
5. Since one class of bodies puts the CM inside and another puts it outside, the only statement true for all bodies is (d): it may lie within or outside.In "always" questions a single counter-example kills the option; only the permissive option can survive.
6. Worth adding: the CM may also lie exactly on the surface — a solid hemisphere's CM lies on the axis at 3R/8 from the flat face, and a half-ring's CM at 2R/π also lies in empty space. Option (d) covers every case.

2The centre of mass of a system of particles does not depend on: (a) masses of the particles (b) forces acting on the particles (c) position of the particles (d) relative distances between the particlesWhat fixes the CMThe center of mass of a system of particles does not depend on ...........🟢 Exactbforces acting on the particles
Concept Read the defining equation and see what symbols actually appear in it: rcm = Σmiri/Σmi contains only masses and positions. No force, no acceleration, no time. The position of the centre of mass at a given instant is therefore a pure geometry-plus-mass quantity, fixed the moment you know where each particle is and how heavy it is. Forces matter for how that point moves later (Macm = Fext), but that is a statement about its acceleration, not about where it is now.
Formula rcm = Σmiri / Σmi versus Macm = Fext. The first fixes the location, the second fixes its motion; the question asks about location, so only the first is relevant.
Solution
1. Option (a), masses: change m2 from 2 kg to 4 kg in a two-particle system 3 m apart and the CM moves from 2.0 m to 2.4 m from m1 = 1 kg. The CM depends on masses — not the answer.A single worked counter-example is faster and safer than arguing from words.
2. Options (c) and (d), positions and relative distances: the position vectors ri sit explicitly in the numerator, and separations are just differences of those. Move a particle and the CM moves — neither is the answer.
3. Option (b), forces: no force symbol occurs anywhere in the definition. Two identical particle arrangements, one in deep space and one in a strong field, have their CM at the same point at that instant.Internal forces in particular can never shift the CM at all — they cancel in pairs by Newton's third law, which is why an exploding shell's CM continues on its original parabola.
4. Guard against the classic over-reading: an external force does change the CM's position over time, via Macm = Fext. But that is the CM responding to the resulting motion of the particles; at any given instant the CM is still computed from masses and positions alone.
5. So exactly one option names a quantity absent from the defining formula, and (b) is it."Does not depend on" questions are decided by inspecting which symbols appear in the definition — a five-second test.
6. Cross-link: this is exactly why a diver's CM traces a clean parabola no matter how violently the limbs push on each other — internal forces reshape the body but never displace the CM.

Answer: (b) forces acting on the particles.

Concept Separate two different things the word "centre of mass" is used for: the physical point of the body, and the numbers we write down for it. Shift or rotate the axes and the numbers change — but they change in exactly the way every other point's numbers change, so the point itself, identified by where it sits inside the body, is untouched. A cricket bat's balance point is a scratch on the wood; no re-labelling of axes can move that scratch. That is why the CM can be found experimentally (by balancing) with no coordinate system at all.
Formula rcm = Σmiri/Σmi; shifting the origin by a sends ri → ri + a. The definition is a weighted average, and averages transform exactly like the individual points they average — that is the whole proof.
Solution
1. Translate every position by a constant vector a: r'cm = Σmi(ri + a)/Σmi = Σmiri/Σmi + a(Σmi/Σmi) = rcm + a.The CM label shifts by exactly a — the same shift every particle got — so its position relative to the body is unchanged.
2. Concrete check with two 1 kg particles at x = 0 and x = 4 m: xcm = 2 m. Re-origin at the first particle's left by 10 m so they sit at 10 and 14: xcm = (10 + 14)/2 = 12 = 2 + 10 ✓. Same physical midpoint, new label.
3. Rotation behaves the same way: rcm is a linear combination of the ri, and rotation is a linear operation, so Rrcm is the CM of the rotated positions.
4. So (a) is wrong if read as "the physical point moves", and (c) is wrong because there is no case in which it moves — the proof in step 1 holds for every body and every origin."May or may not" options survive only when a genuine counter-case exists; here the algebra is unconditional.
5. Physical clincher: the balance point of a lamina on a pin, or the point about which a thrown spanner spins, is found without any axes. A quantity you can locate experimentally cannot depend on a bookkeeping choice.
6. Hence (b): the centre of mass is independent of the choice of co-ordinate system. Its coordinates are not — and the question, following standard usage, asks about the point.

5The position of the centre of mass of a cube of uniform density will be at: (a) edge of a cube (b) the centre of one face (c) the centre of the intersection of diagonals of one face (d) the geometric centre of the cubeCM of a uniform cubeThe position of the centre of mass of a cube of uniform mass density will be at (a) the centre of...🟢 Exactdthe geometric centre of the cube
Concept Uniform density plus symmetry pins the centre of mass exactly. If a body has a plane of symmetry, its CM must lie in that plane — otherwise reflecting the body would move the CM while leaving the body unchanged, which is impossible. A cube has three mutually perpendicular mirror planes through its middle, and the CM must lie in all three at once. Three non-parallel planes meet in a single point: the cube's own centre. No integration is needed; symmetry alone decides it.
Formula rcm = ∫r dm / ∫dm, with dm = ρ dV and ρ constant ⇒ rcm = ∫r dV / V. Constant ρ cancels top and bottom, so for a uniform body the centre of mass is exactly the centroid of the volume.
Solution
1. Put the cube of side a with one corner at the origin, occupying 0 ≤ x, y, z ≤ a. By uniformity, xcm = (1/V)∫x dV = (1/a³)∫0ax·a²dx = (a²/a³)(a²/2) = a/2.A slab of thickness dx at position x has volume a²dx, so the 3-D integral collapses to a 1-D one — the standard slicing trick for a prism.
2. By the identical argument in the other two directions, ycm = a/2 and zcm = a/2, giving rcm = (a/2, a/2, a/2) — the geometric centre, the point where the four body diagonals meet.
3. Symmetry route (one line, no calculus): the cube is unchanged by reflection in each of the three mid-planes x = a/2, y = a/2, z = a/2, so the CM lies on all three; their intersection is the single point (a/2, a/2, a/2).
4. Reject (a): an edge point has z or y equal to 0 or a, violating two of the three symmetry conditions — and mass sits on both sides of it in every direction.
5. Reject (b) and (c): these describe the same point, the middle of one face, since a square face's diagonals intersect at its centre. Two options naming one point cannot both be right, so neither is.Spotting the (b) = (c) duplication eliminates half the option set in about three seconds, before any physics is done.
6. Any face centre fails the third symmetry: it lies at the boundary in the direction perpendicular to that face, whereas the CM must be equidistant from both opposite faces — hence at depth a/2, not 0.

The word "uniform" is the licence to use pure symmetry; delete it (say, a cube whose density grows with height) and the CM slides along the vertical axis away from the centre while staying on it. So read for "uniform" first, then count symmetry planes; three of them through the middle always land you on the geometric centre.
Answer: (d) the geometric centre of the cube.
