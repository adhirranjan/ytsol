Concept Combustion is the reaction of a substance with oxygen, and it is the archetypal heat-releasing process — that is precisely why fuels are burnt. Energy flows out of the system into the surroundings, so the products sit lower in enthalpy than the reactants and the enthalpy change carries a minus sign.
Key relation ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants; heat released ⇒ ΔH < 0. The strong C=O and O–H bonds formed in CO2 and H2O release far more energy than is spent breaking the weaker C–C, C–H and O=O bonds, so the net balance is always negative.
Solution
1. Recall what combustion is: complete reaction with O2, typically giving CO2 and H2O for an organic substance.
2. Observe the everyday evidence: burning fuel warms its surroundings, so heat leaves the system. A reaction that releases heat is exothermic — the first blank.
3. Translate into the sign convention. Heat leaving the system at constant pressure means qp is negative, and qp = ΔH.The convention is fixed from the system's point of view: energy in is positive, energy out is negative.
4. So ΔH is negative — the second blank.
5. Check with a standard value: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l), ΔH = −890.3 kJ mol−1. Negative, as expected.
6. Note the word "always": every combustion is exothermic, which is why a positive heat of combustion in a question is an immediate red flag.
 combustion = burning in O2 -> CO2 + H2O
 heat flows OUT of the system
   -> exothermic
   -> q_p negative  and  q_p = dH
   -> dH is NEGATIVE

1 (ii)Thermochemical equation can be reversed by changing ……… of ΔH.Reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔHClass 11 Chapter 6 | Thermodynamics 08 || Hess's Law || Hess's Law Enthalpy Change IIT JEE / NEET |🟡 Similarsign
Concept A thermochemical equation is a balanced chemical equation with its enthalpy change attached. Because enthalpy is a state function, running the reaction backwards returns the system to where it started, so the enthalpy change must be exactly undone — same magnitude, opposite sign.
Key relation If A → B has ΔH = +x, then B → A has ΔH = −x. Adding the two gives A → A with ΔH = 0, which must be true for any closed loop of a state function — the consistency check that forces the sign flip.
Solution
1. Write a forward reaction with its enthalpy: H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH = −286 kJ.
2. Reverse it: H2O(l) → H2(g) + ½O2(g). Forming water releases 286 kJ, so splitting it must cost 286 kJ.
3. Therefore the reversed reaction has ΔH = +286 kJ — the magnitude is unchanged and only the sign flips.If the magnitude changed too, you could run the loop forwards and backwards and create or destroy energy — a violation of the first law.
4. So the blank is sign.
5. Note the companion rule: multiplying an equation by a factor multiplies ΔH by the same factor, whereas reversing it only changes the sign.
6. Both rules together are what make Hess's-law manipulations legal — thermochemical equations can be reversed, scaled, added and subtracted like algebra.
 forward:  H2 + 1/2 O2 -> H2O(l)   dH = -286 kJ
 reverse:  H2O(l) -> H2 + 1/2 O2   dH = +286 kJ
 only the SIGN changes, magnitude is unchanged

 reverse  -> flip sign
 multiply -> multiply dH by the same factor
⚡ JEE Trick Keep the two manipulations distinct: reversing flips the sign, scaling multiplies the value. Nearly every Hess's-law problem is just a sequence of those two moves applied to the given equations until they add up to the target — get them straight and the arithmetic follows automatically.
Answer: sign — reversing a thermochemical equation reverses the sign of ΔH.

Concept Enthalpy changes depend on the conditions, so tabulated values are meaningless unless everyone agrees on a reference. Thermochemistry fixes that reference as 298 K (25°C) and 1 atmosphere, with every substance in its most stable physical form at those conditions. Values quoted there carry the superscript degree symbol, ΔH°.

Solution
1. Recall why a standard state is needed: ΔH depends on temperature and, for gases, on pressure, so tabulated data need agreed conditions.
2. Fill the temperature blank: the conventional reference is 25°C, i.e. 298 K.
3. Fill the pressure blank: 1 atmosphere.Modern IUPAC usage prefers 1 bar, which differs from 1 atm by only about 1%; Indian syllabus questions still say 1 atm and the difference never matters numerically at this level.
4. Add the third, unstated part of the definition: each substance must be in its most stable form at those conditions — graphite not diamond, O2 not O3, liquid water not steam.
5. Note the consequence used constantly in this lecture: an element in its standard state has ΔHf° = 0 by definition.
6. So the blanks are 298 K and 1 atmospheric pressure.

1. Define the quantity: calorific value is the heat evolved on complete combustion of one gram of fuel.

2 (i)State whether true or false.
The heat of reaction does not depend upon the time consumed for the completion of the reaction but depends upon the manner how the change has been brought.		ΔH is a state function — independent of pathHess's Law and Heats of Formation🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept The statement is a two-part claim, and it gets the first part right and the second part exactly backwards. Enthalpy is a state function: its change depends only on the initial and final states, not on how long the reaction took and not on the route by which it happened.
Key relation ΔH = Hfinal − Hinitial, independent of path. This is precisely the property that makes Hess's law work — if ΔH depended on the manner of the change, adding equations algebraically would be illegal.
Solution
1. Split the statement into its two claims: (a) ΔH does not depend on the time taken; (b) ΔH does depend on the manner in which the change is brought about.
2. Claim (a) is true — thermodynamics says nothing about rate, so a fast and a slow route give the same ΔH.
3. Claim (b) is false. Because H is a state function, the enthalpy change is fixed by the endpoints alone; the path is irrelevant.Contrast with q and w individually, which are path functions — but their sum, and ΔH at constant pressure, are not.
4. Demonstrate it: carbon can be burnt straight to CO2, or first to CO and then to CO2. Both routes give the same total ΔH — that is Hess's law in action.
5. Since one half of the compound statement is wrong, the statement as a whole is false.
6. The corrected version would read: "the heat of reaction depends neither on the time consumed nor on the manner in which the change is brought about".
 H is a STATE FUNCTION
   dH = H(final) - H(initial)     path-independent

 "not on time"      TRUE
 "but on manner"    FALSE  <- this breaks the statement

 proof: C -> CO2 direct, or C -> CO -> CO2
        both give the same total dH   (Hess's law)
⚡ JEE Trick True/false items often bolt a true clause onto a false one — read to the end and judge every clause, since one wrong clause makes the whole statement false. Keep the classification firm: U, H, S, G, T, P, V are state functions; q and w are path functions.
Answer: False — ΔH depends on neither the time nor the manner of the change.

2 (iii)Heat of reaction is independent of temperature.Kirchhoff's law — ΔH does vary with TKIRCHHOFF'S EQUATION : Variation of heat of a reaction with temperature.🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept Heat of reaction is not independent of temperature. Reactants and products generally have different heat capacities, so warming both sides by the same amount changes the enthalpy gap between them. Kirchhoff's law is the quantitative statement of exactly this dependence — its existence disproves the claim.
Key relation ΔH2 = ΔH1 + ΔCP(T2 − T1), with ΔCP = ΣCP(products) − ΣCP(reactants). Only in the special case ΔCP = 0 does ΔH stay constant with temperature — a coincidence, not a rule.
Solution
1. Read the claim: heat of reaction is independent of temperature.
2. Recall Kirchhoff's law, which explicitly gives ΔH as a function of T. Its very form contradicts the claim.
3. See why physically: heating the reactants stores ΣCP(reactants)ΔT in them and heating the products stores ΣCP(products)ΔT. Unless those match, the difference between the two sides shifts.That is exactly why enthalpy tables must state a temperature, usually 298 K.
4. Give a concrete case from this chapter: for ½N2 + 3/2 H2 → NH3, ΔCP = −4.5 cal K−1, so ΔH falls from −11.00 kcal at 298 K to −13.14 kcal at 773 K — a shift of over 2 kcal.
5. Therefore the statement is false.
6. The corrected version: heat of reaction varies with temperature according to Kirchhoff's equation, the direction of the change being set by the sign of ΔCP.
 claim: dH independent of T          FALSE

 dCp > 0 -> dH rises with T
 dCp < 0 -> dH falls with T
 dCp = 0 -> dH unchanged (special case only)

 e.g. NH3 formation: -11.00 kcal (298 K) -> -13.14 (773 K)

2 (v)Thermochemical equations can be added or subtracted like algebraic equations.Hess's law licenses algebraic manipulationHess's Law | Hess's Law Examples | Hess's Law Numerical Problems🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept This is Hess's law expressed operationally. Because enthalpy is a state function, the enthalpy change of an overall reaction equals the sum of the changes of any set of steps that add up to it — so thermochemical equations really can be treated as algebra, added, subtracted, reversed and scaled.
Key relation ΔHoverall = ΣΔHsteps for any route linking the same initial and final states. Path-independence is the licence: if ΔH depended on the route, summing arbitrary intermediate steps would give the wrong total.
Solution
1. Recall Hess's law: the total enthalpy change is the same whether the reaction happens in one step or several.
2. Note what that permits operationally — equations may be added, subtracted, reversed (flip the sign) and multiplied by a factor (scale ΔH by the same factor).
3. Demonstrate with the lecture's own worked example: 6×[C + O2 → CO2] plus 3×[H2 + ½O2 → H2O] minus [C6H6 + 15/2 O2 → 6CO2 + 3H2O] gives 6C + 3H2 → C6H6, ΔH = +49.6 kJ.Species appearing on both sides cancel exactly like algebraic terms — that cancellation is the whole technique.
4. Since the manipulation is valid, the statement is true.
5. Note the practical importance: it lets you obtain enthalpies for reactions that cannot be measured directly, such as the formation of benzene or of CO from carbon.
6. The one discipline to keep: whatever you do to an equation you must do to its ΔH.
 Hess: dH(overall) = sum of dH(steps)

 allowed operations on a thermochemical equation:
   add / subtract      -> add / subtract dH
   reverse             -> flip sign of dH
   multiply by n       -> multiply dH by n

 e.g. 6(iii) + 3(iv) - (ii) gives dHf(benzene) = +49.6 kJ
⚡ JEE Trick Work Hess's-law problems backwards from the target: write the equation you want, then ask what each given equation must be multiplied by or reversed to produce it. Chasing forwards through the data is far slower and is where sign errors creep in.
Answer: True — Hess's law makes thermochemical equations algebraically manipulable.
2 (vi)The heat of combustion as determined from bomb calorimeter gives the value of heat evolved at constant volume.Bomb calorimeter measures qv = ΔUBomb Calorimeter & Tricks to solve Bomb Calorimeter questions easily🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept A bomb calorimeter is a sealed, rigid steel vessel. Its walls cannot move, so the volume is fixed and no expansion work can be done. With the work channel closed, the heat measured is exactly the internal energy change — heat evolved at constant volume, precisely as the statement says.
Key relation ΔV = 0 ⇒ w = 0 ⇒ qv = ΔU. To convert the measured value into the constant-pressure heat that thermochemical tables quote, apply ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT afterwards.
Solution
1. Recall the apparatus: the sample burns inside a bolted steel bomb pressurised with excess oxygen, immersed in a stirred water bath.
2. Note the mechanical constraint: the bomb cannot expand, so ΔV = 0 throughout the combustion.
3. Apply the work formula: w = −PextΔV = 0. No energy escapes as expansion work.The pressure inside rises sharply during the burn, but with no volume change that pressure does no work.
4. Apply the first law: ΔU = q + w = qv. So the heat measured is the heat at constant volume, and the statement is true.
5. Contrast with an open (coffee-cup) calorimeter, which runs at constant atmospheric pressure and therefore measures qp = ΔH.
6. Practical consequence used in Level-6 Q9 of this lecture: a bomb value must be corrected by ΔngRT before it can be quoted as an enthalpy of combustion.
 bomb calorimeter = sealed RIGID vessel
   dV = 0  ->  w = 0  ->  q_v = dU        statement TRUE

 open calorimeter, P = 1 atm  ->  q_p = dH
 convert: dH = dU + dn(gas) R T
⚡ JEE Trick Treat "bomb" as shorthand for constant volume ⇒ ΔU. Then ask what the question wants: if it asks for ΔH you must add ΔngRT, and if Δng = 0 (as for C + O2 → CO2) the two coincide and no correction is needed.
Answer: True — a bomb calorimeter measures qv = ΔU.
2 (vii)In a chemical reaction heat of formation is always evolved.Endothermic formations exist (ΔHf > 0)The enthalpies of elements in their standard states are taken as zero. The enthalpy of formation...🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept The claim that heat is "always evolved" on formation is simply untrue. Many compounds have positive enthalpies of formation — they absorb heat when made from their elements, sitting higher in enthalpy than the elements themselves. NO, HI, N2O and acetylene are standard examples.
Key relation ΔHf < 0 for an exothermic (endergonically stable) compound, ΔHf > 0 for an endothermic one. The sign depends on whether the bonds formed in the compound release more energy than is spent breaking the bonds within the elements — for very strongly bonded elements like N2, they do not.
Solution
1. Read the claim: heat of formation is always evolved, i.e. ΔHf is always negative.
2. Look for a counterexample. This very Quick Recap supplies one: ΔHf(NO) = +33 kJ mol−1, which is absorbed, not evolved.
3. Add more: ΔHf(HI) = +30.4 kJ mol−1 (used in Level-4 Q1 here), ΔHf(N2O) ≈ +82, ΔHf(C2H2) ≈ +227 kJ mol−1.Nitrogen compounds dominate this list because the N≡N triple bond (946 kJ mol−1) is so costly to break that the products rarely recover the outlay.
4. One counterexample is enough, so the statement is false.
5. Interpret the meaning: a positive ΔHf marks an endothermic compound, generally less stable with respect to its elements — which is why such compounds are often reactive or explosive.
6. Corrected statement: heat of formation may be either evolved (ΔHf < 0, the common case) or absorbed (ΔHf > 0).

Concept Enthalpies cannot be measured absolutely — only changes can. Thermochemistry therefore fixes an arbitrary but universal zero: every element, in its most stable physical form at 298 K and 1 atm, is assigned zero enthalpy of formation. That reference is called the element's standard state.
Key relation ΔHf°(element in its standard state) = 0, defined at 298 K, 1 atm. Because only differences matter, choosing the elements as the zero makes every compound's ΔHf° a measurable, tabulatable number.
Solution The expected Board answer:
1. Definition. The standard state of an element is its most stable physical form at 298 K (25°C) and 1 atmosphere pressure.
2. Its enthalpy is taken as zero. By convention ΔHf° = 0 for an element in this state, since forming an element from itself involves no change.
3. The most stable allotrope is specified. Carbon is graphite, not diamond; oxygen is O2, not O3; sulfur is rhombic, not monoclinic.Phosphorus is the standing exception in the syllabus: white phosphorus is taken as the reference even though black is the more stable allotrope.

Concept Enthalpy of reaction is the heat exchanged when a reaction proceeds exactly as its balanced equation is written, at constant pressure. Concept Resonance energy measures how much more stable benzene is than a hypothetical molecule with three isolated double bonds. Key relation ΔHactual = ΔHf(cyclohexane) − ΔHf(benzene); ΔHtheoretical = 3 × ΔHhyd(cyclohexene); resonance energy = actual − theoretical. Benzene releases less heat than three isolated double bonds would, precisely because it starts out already stabilised.
Solution
1. Write the real reaction: C6H6(l) + 3H2(g) → C6H12(l).
2. Compute its actual enthalpy from formation data: ΔH = ΔHf(cyclohexane) − ΔHf(benzene) = −159 − (+46) = −205 kJ mol−1.
3. Compute the theoretical value for three isolated double bonds: 3 × (−119) = −357 kJ mol−1.Cyclohexene has exactly one C=C, so its hydrogenation enthalpy is the natural per-double-bond benchmark.
4. Take the difference: resonance energy = −205 − (−357) = +152 kJ mol−1 in magnitude, i.e. benzene is 152 kJ mol−1 more stable than predicted.
5. Match the stated form: the resonance energy is given as −38x kJ mol−1, so −38x = −152.
6. Solve: x = 152/38 = 4.

10From the following data of ΔH of the following reactions,

C(s) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + H2(g); ΔH = 132 kJ
Calculate the mole composition of the mixture of steam and oxygen on being passed over coke at 1273 K, keeping temperature constant.		Thermal balance — exothermic leg must offset endothermic legHess's Law | Hess's Law Examples | Hess's Law Numerical Problems🟡 SimilarO<sub>2</sub> : H<sub>2</sub>O = 0.6 : 1
Concept Two reactions run over the hot coke at once: oxygen burns carbon to CO and releases heat, while steam reacts with carbon endothermically. For the bed to hold a constant temperature, the heat released by the first must exactly cancel the heat absorbed by the second — and that balance fixes the mole ratio.
Key relation Thermal balance: n(O2) × heat released per mole O2 = n(H2O) × heat absorbed per mole H2O. The first equation is written per ½ mole of O2, so its heat must be doubled before the comparison is fair.
Solution
1. Write the exothermic leg: C(s) + ½O2(g) → CO(g), ΔH = −110 kJ. That is 110 kJ released per half mole of O2.
2. Scale it to a full mole of oxygen: 2C + O2 → 2CO releases 220 kJ per mole of O2.Missing this doubling is the main trap — it would give a ratio of 1.2 : 1 instead.
3. Write the endothermic leg: C(s) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + H2(g), ΔH = +132 kJ, i.e. 132 kJ absorbed per mole of steam.
4. Impose constant temperature: heat released = heat absorbed, so 220 × n(O2) = 132 × n(H2O).
5. Solve for the ratio: n(O2)/n(H2O) = 132/220 = 0.6.
6. State the composition: O2 : H2O = 0.6 : 1, i.e. 0.6 mole of oxygen for every mole of steam — equivalently 3 : 5.

Answer: O2 : H2O = 0.6 : 1 (3 : 5 by moles).
