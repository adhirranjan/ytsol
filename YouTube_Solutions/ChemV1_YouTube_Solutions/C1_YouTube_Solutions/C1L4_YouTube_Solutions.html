1Dalton's law: total pressure of a non-reacting mixture equals which combination of partial pressures? (MCQ)Dalton's law statementA-DALTON🟡 SimilarbThe sum of the partial pressures
Concept Dalton's law of partial pressures treats a mixture of non-reacting gases as if each gas were alone in the container. Every gas exerts its own pressure — its partial pressure — completely independently of the others, because ideal-gas molecules do not interact. The total pressure the mixture pushes on the walls is therefore just the running total of those independent contributions, added up.
Formula Ptotal = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …, where each Pi is the pressure that gas i alone would exert in the same volume at the same temperature. Pressures add (not multiply, not average) because each gas independently bombards the walls and the impacts simply accumulate.
Solution
1. Identify what the question asks: how Ptotal relates to the individual partial pressures.The four options offer sum, product, difference and average — only one is Dalton's law.
2. Apply Dalton's law: the total is the sum of all partial pressures, Ptotal = ΣPi.
3. Reject the distractors: a product or average of pressures has the wrong units/meaning; only addition conserves the total force per unit area.
 combination        matches Dalton?
 sum   P₁+P₂+…        ✓  ← Dalton's law
 product P₁·P₂        ✗  wrong units
 average (P₁+P₂)/2    ✗  not additive
 difference P₁−P₂     ✗  meaningless
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever gases do not react, pressures simply add — Ptotal = ΣPi. Trap: the law fails the moment the gases react (e.g. NH₃ + HCl), because moles are consumed and the simple sum no longer holds.
Answer: (b) The sum of the partial pressures.
22 mol H₂ + 1 mol N₂ in a 10 L vessel at 300 K — total pressure (R=0.0821)Total pressure via PV=nRTA-DALNUM🟡 Similarb7.39 atm
Concept For a non-reacting mixture, the pressure depends only on the total number of moles of gas — the chemical identity of each gas is irrelevant to pressure (ideal-gas behaviour). So the fastest route is to add the moles of every component and feed that single total into the ideal-gas equation. Dalton's law is built into this: PV = ntotalRT already equals the sum of each gas's PV = niRT.
Formula P = ntotalRT ÷ V, with ntotal in mol, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T in K, V in L, P in atm. Multiplying moles by RT and dividing by volume gives the pressure the whole collection exerts.
Solution
1. Add the moles: ntotal = 2 (H₂) + 1 (N₂) = 3 mol.Pressure cares only about how many gas particles are present, not which kind.
2. List the data in matching units: T = 300 K, V = 10 L, R = 0.0821.
3. Substitute: P = (3 × 0.0821 × 300) ÷ 10.
4. Numerator: 3 × 0.0821 = 0.2463; 0.2463 × 300 = 73.89.
5. Divide by volume: P = 73.89 ÷ 10 = 7.389 ≈ 7.39 atm.
 quantity     value
 n_total      2 + 1 = 3 mol
 R·T          0.0821 × 300 = 24.63
 n·R·T        3 × 24.63    = 73.89
 P = /V       73.89 / 10   = 7.39 atm
⚡ JEE Trick For total pressure of a mixture, just sum the moles and use PV = ntotalRT once — never compute each gas separately and re-add. Trap: temperature must be in kelvin (300 K here); plugging 27 in a Celsius figure or forgetting the +273 wrecks the answer.
Answer: (b) 7.39 atm.
31 L, O₂ & He: PO₂=0.6 atm, PHe=1.4 atm; volume doubled at const T — new partial pressuresPartial pressure + BoyleA-DALNUM🟡 Similarb0.3 atm and 0.7 atm
Concept Each gas in a mixture obeys Boyle's law independently — its own partial pressure responds to a volume change exactly as if the other gas were absent. When the container volume is enlarged at constant temperature, every partial pressure falls in inverse proportion to the volume. Doubling the volume therefore halves each partial pressure, one gas at a time.
Formula Boyle's law per component: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ ⇒ P₂ = P₁ × (V₁ ÷ V₂), at constant T. Pressure × volume is a constant for each gas, so if volume doubles the pressure must halve to keep the product fixed.
Solution
1. Volume goes from V to 2V, so V₁ ÷ V₂ = V ÷ 2V = 1/2 for both gases.The same volume ratio applies to every component present.
2. Oxygen: P₂(O₂) = 0.6 × ½ = 0.3 atm.
3. Helium: P₂(He) = 1.4 × ½ = 0.7 atm.
4. (Check) New total = 0.3 + 0.7 = 1.0 atm, exactly half the old total 2.0 atm — consistent with doubling the volume.
 gas   P₁(atm)   ×(V₁/V₂)=½   P₂(atm)
 O₂     0.6       ½            0.3
 He     1.4       ½            0.7
 total  2.0                    1.0
⚡ JEE Trick Volume changes act on each partial pressure separately by Boyle's law — double V ⇒ halve every Pi. You do not need mole fractions here. Trap: don't halve only the total and split it by the wrong ratio; scale each gas's own pressure.
Answer: (b) 0.3 atm and 0.7 atm.

1Obtain a relation between partial pressure and mole fraction of a gasPi = xi·Ptotal derivationDerive the relationship between partial pressure of gas and total pressure of gas and mole fraction🟢 Exact—
Concept Each gas in an ideal mixture behaves independently, so we can write the ideal-gas equation twice: once for a single component and once for the whole mixture, both sharing the same volume V and temperature T. Dividing one by the other makes V, R and T cancel, leaving a clean relation between a gas's partial pressure and its mole fraction. This is the workhorse formula behind almost every numerical in this lesson.
Key relation for component i: PiV = niRT; for the mixture: PtotalV = ntotalRT. Same V and T for every gas because they share one container — that is what lets the ratio simplify.
Solution
1. Write both ideal-gas equations at common V, T: PiV = niRT and PtotalV = ntotalRT.
2. Divide the first by the second: PiV ÷ (PtotalV) = niRT ÷ (ntotalRT).
3. Cancel the common factors V, R, T on each side: Pi ÷ Ptotal = ni ÷ ntotal.
4. Recognise ni ÷ ntotal as the mole fraction xi, giving Pi ÷ Ptotal = xi.
5. Rearrange: Pi = xi × Ptotal.
 P_i V     = n_i     R T
 P_tot V   = n_tot   R T
 ───────────────────────  (divide)
 P_i/P_tot = n_i/n_tot = x_i   ⇒   P_i = x_i · P_total
⚡ JEE Trick The partial pressure of a gas is its mole fraction times the total pressure — memorise Pi = xi·Ptotal. Trap: mole fraction uses moles, not masses or volumes; convert masses to moles first when the data is in grams.
Key point: dividing PiV = niRT by PtotalV = ntotalRT cancels V, R, T and yields Pi = xi·Ptotal, i.e. partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure.

2Dalton's law is applicable to … (non-reacting gases only)Applicability of Dalton's lawA-DALTON🟡 SimilarbNon-reacting gases only
Concept Dalton's law assumes each gas keeps its own identity and mole count, so its partial pressure is well-defined and the pressures merely add. This breaks the instant the gases react — a reaction consumes some molecules and creates others, changing the total moles, so the simple sum of the "original" partial pressures is no longer valid. Hence the law applies only to non-reacting (chemically inert toward each other) gases.
Key idea Ptotal = ΣPi holds only if ni of every gas is unchanged. A reaction alters the moles, so partial pressures are redefined and the plain sum fails.
Solution
1. Ask when Ptotal = ΣPi stays true: only while each gas's amount is fixed.
2. Reacting gases change their moles, violating the assumption; inert mixtures do not.
3. Therefore the law is valid for non-reacting gases only — option (b).
 mixture type        Dalton's law?
 non-reacting gases   ✓  moles fixed
 reacting gases       ✗  moles change (e.g. NH₃+HCl)
⚡ JEE Trick The single condition to remember is "non-reacting." If any two gases in the list would react (NH₃ + HCl, NO + O₂), the law is void. Trap: options saying "all gases" or "ideal gases only" miss the real restriction — it is about reaction, not ideality.
Answer: (b) Non-reacting gases only.
