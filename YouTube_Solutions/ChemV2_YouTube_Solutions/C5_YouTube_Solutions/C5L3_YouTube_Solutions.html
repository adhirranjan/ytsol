1According to valence bond theory, which condition is essential for the formation of a covalent bond? (Level 1)
(a) Orbitals must be fully filled   (b) Orbitals must contain unpaired electrons   (c) Orbitals must be of the same energy always   (d) Orbitals must be vacant		VBT — overlap conditionValence Bond Theory — the overlapping condition for a covalent bond🟡 SimilarbOrbitals must contain unpaired electrons
Concept The Heitler–London picture of a covalent bond is one shared pair produced by overlap of two half-filled orbitals. For the overlap to produce a bonding (lower-energy) state, each orbital must bring exactly one electron, and those two electrons must be of opposite spin so that the Pauli principle allows them to occupy the same region of space between the nuclei. A completely filled orbital has both spins already used up — pushing a third electron into that region is forbidden, so a filled orbital cannot form a normal covalent bond. A completely empty orbital brings no electron at all, so there is nothing to share (that is the route to a coordinate bond, not a normal covalent bond).
Statement Heitler–London conditions: two orbitals must overlap, and the overlapping orbitals must be (i) half-filled (unpaired electron) and (ii) carry anti-spin electrons (↑ and ↓). Condition (i) fixes how many electrons arrive; condition (ii) fixes whether they may pair. Fail either one and no localized bond orbital of maximum electron-pair probability can form.
Solution
1. Write the electron bookkeeping for a bond that does form — H₂:H: 1s¹ (↑) + H: 1s¹ (↓) ⇒ two half-filled orbitals, anti-spin ⇒ one σ(1s–1s) bond pair. Bond forms.
2. Now test option (a), fully filled orbitals — He₂:He: 1s² (↑↓) + He: 1s² (↑↓) ⇒ four electrons for one bond region. Two of them must go into an antibonding arrangement, net stabilisation = 0, so helium is monatomic. Fully filled orbitals therefore cannot bond.
3. Test option (d), vacant orbitals: an empty orbital contributes zero electrons, so no shared pair is possible.An empty orbital can accept a whole lone pair — that gives a coordinate (dative) bond, e.g. H₃N: → BF₃ — but the question asks about the ordinary covalent bond of VBT.
4. Test option (c), "same energy always": HF is built from H(1s) overlapping F(2pz) — two orbitals of clearly different energy — and the bond is perfectly good. So equal energy is not required.
5. Only option (b) survives: the orbitals must be half-filled, i.e. contain unpaired electrons.

 He + He      1s²(↑↓) + 1s²(↓↑)          2 + 2 = 4   NO   (filled)
 H  + (empty) 1s¹     + vacant            1 + 0 = 1   NO   (nothing to share)
 H  + F       1s¹     + 2p 1              1 + 1 = 2   YES  σ(1s-2p)  ⇒ equal energy NOT needed

Trap: option (d) "vacant orbitals" is the condition for a coordinate bond and option (a) "fully filled" is the condition for a lone pair — both are real ideas planted in the wrong question.

2Which of the following types of overlapping will have the highest bond energy? (Level 2)
(a) 2s–2s   (b) 2s–2p   (c) 2p–2p   (d) 1s–2p		Bond energy of overlapss–s, s–p and p–p overlapping compared — which gives the strongest bond🟡 Similarc2p–2p
Concept Bond energy tracks the extent of overlap, and the extent of overlap is decided by how directional the participating orbitals are. An s-orbital is a sphere: its electron density is smeared uniformly in all directions, so only a small fraction of it can ever be pushed into the internuclear region. A p-orbital is a dumb-bell pointing along one axis: essentially all of one lobe can be aimed straight at the partner. Hence, within the same shell, the ordering of head-on overlap strength is s–s < s–p < p–p, exactly as the theory of this lecture states.
Key relation For head-on (axial) overlap of the same shell: B.E.(2s–2s) < B.E.(2s–2p) < B.E.(2p–2p) the more directionally concentrated the sub-shell, the greater the overlap integral and hence the bond energy. Also from the theory: p–p (lateral) < s–s < s–p < p–p (head-on) sideways p–p overlap is a π interaction and is the weakest of all.
Solution
1. Note that all four options are head-on (σ) overlaps, so the only variable is orbital directionality.
2. Count directional partners in each option:
  • (a) 2s–2s → 0 directional orbitals — weakest of the same-shell set.
  • (b) 2s–2p → 1 directional orbital — intermediate.
  • (c) 2p–2p → 2 directional orbitals — maximum overlap, strongest.
3. Deal with option (d) 1s–2p separately: it mixes shells.The theory of this lecture also ranks overlap by shell: 1–1 > 1–2 > 2–2 > 2–3 — but that ordering is about how close the valence shell sits to the nucleus, and the question's own comparison set (2s–2s, 2s–2p, 2p–2p) is a same-shell set. Within the shell asked about, 2p–2p wins; the printed key is (c).
4. Sanity check on real bonds: F–F is a 2p–2p σ bond and Cl–Cl is a 3p–3p σ bond — both are genuine single bonds; the H–H bond (1s–1s, shell 1) is stronger still because shell 1 sits closest to the nucleus, which is exactly the "1–1 > 1–2 > 2–2" rule and not part of this option set.
5. Therefore the highest bond energy among the listed overlaps is 2p–2p — option (c).

⚡ JEE Trick Score each overlap by "how many dumb-bells are pointing at each other": two = strongest, one = middle, none = weakest. That single count settles every s/p overlap-ordering MCQ in seconds. 3When two hydrogen atoms approach each other, a covalent bond is formed at the internuclear distance where: (Level 3)
(a) Potential energy is maximum   (b) Potential energy is minimum   (c) Repulsive forces dominate   (d) Attractive and repulsive forces both become zero		PE curve of H₂The potential energy (y) curve for H2 formation as a function of internuclear distance (x)🟢 ExactbPotential energy is minimum
Concept Bring two H atoms together and four electrostatic interactions switch on at once: two new attractions (HA–eB and HB–eA) and two repulsions (eA–eB and HA–HB). At long range the attractions win because the electrons sit between the nuclei while the nuclei themselves are still far apart, so the potential energy falls. At very short range the bare nucleus–nucleus repulsion blows up as 1/r and the curve turns sharply upward. Between the two regimes there is a single distance at which the net force is zero and the energy is lowest — that is where the molecule actually sits.
Key relation Bond forms at r = d₀ where dU/dr = 0 and U = Umin. Zero slope means the net force is zero — attractions exactly balance repulsions. It does not mean the individual forces are zero; they are large and equal and opposite. For H₂, d₀ = 74 pm and the depth of the well is the bond dissociation energy, 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. List the forces the lecture identifies, with their sign:
  • Attractive: HA–eA, HB–eB (own electron) and HA–eB, HB–eA (the new ones created by approach).
  • Repulsive: eA–eB (electron–electron) and HA–HB (nucleus–nucleus).
2. Follow the curve from right to left: at large r there is no interaction and U = 0; as r shrinks the new attractions dominate and U becomes increasingly negative.
3. At r = 74 pm the curve bottoms out — maximum stability, minimum potential energy — and this is precisely where the bond is said to be formed.Stability and potential energy run in opposite directions: the lower the energy, the more stable the system. A system always settles at the bottom of its energy well.
4. Kill the distractors:
  • (a) maximum PE = the steep repulsive wall at very small r — the least stable arrangement, the opposite of bonding.
  • (c) "repulsive forces dominate" describes r < d₀, where the atoms are being squeezed and are pushed apart again.
  • (d) both forces zero only at r → ∞, i.e. two isolated atoms with U = 0 — no molecule at all.
5. Hence the bond forms at the point of minimum potential energy — option (b).

                       and H_B-e_A

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the H₂ well with numbers attached: d₀ = 74 pm, well depth = 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹. 4Why is head-on overlap stronger than lateral overlap? (Level 4)
(a) Because p-orbitals cannot undergo lateral overlap   (b) Because electron cloud concentration is greater along the internuclear axis   (c) Because energy of p-orbitals decreases during lateral overlap   (d) Because s-orbitals cannot overlap head-on		σ vs π overlap strengthWhy Sigma Bond is Stronger than Pi Bond🟢 ExactbBecause electron cloud concentration is greater along the internuclear axis
Concept In a head-on (axial) overlap the two lobes meet nose-to-nose on the line joining the nuclei, so the accumulated electron cloud sits exactly where it is most useful — screening both nuclei from each other and being attracted by both at once. In a lateral (sideways) overlap the lobes meet above and below that line, so the charge builds up off-axis, where each nucleus sees it at a larger distance and at an unfavourable angle. Since bond energy is governed by how much electron density can be placed in the internuclear region, axial overlap always wins. This is why a σ bond is stronger than a π bond, and why a π bond can never exist on its own.
Key relation bond energy ∝ extent of overlap in the internuclear region. Axial: σ — density maximum ON the internuclear axis, cylindrically symmetric, free rotation allowed. Lateral: π — density above and below the axis, nodal plane containing the axis, rotation locked. A π bond has a node exactly along the line joining the nuclei — the region where density would help most has zero electron density.
Solution
1. Ask where the shared cloud ends up in each case.Head-on: on the axis, between the nuclei. Sideways: in two banana-shaped lobes above and below the axis.
2. Ask how effectively that cloud glues the nuclei together: charge on the axis is pulled by both nuclei along the line of the bond, so its stabilisation is maximal; off-axis charge is farther away and pulls partly sideways, so it stabilises less.
3. Conclude that the greater electron-cloud concentration along the internuclear axis is exactly what makes head-on overlap stronger — option (b).
4. Eliminate the rest:
  • (a) false — p-orbitals overlap laterally all the time; that is precisely how the two π bonds of N₂ are made.
  • (c) false and backwards — the poorer lateral overlap gives less stabilisation, not a decrease in orbital energy that would favour it.
  • (d) false — s-orbitals overlap only head-on (they are spherical, so there is no "sideways" for them); this statement is the exact opposite of the truth.
5. Confirm with data: in N₂ the first (σ) bond is worth far more than either π bond, and this is why π bonds break first in addition reactions.

 (axial)        pulled by BOTH nuclei along bond

⚡ JEE Trick Say it as a picture: "σ puts the glue on the line, π puts it off the line." That one sentence answers σ-vs-π strength, why π breaks first, why C=C cannot rotate, and why bond energy of a double bond is less than twice a single bond. Trap: options (a) and (d) are absolute statements ("cannot") — in bonding questions an absolute is almost always the planted false answer, because both lateral p–p overlap and head-on s–s overlap are textbook realities.
Answer: (b) Because electron cloud concentration is greater along the internuclear axis.
5In methane (CH₄), all C–H bonds are identical because: (Level 5)
(a) Carbon uses only p orbitals for bonding   (b) All hydrogen atoms approach carbon from equal distances   (c) Carbon undergoes excitation followed by hybridisation to generate equivalent orbitals   (d) Overlap between s and p orbitals is naturally equal		Excitation & hybridisation in CH₄Why does methane have four identical C-H bonds?🟢 ExactcCarbon undergoes excitation followed by hybridisation to generate equivalent orbitals
Concept Ground-state carbon is 1s² 2s² 2px¹ 2py¹ 2pz⁰ — only two unpaired electrons, so plain VBT predicts a covalency of two (CH₂). To reach four bonds an electron is first promoted from 2s to the empty 2pz, giving four half-filled orbitals: 2s¹ 2px¹ 2py¹ 2pz¹. But those four are not equivalent — one is spherical and three are mutually perpendicular dumb-bells, which would predict three strong p–s bonds at 90° and one weaker, directionless s–s bond. Experiment says all four C–H bonds are identical in length and strength at 109°28′. The repair is hybridisation: the one 2s and the three 2p orbitals are mathematically mixed into four identical sp³ orbitals pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron.
Key relation 1 × 2s + 3 × 2p → 4 × sp³ (each 25% s, 75% p), directed at 109°28′ (109.5°). Hybrid orbitals are, by construction, degenerate and identical in shape, so the four C–H overlaps are identical — which is exactly the observation to be explained.
Solution
1. Write the ground state and count unpaired electrons:₆C: 1s² 2s²(↑↓) 2px¹(↑) 2py¹(↑) 2pz⁰ ⇒ only 2 unpaired ⇒ covalency 2. Contradicts CH₄.
2. Promote (excite) one 2s electron into 2pz:₆C*: 1s² 2s¹(↑) 2px¹(↑) 2py¹(↑) 2pz¹(↑) ⇒ 4 unpaired ⇒ covalency 4. The small promotion energy is repaid many times over by forming two extra bonds.
3. Notice the problem that is still left: 2s is spherical while 2px, 2py, 2pz are perpendicular, so the naive prediction is three p–s bonds at 90° and one different s–s bond of uncertain direction.
4. Apply hybridisation: mix the four orbitals to get four equivalent sp³ hybrids at 109°28′, which then overlap head-on with four H 1s orbitals — giving four identical σ bonds. That is option (c).
5. Eliminate the others: (a) is false — the 2s orbital is fully involved through the hybrid; (b) confuses cause with effect, since equal H distances are the result of equivalent hybrids, not the reason for them; (d) is plainly false — s–s and p–s overlaps are not naturally equal, which is precisely why hybridisation was needed.

1Explain how are formal charges calculated on oxygen atom in ozone molecule.Formal charge in O₃Calculating the formal charge on every oxygen of ozone, O₃🟢 Exact—
Concept A formal charge is a bookkeeping device, not a real charge. It answers one question: "compared with the free atom, how many electrons does this atom appear to own in the Lewis structure?" The convention is deliberately crude — every bond pair is split exactly in half, and every lone pair is assigned entirely to its own atom. Ozone is the standard illustration because its three oxygen atoms, all of the same element, end up with three different formal charges, which is only possible if the electron bookkeeping is done atom by atom.
Formula F.C. = (valence e⁻ of free atom) − (non-bonding e⁻) − ½ (bonding e⁻), i.e. F.C. = V − L − ½B. V = 6 for every oxygen. L counts electrons sitting in lone pairs on that atom. B counts all electrons in bonds to that atom, so a double bond contributes 4 and a single bond 2. Equivalently F.C. = V − L − (number of bonds). Check: Σ F.C. = total charge on the species — for neutral O₃ the three formal charges must add to 0.
Solution
1. Draw the Lewis structure of O₃. Total valence electrons = 3 × 6 = 18. The accepted structure is bent, with the central oxygen joined by one double bond and one single bond:O(1)=O(2)–O(3), where O(2) is central. O(1) carries 2 lone pairs, O(2) carries 1 lone pair, O(3) carries 3 lone pairs. Count: 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 lone-pair electrons + 6 bonding electrons = 18 ✓.
2. Central oxygen O(2): V = 6, lone-pair electrons L = 2 (one lone pair), bonding electrons B = 4 (double bond) + 2 (single bond) = 6.F.C. = 6 − 2 − ½(6) = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1.
3. Doubly-bonded terminal oxygen O(1): V = 6, L = 4 (two lone pairs), B = 4.F.C. = 6 − 4 − ½(4) = 6 − 4 − 2 = 0.
4. Singly-bonded terminal oxygen O(3): V = 6, L = 6 (three lone pairs), B = 2.F.C. = 6 − 6 − ½(2) = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1.
5. Verify the total: (+1) + (0) + (−1) = 0, which matches the neutral molecule — the bookkeeping is consistent.
6. Interpret the result: the +1/−1 pair does not mean ozone is ionic. Because the two O–O bonds are equivalent by resonance, the real molecule has two identical bonds of length 128 pm (between a single bond, 148 pm, and a double bond, 121 pm) and the −1 is smeared equally over both terminal atoms as −½ each.

2Write the postulates of Valence bond theory.Postulates of VBTValence bond theory | VBT postulates🟢 Exact—
Concept Valence bond theory is the quantum-mechanical rewrite of Lewis's shared pair. It was proposed by Heitler and London (1927) and extended by Pauling and Slater. Its central claim is that a covalent bond is a localized object: it lives in the region where two atomic orbitals overlap, and the atoms otherwise keep their own orbitals and their own identity. Everything else — bond strength, bond direction, bond angles — follows from the geometry and extent of that overlap. Writing the postulates therefore means writing (i) the condition for overlap, (ii) what overlap produces, (iii) where the bond energy comes from, and (iv) how the extent of overlap is controlled.
Key relations bond energy ∝ extent of overlap and bond length ∝ 1 / extent of overlap. Greater overlap ⇒ more electron density between the nuclei ⇒ stronger attraction ⇒ shorter, stronger bond. Overlap ordering of the lecture: 2s–2s < 2s–2p < 2p–2p (same shell) and 1–1 > 1–2 > 2–2 > 2–3 (shell distance), with p–p lateral < s–s < s–p < p–p head-on.
Solution
1. Overlap postulate (Heitler–London): a covalent bond forms when two atoms come close enough that an orbital of one overlaps an orbital of the other.
2. Condition on the orbitals: the overlapping orbitals must (a) be half-filled, i.e. contain an unpaired electron, and (b) contain electrons of opposite (anti-) spin.Both are Pauli requirements — two electrons may share the same region of space only if their spins are paired.
3. Result of overlap: a new localized bond orbital is created in the overlap region, in which the probability of finding the shared electron pair is a maximum.
4. Origin of bond energy: it arises from (a) the electrostatic attraction between the nuclei and the accumulated electron cloud, and (b) the cancellation/attraction between the spins of the two anti-spin electrons.
5. Overlap–strength relation: the greater the overlap, the shorter the bond length and the greater the bond energy and bond strength.
6. Pauling–Slater extension — what controls the extent of overlap:(i) the nature of the orbitals: a more directionally concentrated sub-shell overlaps better, giving 2s–2s < 2s–2p < 2p–2p; (ii) the nature of the overlapping: head-on (axial) beats lateral (sideways); (iii) the closeness of the valence shell to the nucleus: 1–1 > 1–2 > 2–2 > 2–3.
7. Directional character: s-orbitals are spherically symmetrical and can therefore show only head-on overlap (only σ bonds); p-orbitals are directional and can show head-on or lateral overlap, so they can form σ or π bonds.
8. Bond types produced: head-on overlap gives a σ bond (strong, freely rotating); lateral overlap gives a π bond (weaker, no free rotation, never found without an accompanying σ bond).
9. Hybridisation (Pauling's addition): to explain observed covalency and equivalent bond angles, atomic orbitals of comparable energy on the same atom mix to give an equal number of identical hybrid orbitals — this is what makes all four C–H bonds of CH₄ identical at 109°28′.

Key point: VBT (Heitler–London, extended by Pauling & Slater) — a covalent bond is formed by overlap of two half-filled orbitals carrying anti-spin electrons; overlap creates a localized bond orbital of maximum electron-pair probability; bond energy comes from nucleus–electron-cloud attraction plus spin pairing; greater overlap ⇒ shorter, stronger bond; extent of overlap depends on the nature of the orbitals, the type of overlapping and the closeness of the valence shell to the nucleus; head-on overlap gives σ, lateral overlap gives π; hybridisation explains observed covalency and equivalent bond angles.

1What type of electron spins are required for covalent bond formation?
(a) Same spin   (b) Paired spin   (c) Anti-spin   (d) Parallel spin		Anti-spin requirementOverlapping of orbitals — σ and π bonds and the spin condition🟡 SimilarcAnti-spin
Concept Two electrons may occupy the same region of space only if they differ in spin — that is the Pauli exclusion principle written for a bond. Before the bond forms, the two electrons sit in separate half-filled orbitals on separate atoms; once overlap creates a single localized bond orbital they must share it, and sharing is allowed only when one spin is ↑ and the other ↓. If both arrive with the same spin, the wavefunction becomes antibonding: the electron density is expelled from the internuclear region, a node appears there, and the two atoms repel instead of bonding.
Key relation Bonding: ↑ (on A) + ↓ (on B) → one shared pair (↑↓) in the overlap region ⇒ energy falls. Non-bonding: ↑ (on A) + ↑ (on B) → repulsion, node between the nuclei ⇒ energy rises. "Anti-spin", "opposite spin", "antiparallel spin" and "paired spins after bonding" all describe the same requirement — the two spins must cancel, giving a net spin of zero for the bond pair.
Solution
1. Recall the lecture's stated Heitler–London requirement: overlapping orbitals must have (a) half-filled nature and (b) anti-spin electrons.
2. Translate into arrows for H₂:HA 1s¹(↑) + HB 1s¹(↓) ⇒ the pair (↑↓) can be accommodated in the same bond orbital, so a σ(1s–1s) bond forms and the energy drops by 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. 2What does hybridisation help explain in molecules?

Concept Hybridisation was invented to repair two specific failures of plain VBT, both geometric. First, pure atomic orbitals are of different shapes (s spherical, p dumb-bell), so the bonds they form would be of different strengths — contradicted by experiment. Second, pure p-orbitals are mutually perpendicular, so all bond angles would be forced to 90° — again contradicted by the observed 109°28′ in CH₄, 120° in BF₃ and 180° in BeCl₂. Mixing orbitals of comparable energy into an equal number of identical hybrids fixes both problems at once: the hybrids are degenerate (equal bonds) and they splay out to definite angles (definite shape). Key relation n atomic orbitals mixed → n identical hybrid orbitals, arranged to keep themselves as far apart as possible: sp → 180° linear, sp² → 120° trigonal planar, sp³ → 109.5° tetrahedral. Solution
1. Ask what plain VBT gets wrong for CH₄: it predicts three p–s bonds at 90° plus one different s–s bond of no fixed direction.

  • (b) Mass is fixed by the nuclei and is completely untouched by how orbitals mix.
  • (d) Density is a bulk, intermolecular property (packing), not a statement about one molecule's orbitals.

4The condition for strongest overlapping between orbitals is:

Concept The Pauling–Slater extension of VBT states that the extent of overlapping depends on the nature of the orbitals and the nature of the overlapping, and adds that the closer the valence shell is to the nucleus, the greater the overlapping. Two independent factors are therefore at work. Directionality decides how much of an orbital can be aimed at the partner: a dumb-bell p-orbital concentrates its density along one axis, a sphere spreads it everywhere. Compactness decides how tightly that density is held: a shell close to the nucleus is small and dense, so when two such shells meet the charge build-up in the overlap region is large; a diffuse outer shell is thin and smeared, and its overlap is correspondingly feeble.
Key relation extent of overlap ↑ with directionality and with compactness (closeness of the valence shell to the nucleus), hence B.E. : 1–1 > 1–2 > 2–2 > 2–3 shells and B.E. : 2s–2s < 2s–2p < 2p–2p. Solution
1. Screen the options against the two factors named in the theory — directionality and closeness to the nucleus.

3. (b) "Orbitals are diffused" — a diffused orbital spreads its electron density thinly over a large volume, so the density that ends up in the overlap region is small. This is the reason bond energy falls as you go down a group (H–F ≫ H–I). Wrong, and in fact the opposite of the requirement.

Head-on p–p overlap likewise piles density on the axis and is σ. Only the sidewise (lateral) p–p arrangement gives the two-lobed, nodal-plane pattern of a π bond.
Key relation σ ⇒ head-on (axial) overlap ⇒ density ON the axis ⇒ no node along the bond. π ⇒ lateral (sidewise) overlap of parallel p orbitals ⇒ density ABOVE and BELOW ⇒ nodal plane through both nuclei. Bond energy order p–p(lateral) < s–s < s–p < p–p(head-on): the π is the weakest because its density is farthest from the internuclear line.
Solution

  • (a) s–s — two spheres meeting; density piles up on the axis ⇒ σ. Also, an s orbital is spherically symmetrical and by the lecture's own statement "shows only head-on overlapping", so it can never give π.
  • (b) s–p — again head-on, density on the axis ⇒ σ (this is the HF and HCl bond).
  • (c) p–p end-to-end — head-on, density on the axis ⇒ σ (this is the F₂ and Cl₂ bond).
  • (d) p–p sidewise — two parallel dumb-bells perpendicular to the axis overlap lobe-to-lobe above and below ⇒ π. ✔

3. Concept The central postulate of valence bond theory is that the strength of a covalent bond is proportional to the extent of overlap of the two atomic orbitals. The mechanism behind that postulate is purely electrostatic: overlapping the orbitals piles shared electron density into the region between the two nuclei, and both nuclei are attracted to that same cloud. The more density sits there, the more the energy of the system is lowered relative to the free atoms, and the deeper the potential-energy well — which is what "higher bond energy" means. So the Reason here is not just a true statement standing alongside the Assertion; it is the actual cause of it.
Key relation bond energy ∝ extent of overlap, because overlap ⇒ shared density between the nuclei ⇒ increased nucleus–electron cloud attraction ⇒ lower total energy ⇒ deeper well. The same principle explains the whole hierarchy of this lecture: σ > π (more overlap), p–p > s–p > s–s for directional orbitals, and HF > HCl > HBr > HI (compact orbitals overlap better).
Solution
1. Judge the Assertion. "Greater overlap ⇒ higher bond energy" is the defining statement of VBT and is confirmed everywhere: a σ bond (large overlap) is stronger than a π bond (small overlap); the H–H bond of 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹ corresponds to the point of maximum overlap at 74 pm. A is TRUE.
2. Judge the Reason. During overlap the electron cloud is concentrated between the nuclei; each nucleus attracts that cloud, and the sum of these attractions is what pulls the atoms together. R is TRUE.
3. Test whether R explains A. Ask: does the increased nucleus–cloud attraction actually produce the higher bond energy?Yes — bond energy is defined as the depth of the potential-energy well, and the well is deep precisely because the nucleus–shared-cloud attraction outweighs the nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron repulsions. More overlap ⇒ more shared density ⇒ more attraction ⇒ deeper well ⇒ higher bond energy. The chain is unbroken.
4. Therefore both are true and R is the correct explanation of A ⇒ option (a).
5. Reject the alternatives: (b) would require R to be a true but irrelevant fact, yet R states the exact mechanism; (c) and (d) each require one of the statements to be false, but both are textbook-correct.

Concept Both statements are true, but they are about two different variables. Solution
1. Judge the Assertion. Does the extent of overlap depend on which kind of orbital is used? That is why this lecture states the overlap ordering s–s < s–p < p–p. A is TRUE.
