Concept The opening definition of the whole lecture. A substance that furnishes ions when dissolved — and therefore conducts electricity in solution — is called an electrolyte.
Key relation electrolyte → ions in solution → solution conducts; non-electrolyte → no ions → no conduction Conduction is the observable test; ion production is the underlying cause.
Solution
1. The book defines an electrolyte as “a substance capable to furnish ions in its solution”.
2. NaCl is the standard example: in water it furnishes Na+ and Cl−, so it is an electrolyte.
3. The blank is therefore filled with Electrolytes.
4. The contrasting class is the non-electrolytes — urea, sucrose, CCl4, C2H5OH in water — which produce no ions and so cannot carry a current.
5. Note the phrase “in solution”: an ionic solid such as NaCl also conducts when molten, which is what makes it a true electrolyte as well as a strong one.
6. Electrolytes split two ways in this lecture — strong/weak by the extent of ionisation, and true/potential by whether the ions pre-exist in the pure substance.
7. Those two classifications are independent: HCl is a strong electrolyte in water but a potential one, because pure liquid HCl is covalent and does not conduct.

                   -> no conduction

1 (ii)Fill in the blank: The degree of ionization is a measure of the fraction of molecules that ______ into ions when a substance is dissolved in water.Definition of αIonization Constant (Kₐ), Degree of Ionization (α), and Factors Affecting Degree of Ionization🟡 Similardissociate (ionize)
Concept The degree of ionisation α is a fraction, and what it counts is the fraction of the dissolved molecules that have actually broken up — that have dissociated into ions.
Key relation α = (moles of electrolyte dissociated)/(moles of electrolyte added initially) Both quantities are mole counts, so α is a pure number between 0 and 1.
Solution
1. Dissolve one mole of an electrolyte and let the solution reach ionic equilibrium.
2. Some fraction of it has broken into ions; call that fraction α.
3. So α is the fraction of molecules that have dissociated (equivalently, ionised) into ions. That is the blank.
4. Because it is a ratio of two amounts of the same substance, α carries no units and cannot exceed 1.
5. For a strong electrolyte α ≈ 1 (90–100%); for a weak one it is typically only 1–10% at ordinary dilutions.
6. That numerical split is exactly what separates the two classes, and it is why item 1(iii) of this Quick Recap can identify a “high degree of ionization” with a strong electrolyte.
7. Note that α here means the same thing it meant in L-6, where it was measured through vapour density; here it is measured through conductivity instead.

 strong electrolyte : alpha ~ 0.9 to 1
 weak electrolyte   : alpha ~ 0.01 to 0.10

 dimensionless , 0 <= alpha <= 1
⚡ JEE Trick “Dissociate” and “ionise” are used interchangeably throughout this chapter. Strictly, dissociation separates pre-existing ions and ionisation creates them from a covalent molecule — but no examination question in this lecture turns on the distinction.

1 (iii)Fill in the blank: A substance with a high degree of ionization is a ______ electrolyte.Strong vs weakIdentifying Strong Electrolytes, Weak Electrolytes, and Nonelectrolytes - Chemistry Examples🟡 Similarstrong
Concept The definition of the two classes rests entirely on α. High degree of ionisation means a strong electrolyte; low means a weak one.
Key relation α ≈ 1 (90–100%) ⇒ strong; α ≪ 1 (1–10%) ⇒ weak There is no third category here — a substance producing no ions at all is a non-electrolyte, not a weak one.
Solution
1. Strong electrolytes are those “completely ionized in aqueous solution”, with α = 1 in the idealised treatment.
2. Weak electrolytes are “partially ionized in aqueous solution”, with α less than one.
3. So a substance with a high degree of ionisation is a strong electrolyte — that is the blank.
4. Examples of the strong class: strong acids (H2SO4, HCl, HNO3), strong bases (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2) and essentially all soluble salts.
5. Examples of the weak class: HCN, CH3COOH, H2CO3 among acids; NH4OH, Cu(OH)2, Al(OH)3 among bases.
6. High α also means many ions per unit volume, so a strong electrolyte’s solution is highly conducting — the observable consequence.
7. Note that strength here is about the extent of ionisation, not about concentration: a dilute HCl solution is still a strong electrolyte.
 alpha high  ->  STRONG electrolyte
                 solution highly conducting
 alpha low   ->  weak electrolyte
                 solution poorly conducting

 strong : HCl, H2SO4, NaOH, NaCl
 weak   : CH3COOH, HCN, NH4OH
⚡ JEE Trick Do not confuse “strong” with “concentrated”. Strength is a fixed property of the substance in a given solvent; concentration is how much of it you put in. A 0.001 M HCl is dilute but still strong.
Answer: strong electrolyte.

1 (v)Fill in the blank: The solubility of gas in water ______ with an increase in temperature.Gas solubility and temperatureDielectric Constant|Polarity Of Solvent|Solubility| GS🟡 Similardecreases
Concept Dissolving a gas in water is exothermic, so by Le Chatelier heating drives the gas back out. Gas solubility therefore decreases as the temperature rises.
Key relation X(g) ⇌ X(aq) + heat; T ↑ ⇒ shift left ⇒ solubility ↓ The reverse of the pattern for most solids, whose dissolution is usually endothermic.
Solution
1. Write the dissolution of a gas as X(g) ⇌ X(aq), with heat evolved on the forward step.
2. Treating heat as a product, raising the temperature adds a product and pushes the equilibrium backward.
3. Gas therefore comes out of solution, so the solubility decreases with increasing temperature. That is the blank.
4. Everyday confirmation: a fizzy drink goes flat much faster when warm, and boiled water tastes flat because the dissolved air has been driven off.
5. Contrast this with most solids, whose dissolution is endothermic, so their solubility rises with temperature — the familiar sugar-in-hot-tea observation.
6. Level-3 Q4 of this lecture asks the same fact as a multiple-choice question, with (b) “decreases with increase in temperature” as the answer.
7. And item 3(E)→(s) of this Quick Recap pairs gas solubility with the entry that names this very effect.
 X(g)  <=>  X(aq) + heat       (exothermic)

 T up   -> shift left  -> solubility DOWN
 T down -> shift right -> solubility up

 solids : usually endothermic, opposite trend
⚡ JEE Trick Gases and solids move in opposite directions with temperature. Fix the gas case with the flat-warm-soda picture and the solid case with sugar in hot tea, and neither needs to be memorised as a rule.
Answer: decreases.
1 (vi)Fill in the blank: The ionization of ______ electrolytes is not affected by dilution.Dilution and strong electrolytesEffect of dilution on strong and weak electrolytes/conductance/Electrochemistry.🟡 Similarstrong
Concept A strong electrolyte is already essentially fully ionised, so there is nothing left for dilution to do. Only weak electrolytes have room to ionise further.
Key relation α ≈ 1 already ⇒ dilution cannot raise it further The ceiling α = 1 is what makes the strong case insensitive.
Solution
1. For a weak electrolyte, diluting shifts the ionic equilibrium forward and raises α toward 1.
2. For a strong electrolyte, α is already at or near its ceiling of 1 before any dilution.
3. There is therefore nothing for dilution to increase, so the ionisation of strong electrolytes is not affected by dilution. That is the blank.
4. Level-3 Q5(d) of this lecture states the same fact as a true statement: “the ionization of strong electrolytes is almost unaffected by dilution”.
5. Note the word “almost” in that statement. In reality inter-ionic attractions make a concentrated strong-electrolyte solution behave as if slightly less than fully ionised, and dilution relieves that — but the effect is small.
6. Contrast item 3(D)→(q), which pairs dilution with “increases degree of ionization” — that entry is about weak electrolytes.
7. So dilution splits the two classes cleanly: it moves α for a weak electrolyte and leaves it alone for a strong one.
 weak   : alpha small -> dilution RAISES it
                          toward 1
 strong : alpha ~ 1 already
                       -> dilution changes
                          essentially nothing

 ceiling alpha = 1 is the reason
⚡ JEE Trick Ask “is there room to move?” before applying any dilution rule. A quantity already at its maximum cannot be increased, and that one observation answers every strong-versus-weak dilution question in this lecture.

Solution
1. The lecture lists strong electrolytes as “all salts (except a few like CdBr2, HgCl2)”.
2. The word “except” is the whole answer: since exceptions exist, the blanket statement is false.

2 (ii)True or false: The degree of ionization of a weak electrolyte increases with dilution.Dilution effectHow does degree of ionization of a weak electrolyte depend upon concentration of the solution?🟢 ExactTrue dilution shifts the ionic equilibrium forward
Concept Dilution reduces the ion concentrations, which pushes the ionic equilibrium toward more ions. For a weak electrolyte that raises α, so the statement is true.
Key relation Ostwald: K = Cα2/(1−α) ≈ Cα2, so α ≈ √(K/C) K is fixed at a given temperature, so α rises as C falls.
Solution
1. Write the ionisation AB ⇌ A+ + B− for a weak electrolyte at concentration C with degree α.
2. At equilibrium: [AB] = C(1−α), [A+] = [B−] = Cα.
3. So K = Cα2/(1−α), and for small α this is approximately Cα2.
4. Rearranging, α ≈ √(K/C). Since K is fixed by the temperature, reducing C increases α.
5. So the statement is true — and at infinite dilution α approaches 1, which is item 3 of the Suggested Problems.
6. The Le Chatelier reading gives the same answer without algebra: adding solvent lowers the ion concentrations, so the system responds by producing more ions.
7. Note the inverse-square-root form: to double α you must dilute fourfold, so the effect is real but slow.
 AB <=> A+ + B-      conc. C , degree alpha

 K = C alpha^2 / (1 - alpha) ~ C alpha^2
 alpha ~ sqrt(K/C)

 C down  ->  alpha UP
 C -> 0  ->  alpha -> 1  (infinite dilution)
⚡ JEE Trick Ostwald’s dilution law in the form α ≈ √(K/C) answers most of this lecture’s dilution questions in one line, and it also shows how fast α rises — as the inverse square root, not linearly.
Answer: True — α of a weak electrolyte increases with dilution.
2 (iii)True or false: The degree of ionization is not affected by the concentration of the electrolyte.Concentration dependenceHow does degree of ionization of a weak electrolyte depend upon concentration of the solution?🟡 SimilarFalse α depends strongly on concentration for a weak electrolyte
Concept This is the previous statement inverted, and it fails for the same reason. Ostwald’s law makes α an explicit function of concentration, so the claim is false.
Key relation α ≈ √(K/C) — explicitly concentration-dependent Only K is independent of concentration; α is not.
Solution
1. From Ostwald’s dilution law, α ≈ √(K/C) for a weak electrolyte.
2. C appears explicitly in that expression, so α does depend on the concentration. The statement is false.
3. Numerically: for K = 1.8×10−5, a 0.1 M solution gives α ≈ 0.013 while a 0.001 M solution gives α ≈ 0.13 — ten times larger.
4. What is independent of concentration is the equilibrium constant K itself, which responds only to temperature.
5. Confusing the two is precisely what the statement invites, and it is the single most common error in this topic.
6. The one case where the statement is nearly true is a strong electrolyte, whose α sits at its ceiling of 1 regardless of concentration — but the statement is unqualified, so that does not save it.
7. Item 1(vi) of this Quick Recap states that restricted version correctly, which is a useful contrast.

2 (iv)True or false: Electrolytes are only found in liquid form.True electrolytesTrue & Potential Electrolyte || Physical Chemistry || By learn with Utkarsh🟡 SimilarFalse true electrolytes are ionic solids and conduct when fused
Concept The lecture’s second classification — true versus potential electrolytes — exists precisely because some electrolytes are ionic in the solid state. So the claim is false.
Key relation true electrolytes: made of ions, conduct in liquid or fused state; potential: covalent, conduct only in solution NaCl is a solid built from Na+ and Cl− before any water is added.
Solution
1. The book defines true electrolytes as substances “made up of ions” that “pass current in liquid or fused state”.
2. All ionic solids qualify — NaCl, KCl and the rest — and they exist as solids, not liquids.
3. So electrolytes are certainly not only found in liquid form, and the statement is false.
4. Molten NaCl conducts electricity with no solvent present at all, which is the direct experimental demonstration.
5. The contrasting class is the potential electrolytes: covalent substances such as HCl which do not conduct in the pure liquid state but do once dissolved in a solvent of high dielectric constant.
6. So the property that matters is whether ions are present or producible, not the physical state of the substance on the shelf.
7. Note the pure solid does not conduct either — the ions are locked in the lattice — but that is about mobility, not about whether the substance is an electrolyte.

2 (v)True or false: A weak electrolyte is one that only partially ionizes in a solution.Definition of a weak electrolyteHow to Identify Strong, Weak, and Non-Electrolytes Examples & Practice Problems🟡 SimilarTrue that is the definition of a weak electrolyte
Concept This is the textbook definition restated, so it is true. Partial ionisation is exactly what distinguishes a weak electrolyte from a strong one and from a non-electrolyte.
Key relation weak electrolyte: 0 < α < 1, ions and undissociated molecules coexist at equilibrium Coexistence of both species is what makes an ionic equilibrium possible at all.
Solution
1. The book defines weak electrolytes as “those electrolytes which are partially ionized in aqueous solution”, with α less than one.
2. The statement says exactly that, so it is true.
3. The consequence is that both ions and undissociated molecules are present together — CH3COOH alongside CH3COO− and H+.
4. That coexistence is what makes a genuine ionic equilibrium possible, and it is why Ka and Kb are meaningful only for weak electrolytes.
5. Their solutions are correspondingly poor conductors, since only a small fraction of the dissolved material is carrying charge.
6. The three-way distinction to keep straight: strong (α ≈ 1), weak (0 < α < 1), non-electrolyte (α = 0).
7. A non-electrolyte such as urea is not a very weak electrolyte — it produces no ions at all, so it sits outside the classification entirely.

2. Formula. α = (moles of electrolyte dissociated at any time)/(moles of electrolyte added initially).
3. Range. Being a ratio of two amounts of the same substance, α is dimensionless and lies between 0 and 1; multiplied by 100 it becomes the percentage dissociation.
