Concept Work in the frame of the rotating rod. There the ring feels a centrifugal force mω²x pushing it outwards, and the rod is smooth, so nothing opposes it — the ring accelerates outwards with arel = ω²x. That acceleration grows with x, so integrate v dv = ω²x dx rather than using suvat. Finally remember the ring leaves the rod with two perpendicular velocity components: the sliding one and the tangential one it inherits from the rod.
Formula Rotating frame: arel = ω²x, so vreldvrel = ω²x dx. Ground speed: v = √(vrel² + (ωL)²). The tangential component ωL is perpendicular to the rod, the sliding component along it — so they combine by Pythagoras.
Solution
1. (a) In the rod's frame the only unbalanced force along the rod is the centrifugal one, so arel = ω²x, directed outwards.The Coriolis force is perpendicular to the rod and is taken by the rod's own reaction, so it never enters the along-rod equation.
2. Integrate from the release point x = L/2 (at rest) out to x = L: ∫v dv = ∫ω²x dx.
3. vrel²/2 = ω²(L² − L²/4)/2 ⇒ vrel² = (3/4)ω²L², so vrel = (√3/2)ωL along the rod.
4. At the moment of leaving, the ring is at radius L, so it is also carried sideways at the rod's own speed ωL.This tangential part is invisible in the rotating frame — forgetting it is the standard error and gives only (√3/2)ωL.
5. Combine the perpendicular components: v² = (3/4)ω²L² + ω²L² = (7/4)ω²L².
6. (b) v = (√7/2)ωL, directed at angle tan−1(√3/2) to the tangent.

2The minimum speed v with which a small ball should be pushed inside a smooth vertical tube of radius R from a height h such that it may reach the top of the tube is: (a) √(2g(h+2R)) (b) (5/2)R (c) √(g(5R−2h)) (d) √(2g(2R−h))Ball inside a vertical tubeWith what minimum speed v must a small ball should be pushed inside a smooth vertical tube from a...🟢 Exactd√(2g(2R − h))
Concept The single word that decides this question is tube. A tube has an inner wall as well as an outer one, so it can push the ball inwards or outwards; unlike a string or the inside of a loop, it never needs a minimum speed at the top. The ball therefore only has to arrive at the top with speed just above zero, and the whole problem collapses to energy conservation.
Formula Energy from the entry point at height h to the top at height 2R: ½v² = g(2R − h) ⇒ v = √(2g(2R − h)). vtop → 0 is permitted because the inner wall can supply the whole centripetal force there, and in the limit it needs none.
Solution
1. Read the geometry: the ball enters at height h above the bottom of the tube, and the top of the tube is at height 2R.
2. The tube is smooth, so mechanical energy is conserved between those two points.
3. Critical case: vtop = 0 is allowed, because the tube's inner wall can push outwards and hold the ball on the circle even at zero speed.Compare the loop-the-loop, where the track can only push inwards and vtop² ≥ gR is forced.
4. Energy: ½mv² = mg(2R − h) ⇒ v = √(2g(2R − h)), option (d).
5. Screen the distractors: (a) √(2g(h+2R)) adds the heights instead of subtracting; (c) √(g(5R−2h)) is the string version, carrying the extra gR from vtop² = gR; (b) (5/2)R is a length, not a speed.(c) is the trap for anyone who applies the loop-the-loop condition without noticing the word “tube”.
6. Sanity: if the ball is already released at h = 2R the formula gives v = 0, exactly right — it is already at the top.

3A smooth sphere of radius R is made to translate in a straight line with a constant acceleration a. Concept Because the sphere accelerates, its frame is non-inertial: add a pseudo-force ma opposite to the sphere's acceleration and the problem becomes a static-looking energy question. Both gravity and that pseudo-force are constant, so both have potentials, and the work–energy theorem in the sphere frame gives the relative speed directly — no need to solve differential equations.
Formula In the sphere frame: ½mv² = Wgravity + Wpseudo with Wgravity = mgR(1−cosθ) and Wpseudo = maR sinθ. The normal reaction is perpendicular to the relative velocity, so it does no work in this frame.
Solution
1. Sit on the sphere. The particle starts at the top at rest and slides to angular position θ; it feels gravity mg down and a pseudo-force ma horizontally, opposite to the sphere's acceleration.
2. Displacement from the top to angle θ: vertically down by R(1 − cosθ), horizontally by R sinθ in the direction the pseudo-force pushes.The particle slides towards the side the pseudo-force points, so that horizontal work is positive.
3. Work by gravity: mgR(1 − cosθ). Work by the pseudo-force: maR sinθ.
4. Work–energy theorem: ½mv² = mgR(1−cosθ) + maR sinθ.
5. Solve: v² = 2R(g − g cosθ + a sinθ) ⇒ v = [2R(a sinθ + g − g cosθ)]1/2, option (a).
6. Limit check: at a = 0 this reduces to v = √(2gR(1−cosθ)), the familiar sliding-off-a-dome result, and at θ = 0 it gives v = 0 — both correct, which rules out (b), (c) and (d).Testing a = 0 is the fastest way to eliminate options that have swapped sin and cos.

Concept Two facts unlock this. First, geometry: a chord drawn from a point O on the circumference subtends an inscribed angle θ at O equal to half the central angle, so the arc position is 2θ and the speed is v = 2aθ̇. Second, dynamics: because the single force points along the chord PO, the angle between that force and the tangent at P is itself θ, so its tangential and radial parts are F sinθ and F cosθ.
Formula F cosθ = mv²/a (radial) and F sinθ = m dv/dt (tangential), so tanθ = a(dv/dt)/v² with v = 2aθ̇. Dividing the two equations kills F, which is never given.
Solution
1. Place the centre C at the origin and O on the circle. If P is at central angle φ, the inscribed angle at O is θ = φ/2.
2. Arc length s = aφ = 2aθ, so the speed is v = 2aθ̇ and the tangential acceleration is dv/dt = 2aθ̈.The factor 2 is the whole geometric content: θ advances at half the rate of the central angle.
3. Resolving the chord-directed force: its component along the tangent at P is F sinθ and along the inward radius is F cosθ.
4. Newton's second law both ways: F sinθ = m(2aθ̈) and F cosθ = m(2aθ̇)²/a = 4maθ̇².
5. Divide: tanθ = 2aθ̈/(4aθ̇²) = θ̈/(2θ̇²), so θ̈/θ̇² = 2 tanθ.The ratio asked for is therefore a pure function of θ — independent of a, m and F, none of which are given.
6. At θ = 45°: θ̈/θ̇² = 2 tan45° = 2.

Concept A bead threaded on a wire is special: the wire can push it either inwards or outwards, so the reaction is signed rather than one-way. Near the top the bead is moving slowly and needs less centripetal force than gravity's radial component provides, so the wire must push it outwards; lower down it is fast and needs more, so the wire pushes inwards. 1. Geometry: the quarter circle runs from A near the top of the vertical wall to B on the ground, with the centre at the corner.
2. Energy from A: the bead has fallen R(1 − cosθ), so v² = 2gR(1 − cosθ).
