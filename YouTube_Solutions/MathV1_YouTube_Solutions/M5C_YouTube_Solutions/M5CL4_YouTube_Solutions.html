1 (i)If A + B + C = 180°, then secA(cosB cosC − sinB sinC) is equal to ______.cos(B+C) reduction in a triangleConditional identity: A+B+C=180° examples🟡 Similar−1
Concept The whole engine of a conditional identity is the constraint itself. Here A + B + C = 180° lets us replace the awkward pair (B, C) by the single angle A: since B + C = 180° − A, every symmetric combination of B and C collapses onto A. The bracket cosB cosC − sinB sinC is not a random expression — it is exactly the right-hand side of the cos(B+C) expansion, so recognising it is the entire problem.
Formula cos(X+Y) = cosX cosY − sinX sinY; cos(180° − A) = −cosA; secA = 1/cosA. The first folds the bracket into one cosine, the second uses the triangle constraint, the third cancels what is left.
Solution
1. Read the bracket as a known expansion: cosB cosC − sinB sinC = cos(B + C).It matches cos(X+Y) = cosX cosY − sinX sinY term for term, with X = B, Y = C. The minus sign is the tell — a plus sign would have been cos(B−C).
2. Apply the given condition to eliminate B and C: A + B + C = 180° ⇒ B + C = 180° − A.
3. Substitute: cos(B + C) = cos(180° − A) = −cosA.Cosine is negative in the second quadrant; the reduction formula cos(180° − A) = −cosA flips the sign but keeps the same angle.
4. Put this back into the full expression: secA · (cosB cosC − sinB sinC) = secA · (−cosA).
5. Write secA as 1/cosA and cancel: (1/cosA) · (−cosA) = −1.cosA cancels top and bottom, leaving the constant −1 with no angle left in it.

Memorise the pair sin(B+C) = +sinA but cos(B+C) = −cosA: the sine keeps its sign, the cosine flips. Trap: the answer is a pure number (−1), not an expression in A — if an angle survives to the end, you missed a cancellation.
Answer: −1.

Concept This tests one of the most quoted conditional identities: in any triangle A + B + C = 180°, so tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC. The sum of the tangents is never independent — it is forced to equal their product. Once you know the sum is 6, the product is 6 too, and the cotangent product is just its reciprocal.
Formula tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC (valid when A+B+C = 180°); cotθ = 1/tanθ. The first equation is the whole engine; the second converts a product of tangents into a product of cotangents by one reciprocal.
Solution
1. Use the triangle identity: since A + B + C = 180°, tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC.Proof sketch: A+B = 180°−C ⇒ tan(A+B) = −tanC ⇒ (tanA+tanB)/(1−tanA tanB) = −tanC, cross-multiply and rearrange.
2. Feed in the given value: the left side is 6, so tanA tanB tanC = 6.
3. Convert to cotangents: cotA cotB cotC = 1/(tanA tanB tanC) = 1/6.Each cot is the reciprocal of its tan, so the product of three cotangents is the reciprocal of the product of the three tangents.
4. Sanity check: all three factors sit under one reciprocal, so a product of 6 must give exactly 1/6 — no angle survives, as expected for a pure-number answer.

1 (iii)If A + B + C = 180°, then (tanA + tanB + tanC)/(tanA tanB tanC) is equal to ______.Tangent sum-product ratioTriangle: tanA+tanB+tanC = tanA tanB tanC🟡 Similar1
Concept This is the single most-used conditional identity in trigonometry: when three angles sum to 180°, their tangents satisfy tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC — the sum equals the product. Once that is established, the given ratio is a sum divided by a product that are literally the same number, so it must be 1. The real content of this question is proving the identity, not the division.
Formula tan(X+Y) = (tanX + tanY)/(1 − tanX tanY); tan(180° − A) = −tanA. The addition formula turns tanA + tanB into something containing tan(A+B), and the reduction formula ties tan(A+B) back to tanC.
Solution
1. Use the condition to isolate two angles: A + B + C = 180° ⇒ A + B = 180° − C.
2. Take tan of both sides: tan(A + B) = tan(180° − C) = −tanC.Tangent has period 180° and is odd, so tan(180° − C) = −tanC.
3. Expand the left side by the addition formula: (tanA + tanB)/(1 − tanA tanB) = −tanC.
4. Cross-multiply to clear the denominator: tanA + tanB = −tanC (1 − tanA tanB) = −tanC + tanA tanB tanC.
5. Move −tanC across to the left: tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC. The identity is proved.This holds for every triangle (and for any three angles summing to 180°), not just special ones.
6. Now evaluate the asked ratio. Numerator = tanA + tanB + tanC; denominator = tanA tanB tanC. By step 5 these two are equal.
7. A non-zero quantity divided by itself is 1, so the ratio = 1.Valid provided tanA tanB tanC ≠ 0, i.e. no angle is 0° or 180° — guaranteed in a genuine triangle.

3 (i)If cosA = m cosB, then cot((A+B)/2)·cot((B−A)/2) is (p+q)/(p+r). Find p, q, r.Ratio condition to half-angle cotangentsIf cosA = m cosB then cot((A+B)/2)…🟢 Exactp = m, q = 1, r = −1
Concept A ratio condition like cosA = m cosB begs for componendo–dividendo: turn the single equation into a sum-over-difference, then apply the sum-to-product formulas so half-angles appear. The awkward cosine ratio becomes a clean product of two cotangents of half-angles.
Formula cosA + cosB = 2 cos((A+B)/2) cos((A−B)/2); cosA − cosB = −2 sin((A+B)/2) sin((A−B)/2); componendo–dividendo. Dividing these two collapses the four cosines/sines into a pair of cotangents.
Solution
1. Write the condition as a ratio: cosA = m cosB ⇒ cosA/cosB = m/1.
2. Apply componendo–dividendo: (cosA + cosB)/(cosA − cosB) = (m + 1)/(m − 1).Adding 1 to and subtracting 1 from both sides of the ratio a/b = m/1 gives (a+b)/(a−b) = (m+1)/(m−1).
3. Expand each side by sum-to-product: (2 cos((A+B)/2) cos((A−B)/2))/(−2 sin((A+B)/2) sin((A−B)/2)) = −cot((A+B)/2) cot((A−B)/2).
4. Flip the odd factor: cot((A−B)/2) = −cot((B−A)/2), so the left side becomes cot((A+B)/2) cot((B−A)/2).Cotangent is odd, so swapping A and B in the difference introduces a minus sign that cancels the leading minus.
5. Equate: cot((A+B)/2) cot((B−A)/2) = (m + 1)/(m − 1), i.e. of the form (p+q)/(p+r) with p = m, q = 1, r = −1.

1. Handle A. Given sinA = 1/2 and π/2 < A < π, so A is in quadrant II.
2. Find cosA: cos²A = 1 − sin²A = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 ⇒ cosA = ±√3/2. In quadrant II cosine is negative, so cosA = −√3/2.This sign choice is the whole point of quoting the range — A = 150°, not 30°.

1. (A) Start from the tangent identity for a triangle: tanA + tanB + tanC = tanA tanB tanC.

9Passage: For θ ∈ [0, 2π], rθ is the arc length of a circle of radius r and area of sector OAB is ½r²θ. Concept A geometry–trigonometry hybrid. Two chords drawn from a single point P on the circle cut it into three parts; requiring all three to have area πr²/3 pins down the angle θ between the chords. Two facts do the work: the segment area formula ½r²(φ − sinφ), and the inscribed-angle theorem, which converts the angle at P into a central angle. Symmetry lets us handle just one outer region.
Formula Sector of central angle φ: ½r²φ; triangle of two radii with included angle φ: ½r² sinφ; hence segment = sector − triangle = ½r²(φ − sinφ). Inscribed angle: angle at circumference = ½ × central angle on the same arc.
Solution
1. Set up: let the circle have centre O and radius r, with P the point on the circumference. The two chords PA and PB divide the disc into three regions of equal area πr²/3 each.Total area πr² split three ways.
2. Exploit symmetry: for the two outer regions to be equal, the configuration must be symmetric about the diameter through P. So let chord PA subtend central angle α at O, and chord PB subtend the same angle α on the other side.
3. Identify the outer regions: each is the circular segment cut off by its chord — the region between chord PA and the arc PA, lying on the far side from B.
4. Apply the segment formula with central angle α: outer area = ½r²(α − sinα).Segment = sector OPA (½r²α) minus triangle OPA (½r² sinα).
5. Set it equal to a third of the circle: ½r²(α − sinα) = πr²/3.
6. Cancel r² and multiply by 2: α − sinα = 2π/3. This fixes α.
7. Confirm the middle region is then automatic: πr² − 2(πr²/3) = πr²/3 ✓ — so equating the two outer segments to a third each is sufficient.Once two of the three regions are correct, the third has no choice.
8. Now relate α to the asked angle θ = ∠APB. The arc AB not containing P has central angle 2π − α − α = 2π − 2α.
9. By the inscribed-angle theorem, the angle at P subtending that arc is half of it: θ = ½(2π − 2α) = π − α.
10. Invert: α = π − θ. Substitute into step 6: (π − θ) − sin(π − θ) = 2π/3.
11. Use sin(π − θ) = sinθ: π − θ − sinθ = 2π/3.
12. Collect: θ + sinθ = π − 2π/3 = π/3.
