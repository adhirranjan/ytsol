L-1

Basic Graphs & Meaning of the Derivative

Basic graphs to recognise

  • y = x — straight line through the origin (slope 1).
  • y = x² — upward parabola, symmetric about the y-axis.
  • y = x³ — cubic, rising through the origin (S-shape).
  • y = 1/x — rectangular hyperbola in the 1st & 3rd quadrants.

Finite vs infinitesimal difference

  • Finite difference Δy = y₂ − y₁ (written with Δ).
  • Infinitesimal difference — a very-very small change, written with d (e.g. dy, dx).
derivative (definition)f′(x) = dy/dx = limΔx→0 [f(x+Δx) − f(x)] / Δx
  • Differentiation (= derivative) of y w.r.t. x is denoted f′(x) = dy/dx; dx is a small change in x, dy the corresponding change in y.
  • Notations: y′, dy/dx, df/dx, Dxf, and ẏ ("y dot", used for time derivatives, dy/dt).

Slope, secant & tangent

slope of a lineslope = tan θ (θ measured anticlockwise from +x axis)
average rate of changeΔy/Δx = [f(x+Δx) − f(x)] / Δx = slope of secant PQ
  • 1st quadrant: tan θ > 0 (slope positive); 2nd quadrant: tan θ < 0 (slope negative).
  • Secant cuts the curve at two points; tangent touches it at one point.
From secant to tangent
The average rate of change Δy/Δx equals the slope of the secant PQ. As Δx → 0, point Q slides toward P and the secant becomes the tangent at P. Hence dy/dx = slope of the tangent — the geometrical meaning of the derivative, and the tool for reading physics graphs.
💡 Key ideady/dx at a point = slope of the tangent to the curve at that point.

Rules for Differentiation

basic rulesd/dx(c) = 0 · d/dx(xⁿ) = n·xn−1 · d/dx(c·u) = c·du/dx · (u ± v)′ = u′ ± v′
product & quotient(uv)′ = u·v′ + v·u′ · (u/v)′ = (v·u′ − u·v′) / v²

Trigonometric, log & exponential

standard derivatives(sin x)′ = cos x · (cos x)′ = −sin x · (tan x)′ = sec²x · (cot x)′ = −cosec²x · (sec x)′ = sec x·tan x · (cosec x)′ = −cosec x·cot x · (ln x)′ = 1/x · (eˣ)′ = eˣ

Chain & power-chain rule

chain rule ("outside–inside")dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx) · d/dx(uⁿ) = n·un−1·(du/dx)
  • Radian vs degree: d/dx sin(x°) = (π/180)·cos(x°) — the extra π/180 comes from converting degrees to radians before differentiating.

Double differentiation

second derivatived/dx(dy/dx) = d²y/dx² · velocity v = ds/dt · acceleration a = dv/dt = d²s/dt²
When to reach for the product rule
The derivative of a product is not the product of derivatives: d/dx(x·x) = 2x, whereas (x′)(x′) = 1·1 = 1. Use (uv)′ = u·v′ + v·u′ whenever two x-dependent factors are multiplied — a very common JEE trap.
⚠ Trap(u/v)′ has the v·u′ first: (v·u′ − u·v′)/v². Swapping the order flips the sign of the whole answer.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-2
Let y = uv. Find y′(2) given u(2) = 3, u′(2) = −4, v(2) = 1, v′(2) = 2.
Product rule: y′ = u·v′ + v·u′.
y′(2) = u(2)·v′(2) + v(2)·u′(2) = (3)(2) + (1)(−4) = 6 − 4.
y′(2) = 2
Book Example · L-2
Find dy/dx for y = (x² − 1)/(x² + 1).
Quotient rule with u = x² − 1, v = x² + 1: dy/dx = [(x²+1)(2x) − (x²−1)(2x)] / (x²+1)².
Numerator = 2x³ + 2x − 2x³ + 2x = 4x.
dy/dx = 4x / (x² + 1)²
Book Example · L-2
Find the derivative of g(t) = tan(5 − sin 2t).
Chain rule twice: g′(t) = sec²(5 − sin 2t) · d/dt(5 − sin 2t).
d/dt(5 − sin 2t) = −cos 2t · 2 = −2 cos 2t.
g′(t) = −2 cos 2t · sec²(5 − sin 2t)
Book Example · L-2
The position of a particle is s = t³ − 6t² + 9t (s in m, t in s). Find the acceleration at t = 4 s.
v = ds/dt = 3t² − 12t + 9; a = dv/dt = 6t − 12.
a(4) = 6(4) − 12 = 24 − 12.
a(4) = 12 m/s²
L-3

Application of Derivatives — Rate of Change

  • dy/dx is the rate of change of y with respect to x. Most physics "rate" quantities are derivatives:
rates in physicsv = dx/dt · a = dv/dt · F = dp/dt · τ = dL/dt · P = dW/dt · I = dq/dt
💡 Tip"How fast is A changing when B is …?" ⇒ differentiate the relation, then substitute the given instant at the end.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
The area of a circle is A = (π/4)D². How fast is the area changing with the diameter when D = 10 m?
dA/dD = (π/4)·2D = πD/2.
At D = 10: dA/dD = π(10)/2 = 5π.
dA/dD = 5π m²/m ≈ 15.7 m²/m
Book Example · L-3
A body falls from rest so that s = ½gt² = 4.9t². (a) How far does it fall in the first 2 s? (b) Find its velocity, speed and acceleration at t = 2 s.
(a) s(2) = 4.9(2)² = 4.9 × 4 = 19.6 m.
(b) v = ds/dt = 9.8t ⇒ v(2) = 19.6 m/s (downward); speed = |v| = 19.6 m/s.
a = d²s/dt² = 9.8 (constant).
(a) 19.6 m (b) v = 19.6 m/s, speed 19.6 m/s, a = 9.8 m/s²
L-4

Maxima & Minima

  • At a maximum or minimum the tangent is horizontal, so the slope is zero — a stationary point.
stationary pointdy/dx = 0
ConditionFirst derivativeSecond derivative
Maximady/dx = 0d²y/dx² < 0
Minimady/dx = 0d²y/dx² > 0
Why the second-derivative test works
Just before a maximum the slope is positive, zero at the top, then negative after — so the slope is decreasing, i.e. d²y/dx² < 0. At a minimum the slope goes negative → 0 → positive, i.e. it is increasing, so d²y/dx² > 0. If the physical situation already tells you it is a max or min, the second-derivative test may be skipped.
⚠ Trapdy/dx = 0 only locates the stationary point. Always check the sign of d²y/dx² (or the physics) to decide max vs min.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-4
Find the minimum value of y = 1 + x² − 2x.
dy/dx = 2x − 2 = 0 ⇒ x = 1. d²y/dx² = 2 > 0 ⇒ minimum.
ymin = 1 + (1)² − 2(1) = 1 + 1 − 2.
ymin = 0 (at x = 1)
Book Example · L-4
Does the curve y = x⁴ − 2x² + 2 have horizontal tangents? If so, where?
dy/dx = 4x³ − 4x = 4x(x² − 1) = 0 ⇒ x = 0, 1, −1.
Corresponding points: (0, 2), (1, 1) and (−1, 1).
Horizontal tangents at x = 0, 1, −1
Book Example · L-4
A balloon rises straight up, tracked by a range finder 500 ft away. When the elevation angle is π/4, it increases at 0.14 rad/min. How fast is the balloon rising?
y = 500 tan θ ⇒ dy/dt = 500 sec²θ · dθ/dt.
At θ = π/4, sec²θ = (√2)² = 2: dy/dt = 500 × 2 × 0.14 = 1000 × 0.14.
dy/dt = 140 ft/min
Book Example · L-4
Water pours at rate Q into a cylindrical vessel of radius r. Find the rate at which the water height h rises.
Volume V = πr²h ⇒ dV/dt = πr²·dh/dt.
But dV/dt = Q, so dh/dt = Q/(πr²).
dh/dt = Q / (πr²)

Antiderivatives / Indefinite Integrals

  • F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x) if F′(x) = f(x). The set of all antiderivatives is the indefinite integral.
indefinite integral∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C (C = arbitrary constant of integration)
key formulas∫xⁿ dx = xn+1/(n+1) + C (n ≠ −1) · ∫sin(Ax+B) dx = −cos(Ax+B)/A + C · ∫cos kx dx = sin kx / k + C · ∫1/(ax+b) dx = (1/a)ln(ax+b) + C
  • Rules: constant multiple ∫k·f dx = k∫f dx; sum/difference ∫(f ± g) dx = ∫f dx ± ∫g dx; and substitution ∫f(g(x))·g′(x) dx = ∫f(u) du.
Why "+ C" — and only one C
Because d/dx(constant) = 0, every antiderivative can differ by a constant, so 2x integrates to x² + C (covering x²+1, x²−π, …). When integrating term by term, the separate constants C₁, C₂, C₃ merge into a single C — no need to carry several.
⚠ TrapThe power rule ∫xⁿ dx = xn+1/(n+1) fails for n = −1. Instead ∫x⁻¹ dx = ∫(1/x) dx = ln|x| + C.

Definite Integration

fundamental theorem∫ab f(x) dx = [g(x)]ab = g(b) − g(a), where g′(x) = f(x)
area & averageArea = ∫ab f(x) dx · ⟨y⟩ = (∫x₁x₂ f(x) dx) / (∫x₁x₂ dx)
  • A definite integral gives a number (no + C): the area between the curve and the x-axis over [a, b].
  • Average value = (integral of the quantity) ÷ (integral of dx over the same interval).
Area as a sum of thin strips
Divide the region into strips of width dx; each strip has area dA = f(x) dx. Summing all strips from a to b gives A = ∫ab f(x) dx — the geometric meaning of the definite integral (for f(x) ≥ 0).
