The whole chapter on one card

★ The one ideaA complex number is a point in a plane. Learn to jump between its three faces: Cartesian a+ib (add / subtract / multiply), modulus–argument r∠θ (multiply / divide / power — De Moivre), and the special number ω that generates the cube roots of unity. Almost every question is "switch to the face that makes it a one-liner."
iotai = √(−1), i² = −1. Powers cycle with period 4: i4n=1, i4n+1=i, i4n+2=−1, i4n+3=−i.
complex numberz = a + ib, Re(z)=a, Im(z)=b. z1=z2 ⇔ a1=a2 and b1=b2.
conjugatez̄ = a − ib (mirror of z in the real axis). z+z̄=2Re(z), z−z̄=2i·Im(z), z·z̄=|z|².
modulus|z| = √(a²+b²) ≥ 0 (distance of z from the origin). |z1z2|=|z1||z2|, |z1/z2|=|z1|/|z2|, |z̄|=|z|.
argument & polar / Euler formz = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r·eiθ, where r=|z|, θ=arg z. Principal arg ∈ (−π, π].
de moivre(cos θ + i sin θ)n = cos nθ + i sin nθ (n ∈ ℤ). arg(z1z2)=arg z1+arg z2, arg(zn)=n·arg z.
cube roots of unity1, ω, ω² with ω = (−1+i√3)/2. 1 + ω + ω² = 0 and ω³ = 1 (so 1/ω = ω²).
n-th roots of unityRoots of zn=1 are 1, α, α², …, αn−1 with α = ei2π/n. Their sum = 0; they are vertices of a regular n-gon on the unit circle.

⚠ Three killers(1) √a·√b = √(ab) fails when both a,b are negative — always pull out i first: √(−4)=2i, not √4·√(−1) games. (2) Inequalities like z1<z2 are meaningless for non-real complex numbers. L-1

Iota, Powers of i & Algebra of Complex Numbers

  • The number systems grow N ⊂ W ⊂ I ⊂ Q ⊂ R, each added to solve an equation the previous one couldn't. x²+1=0 needs a number whose square is −1 — no real number works, so we invent iota.
  • Definition: i = √(−1), so i² = −1. Every multiple of i is a new imaginary number; there are infinitely many.
  • A complex number z = a + ib combines a real part Re(z)=a and an imaginary part Im(z)=b. Every real number a = a+0i is complex, so R ⊂ C.
  • z with Re(z)=0 is purely imaginary; z with Im(z)=0 is purely real. The number 0 is both.
powers of iota — the 4-cyclei4n=1, i4n+1=i, i4n+2=−1, i4n+3=−i. To evaluate iN, divide N by 4 and read the remainder.

Algebraic operations

equalitya1+ib1 = a2+ib2 ⇔ a1=a2 and b1=b2 (equate real & imaginary parts separately).
add / subtract(x1+iy1) ± (x2+iy2) = (x1±x2) + i(y1±y2).
multiply(x1+iy1)(x2+iy2) = (x1x2 − y1y2) + i(x1y2 + x2y1) — just expand and use i²=−1.
Two notes the book flags
(1) i is neither positive nor negative, so a non-real complex number can't be called "greater" or "smaller" than another — inequalities don't hold for non-real complex numbers. (2) √(−1)·√(−1) ≠ √((−1)(−1)) = √1 = 1; in fact it equals i² = −1. The rule √a·√b = √(ab) breaks when both a and b are negative. Always convert √(−k) to i√k first.
💡 The block-of-four trickAny run of four consecutive powers of i sums to zero: ik+ik+1+ik+2+ik+3=0. So a long sum of i-powers collapses to just the leftover terms that don't fit into a complete block.

Book Example-5 · L-1
If z = x+iy, z1/3 = a−ib and x/a − y/b = k(a²−b²), find k.

Book Example-6 · L-1
Find real x, y for which (x⁴+2xi) − (3x²+yi) = (3−5i) + (1+2yi).
Collect: (x⁴−3x²) + i(2x−y) = 4 + i(−5+2y).

Imag: 2x−y = −5+2y ⇒ 3y = 2x+5. For x=2: y=3; for x=−2: y=1/3.
x = ±2, with y = 3 (for x=2) or y = 1/3 (for x=−2)
Book Example-7 · L-1
Solve ix² − 3x − 2i = 0.
Divide through by i (note 1/i = −i): x² + 3ix − 2 = 0.
x = [−3i ± √(−9 + 8)]/2 = [−3i ± √(−1)]/2 = (−3i ± i)/2.
x = −i or x = −2i
L-2

Division, Argand Plane & Modulus

division — rationalise the denominatorz1/z2 = (x1+iy1)/(x2+iy2) × (x2−iy2)/(x2−iy2) = [(x1x2+y1y2) + i(x2y1−x1y2)] / (x2²+y2²).
  • The move: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. That makes the denominator x2²+y2² (a real number = |z2|²), leaving a clean a+ib.
  • Multiplicative inverse: if z1z2=1 they are inverses of each other, so z⁻¹ = 1/z = z̄/|z|². (Reciprocate by putting the conjugate over the modulus-squared.)

Representation of a complex number

  • Cartesian / Argand plane: z = x+iy is the point P(x, y). The x-axis is the real axis, the y-axis the imaginary axis. Every complex number is one point; every point is one complex number.
modulus = distance from origin|z| = |z − (0+0i)| = √(x²+y²) — the distance of P(x, y) from O(0, 0) by the distance formula.
💡 Powers of (1+i)Because (1+i)²=2i and (1−i)²=−2i, the ratio (1−i)/(1+i) = −i and (1+i)/(1−i) = i. Any high power of these collapses via i's 4-cycle — never expand a binomial to the 40th power.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-2
Express (1+2i)² / (2+i)² in the form x+iy.
Squares: (1+2i)² = 1+4i−4 = −3+4i; (2+i)² = 4+4i−1 = 3+4i.
So the expression = (−3+4i)/(3+4i). Multiply by (3−4i)/(3−4i): numerator = (−3+4i)(3−4i) = −9+12i+12i+16 = 7+24i; denominator = 9+16 = 25.
= 7/25 + (24/25) i
Book Example-2 · L-2
Show that a real value of x satisfies (1−ix)/(1+ix) = a−ib only if a²+b² = 1.
By componendo–dividendo, ix = [1−(a−ib)]/[1+(a−ib)], so x = [1−a+ib]/[i(1+a) + b] after simplifying.
Rationalising, x = [2b + i(a²+b²−1)] / [b² + (1+a)²].
For x to be real the imaginary part must vanish: a²+b²−1 = 0.
x is real ⇔ a² + b² = 1
Book Example-3 · L-2
Find x, y satisfying [(1+i)x−2i]/(3+i) + [(2−3i)y+i]/(3−i) = i.
Clear denominators (multiply by (3+i)(3−i)=10): [(1+i)x−2i](3−i) + [(2−3i)y+i](3+i) = 10i.
Expand: (4+2i)x − 2 − 6i + (9−7i)y − 1 + 3i = 10i, i.e. (4x+9y−3) + i(2x−7y−3) = 10i.
Real: 4x+9y = 3. Imag: 2x−7y = 13 ⇒ 4x−14y = 26. Subtract: 23y = −23 ⇒ y = −1, then x = 3.
x = 3, y = −1
Book Example-4 · L-2
Find the least positive integer n which reduces [(i−1)/(i+1)]n to a real number.
Simplify the base: (i−1)/(i+1) × (1−i)/(1−i) = (i−1)(1−i)/2 = (2i)/2 = i.
So the expression is in, which is real (equal to ±1) exactly when n is even — smallest being n = 2 (i² = −1).
Least n = 2

Conjugate — Properties of Conjugate & Modulus

conjugateIf z = x+iy then z̄ = x−iy. Geometrically z̄ is the mirror image of z in the real (x) axis — same modulus, opposite argument.

Properties of the conjugate

#PropertyReads as
1(z̄) with a second bar = zconjugating twice returns z
2z + z̄ = 2Re(z)purely real
3z − z̄ = 2i·Im(z)purely imaginary

5,6conj(z1±z2) = z̄1 ± z̄2bar distributes over ±

Properties of the modulus

core|z| = √(x²+y²) ≥ 0; |z| = |−z| = |z̄| = |−z̄|; z·z̄ = |z|²; |z1z2| = |z1||z2|.
bounds & expansions−|z| ≤ Re(z) ≤ |z|, −|z| ≤ Im(z) ≤ |z|; |z1 ± z2|² = |z1|² + |z2|² ± 2Re(z1z̄2).
the "az+bz" identity|az1+bz2|² + |bz1−az2|² = (a²+b²)(|z1|² + |z2|²).
💡 Squaring/modulus kills phasesWhen a proof pits zk = something against a²+b², take the modulus of both sides and square — arguments disappear and you are left with a real equation in |z| alone. This is the whole trick behind Examples 4 and 5 below.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-3
If (x+iy)⁵ = p+iq, prove that (y+ix)⁵ = q+ip.
Conjugate the given: (x−iy)⁵ = p−iq.
Multiply by i⁵ = i: i⁵(x−iy)⁵ = [i(x−iy)]⁵ = (xi − i²y)⁵ = (y+ix)⁵, while the right side is i(p−iq) = q+ip.
(y+ix)⁵ = q + ip
Book Example-2 · L-3
If z² = (z̄)², find the location of z on the Argand plane.
Put z = x+iy: z² = x²−y²+2ixy and (z̄)² = x²−y²−2ixy.
z² = (z̄)² ⇒ 4ixy = 0 ⇒ x = 0 or y = 0.
z lies on the x-axis or the y-axis
Book Example-3 · L-3
Find all z satisfying Re(z²) = 0 and |z| = √3.
z = x+iy ⇒ Re(z²) = x²−y² = 0, and |z|² = x²+y² = 3.
Adding/subtracting: x² = y² = 3/2, so x = ±√(3/2), y = ±√(3/2).
z = ±√(3/2) ± √(3/2) i — four complex numbers
Book Example-4 · L-3
If (1+i)(1+2i)(1+3i)…(1+ni) = x+iy, show that 2·5·10·…·(1+n²) = x²+y².
Take the modulus of both sides and use |z1z2…| = |z1||z2|…: |1+i||1+2i|…|1+ni| = |x+iy|.
√(1+1)·√(1+4)·…·√(1+n²) = √(x²+y²).
Square both sides.
2·5·10·…·(1+n²) = x² + y²
Book Example-5 · L-3
If (√8 + i)50 = 349(a+ib), find a²+b².
Take the modulus of both sides and square: |√8+i|100 = 398(a²+b²).
|√8+i|² = 8+1 = 9, so |√8+i|100 = 950 = 3100. Thus 3100 = 398(a²+b²).
a² + b² = 9
Book Example-6 · L-3
If z + |z| = 1 + 7i, find |z|².
z = x+iy ⇒ x + √(x²+y²) + iy = 1 + 7i. Imag: y = 7. Real: x + √(x²+49) = 1.
√(x²+49) = 1−x ⇒ x²+49 = 1−2x+x² ⇒ 48 = −2x ⇒ x = −24.
|z|² = x²+y² = 576 + 49.
|z|² = 625
Book Example-7 · L-3
If α, β are distinct complex numbers with |β| = 1, find |(β−α)/(1−ᾱβ)|.
|β| = 1 ⇒ ββ̄ = 1 ⇒ β̄ = 1/β. Substitute inside the bar-friendly factor: |β−α| = |β||1 − ᾱ/β·… | reduces via |β|=1.
|(β−α)/(1−ᾱβ)| = (1/|β|)·|(1−αβ̄)/(1−ᾱβ)|, and |1−αβ̄| = |conj(1−ᾱβ)| = |1−ᾱβ|.
= 1
Book Example-8 · L-3
If |(z̄1−2z̄2)/(2−z1z̄2)| = 1 and |z2| ≠ 1, show |z1| = 2.
Square and use |w|² = w·w̄: |z̄1−2z̄2|² = |2−z1z̄2|².
Expanding both sides (the cross-term z1z̄2+z̄1z2 cancels): |z1|² + 4|z2|² = 4 + |z1|²|z2|².
Rearrange: |z1|²(1−|z2|²) + 4(|z2|²−1) = 0 ⇒ (|z2|²−1)(|z1|²−4) = 0.
Since |z2| ≠ 1, |z1|² = 4 ⇒ |z1| = 2

Triangle Inequality

  • On the Argand plane, z1+z2 is the far corner R of the parallelogram built on z1 and z2. The sum/difference are the two diagonals. Sides of a triangle then bound the diagonals.
triangle inequality (both signs together)| |z1| − |z2| | ≤ |z1 ± z2| ≤ |z1| + |z2|.
  • Upper bound |z1+z2| = |z1|+|z2| when z1, z2 point the same way (arguments equal).
  • Lower bound |z1+z2| = | |z1|−|z2| | when they point opposite ways.
💡 Max/min of |z| on a locusTo bound |z − c| or |z ± k/z| write z as a sum/difference and squeeze it between the two triangle-inequality bounds; then solve the resulting quadratic in |z|. Never chase x, y coordinates for these.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-4
If |z − 4/z| = 2, find the greatest value of |z|.
|z| = |(z − 4/z) + 4/z| ≤ |z − 4/z| + 4/|z| = 2 + 4/|z|.
So |z|² ≤ 2|z| + 4 ⇒ |z|² − 2|z| − 4 ≤ 0 ⇒ (|z|−1)² ≤ 5 ⇒ |z| − 1 ≤ √5.
Greatest |z| = √5 + 1
Book Example-2 · L-4
Find the greatest and least value of |z1+z2| if z1 = 24+7i and |z2| = 6.
|z1| = √(24²+7²) = √625 = 25.
Greatest: |z1+z2| ≤ |z1|+|z2| = 25+6 = 31. Least: |z1+z2| ≥ | |z1|−|z2| | = 25−6 = 19.
Greatest 31, least 19
Book Example-3 · L-4
For any complex z, find the minimum value of |z| + |z − 2i|.
|2i| = |z + (2i − z)| ≤ |z| + |2i − z|, i.e. 2 ≤ |z| + |z − 2i|.
Equality holds when z is on the segment joining 0 and 2i, so the bound is attained.
Minimum value = 2
L-5

Square Root of a Complex Number & the Argument

Square root of z = x + iy

formula√(x+iy) = ± ( √[(|z|+x)/2] + i·(sign of y)·√[(|z|−x)/2] ), where |z| = √(x²+y²).
  • Derivation: set √z = a+ib, so a²−b² = x and a²+b² = |z|; add and subtract to get a and b.
  • Sign rule: for y > 0 the pair (a, b) has the same signs (+,+) or (−,−); for y < 0 opposite signs, because 2ab = y.

Argument of a complex number

  • arg(z) = angle the join O→z makes with the positive real axis; positive anticlockwise, negative clockwise.
  • Principal argument is the unique value in (−π, π]. General argument = principal + 2nπ.
principal argument by quadrant (α = tan⁻¹|y/x|)Q1 (x>0,y>0): θ = α. Q2 (x<0,y>0): θ = π−α. Q3 (x<0,y<0): θ = α−π. Q4 (x>0,y<0): θ = −α.
properties of argumentarg(real>0)=0, arg(real<0)=π, arg(ki)=π/2 (k>0); arg(z1z2)=arg z1+arg z2; arg(zn)=n·arg z; arg(z1/z2)=arg z1−arg z2; arg(1/z)=−arg z.
⚠ Quadrant, not calculatortan⁻¹(y/x) alone loses the quadrant — for −1−i√3 a blind tan⁻¹ gives +π/3, but the true principal argument is −2π/3 (third quadrant). Always fix θ from the signs of x and y.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-5
Find modulus, argument, principal value & least positive argument of (a) 1+i√3 (b) −1+i√3 (c) 1−i√3 (d) −1−i√3.
Each has |z| = √(1+3) = 2. The reference angle is π/3 (since tan⁻¹(√3/1)=60°).
(a) Q1: principal π/3, least positive π/3. (b) Q2: principal 2π/3, least positive 2π/3.
(c) Q4: principal −π/3, least positive 2π−π/3 = 5π/3. (d) Q3: principal −2π/3, least positive 2π−2π/3 = 4π/3.

Book Example-2 · L-5
Find amp z and |z| if z = [ (3+4i)(1+i)(1+√3 i) / ((1−i)(4−3i)(2i)) ]².
Modulus of the bracket: (5·√2·2)/(√2·5·2) = 1, so |z| = 1² = 1.
Evaluating the bracket gives ½ − (√3/2)i (a point at −60°); squaring doubles the angle.
|z| = 1, amp(z) = −π/3
Book Example-3 · L-5
Find the square root of 3 + 4i.
Let √(3+4i) = a+ib: a²−b² = 3 and 2ab = 4.
a²+b² = √((a²−b²)² + 4a²b²) = √(9+16) = 5, so a² = 4, b² = 1, with a, b same sign (y>0).
√(3+4i) = ±(2 + i)
Book Example-4 · L-5
If z = 1 + cos(6π/5) + i sin(6π/5), find the modulus and amplitude of z.
Use 1+cosθ = 2cos²(θ/2), sinθ = 2sin(θ/2)cos(θ/2) with θ/2 = 3π/5: z = 2cos(3π/5)[cos(3π/5)+i sin(3π/5)].
cos(3π/5) < 0, so factor out its magnitude: 2cos(3π/5) = −2cos(2π/5). Absorbing the minus sign rotates by π: z = 2cos(2π/5)[cos(−2π/5)+i sin(−2π/5)].
|z| = 2cos(2π/5), amp(z) = −2π/5
Book Example-5 · L-5
Find the square root of 7 + 24i.
√(7+24i) = a+ib: a²−b² = 7, 2ab = 24. Then a²+b² = √(49+576) = 25.
So a² = 16, b² = 9 ⇒ a = ±4, b = ±3 (same sign, y>0).
√(7+24i) = ±(4 + 3i)
Book Example-6 · L-5
Solve z² − (3−2i)z = 5i − 5.
z² − (3−2i)z − (5i−5) = 0 ⇒ z = [(3−2i) ± √((3−2i)² + 4(5i−5))]/2.
(3−2i)² = 5−12i; 5−12i + 20i − 20 = −15 + 8i. And √(−15+8i) = ±(1+4i) (since 1²−4²=−15, 2·1·4=8).
z = [(3−2i) ± (1+4i)]/2 = (4+2i)/2 or (2−6i)/2.
z = 2 + i or z = 1 − 3i
L-6

Polar Form, Euler Form & De Moivre's Theorem

polar formz = x+iy = r(cos θ + i sin θ), where r = |z| = √(x²+y²), x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, θ = arg z.
  • To polar: compute r = |z|, find θ from the quadrant. Back to a+ib: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ.
euler formcos θ + i sin θ = eiθ, so z = |z|·eiθ = r·eiθ. (Intuition from the series of ex, sin x, cos x.)
de moivre's theoremn ∈ ℤ: (cos θ + i sin θ)n = cos nθ + i sin nθ. n ∈ ℚ (not integer): cos nθ + i sin nθ is one of the values of (cos θ + i sin θ)n.
💡 Why polar/Euler is worth the conversionMultiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments; a power multiplies the argument by n. So powers, roots and products of complex numbers become arithmetic on angles — De Moivre turns (cos θ+i sin θ)100 into a single cos 100θ + i sin 100θ.
Quick conversions the book runs through inline
1+i → polar: r = √2, θ = 45°, so 1+i = √2(cos 45° + i sin 45°) = √2 eiπ/4. 2(cos 30° + i sin 30°) → a+ib: x = 2cos30° = √3, y = 2sin30° = 1, so it equals √3 + i.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-6
Express in polar and exponential form: (i) (1+3i)/(1−2i) (ii) (i−1)/(cos π/3 + i sin π/3).
(i) Rationalise: (1+3i)(1+2i)/5 = (1+2i+3i−6)/5 = (−5+5i)/5 = −1+i. Then r = √2, arg = 3π/4 (Q2).

Cube Roots & nth Roots of Unity

Cube roots of unity

the three rootsx³ = 1 ⇒ (x−1)(x²+x+1) = 0 ⇒ x = 1, ω, ω² with ω = (−1+i√3)/2, ω² = (−1−i√3)/2.
the two relations to memorise1 + ω + ω² = 0 and ω³ = 1. Hence 1/ω = ω², 1/ω² = ω.
#Property of ω
1,21+ω+ω² = 0; ω³ = 1
3ω3n=1, ω3n+1=ω, ω3n+2=ω² (reduce the exponent mod 3)
41+ωn+ω2n = 3 if 3∣n, and = 0 otherwise
5Cube roots of −1 are −1, −ω, −ω²
6,7(a+b)(a+bω)(a+bω²)=a³+b³; (a−b)(a−bω)(a−bω²)=a³−b³
8,9(a+bω+cω²)(a+bω²+cω)=a²+b²+c²−ab−bc−ca; full product = a³+b³+c³−3abc
101, ω, ω² are vertices of an equilateral triangle on the unit circle

nth roots of unity

the n rootsxn = 1 ⇒ x = α, α², …, αn(=1) where α = ei2π/n. Find α, then just take its powers.
n-th roots of any zz1/n = |z|1/n · ei(2kπ+θ)/n, k = 0, 1, …, n−1, θ = arg z.
  • Sum of all n roots of unity = 0. Product = 1 (n odd) or −1 (n even).
  • They lie on the unit circle, are vertices of a regular n-gon, and their arguments form an A.P. with common difference 2π/n.
💡 Sum/product of roots via the polynomialIf z1,…,zn−1 are the non-trivial roots, then zn−1 = (z−1)(z−z1)…(z−zn−1). Dividing by (z−1) gives 1+z+…+zn−1 = ∏(z−zk) — substitute a value of z to evaluate products like ∏(2−zk) or ∏(1−zk) in one line.
