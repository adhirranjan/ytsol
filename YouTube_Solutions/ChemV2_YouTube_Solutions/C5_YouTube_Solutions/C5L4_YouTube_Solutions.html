1Which of the following hybridisations produces exactly two linear orbitals oriented at 180°? (a) sp (b) sp² (c) sp³ (d) sp³dsp hybridisationsp, sp² and sp³ hybrid orbitals — how many form and at what angle🟡 Similarasp
Concept Hybridisation is the mixing of atomic orbitals of nearly equal energy belonging to the same atom to give an equal number of new, identical orbitals. The single most useful bookkeeping rule is therefore orbitals in = orbitals out: mix 2 orbitals and you get 2 hybrids, mix 4 and you get 4. Those hybrids then arrange themselves as far apart as possible, because each carries an electron pair and pairs repel. Two objects placed as far apart as possible on a sphere sit at opposite poles — that is 180°, a straight line.
Formula The angle between hybrid orbitals follows the ICAD relation cos α = −(1/m), where m = number of p-orbitals mixed. For sp, m = 1 ⇒ cos α = −1 ⇒ α = 180°. For sp², m = 2 ⇒ cos α = −½ ⇒ α = 120°. For sp³, m = 3 ⇒ cos α = −⅓ ⇒ α = 109°28′. Also number of hybrid orbitals = number of atomic orbitals mixed.
Solution
1. The question fixes two things at once: the count must be 2 and the angle must be 180°.
2. Count first — only one s + one p = 2 orbitals mixed, so only sp can give exactly two hybrids.sp² mixes 3 orbitals (s + 2p) → 3 hybrids; sp³ mixes 4 → 4 hybrids; sp³d mixes 5 → 5 hybrids. All are eliminated on count alone.
3. Confirm with the angle formula: m = 1 for sp, so cos α = −1/1 = −1, giving α = 180° — collinear, exactly as demanded.
4. Real examples of sp: BeF₂, BeH₂, BeCl₂, CO₂, C₂H₂, HCN, CS₂, N₂O — every one of them is a linear molecule.

2What is the hybridisation of the central atom in a molecule having 4 bond pairs and 1 lone pair? (a) sp² (b) sp³ (c) sp³d (d) sp³d²Steric number → hybridisationFinding hybridisation and lone pairs from bond pairs — trick🟡 Similarcsp³d
Concept Hybridisation is decided by the steric number (the book calls it Q, the total number of hybrid orbitals needed), not by the shape and not by the number of atoms. Every σ-bond needs one hybrid orbital to house it, and every lone pair also needs one hybrid orbital to sit in. So a lone pair costs exactly as much as a bond as far as hybridisation is concerned — it changes the shape but not the hybridisation. Once Q is known, the hybridisation label is read straight off a fixed 2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d², 7→sp³d³ ladder.
Formula Q = steric number = (number of σ bond pairs) + (number of lone pairs) π-bonds are NOT counted — they are made from unhybridised p (or d) orbitals, which is why CO₂ with two double bonds is still sp. Ladder: 2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d², 7→sp³d³.
Solution
1. Read off the data: bond pairs = 4, lone pairs = 1.
2. Compute the steric number: Q = 4 + 1 = 5.The lone pair must be counted — it occupies a hybrid orbital just like a bond pair does.
3. Q = 5 needs five equivalent orbitals, so five atomic orbitals must be mixed: s + px + py + pz + dz² = sp³d.
4. Electron geometry is trigonal bipyramidal; with one lone pair occupying an equatorial position, the molecular shape is see-saw, bond angles ≈ 89° and 117°.The lone pair goes equatorial because in an axial site it would suffer three 90° repulsions against equatorial pairs, versus only two when equatorial — the lower-repulsion site wins.
5. Kill the distractors by their Q values: sp² needs Q = 3, sp³ needs Q = 4, sp³d² needs Q = 6. Only sp³d matches Q = 5. Textbook example: SF₄ (4 bp + 1 lp).

1Explain with example: s–s overlap of atomic orbitals. OR Explain the formation of the hydrogen molecule on the basis of VBT.VBT · s–s overlapFormation of the hydrogen molecule on the basis of valence bond theory🟢 Exact—
Concept Valence Bond Theory says a covalent bond is formed when two half-filled atomic orbitals on different atoms overlap, and the two electrons in them pair up with opposite spins. The overlap region is where electron density piles up between the nuclei; this shared density is what screens the two positive nuclei from each other and holds them together. The bond's strength is proportional to the extent of overlap, and its type is fixed by the direction of overlap: head-on along the internuclear axis gives a σ bond, sideways gives a π bond. s–s overlap is the simplest case of all, because an s orbital is spherical and therefore has no preferred direction — every s–s overlap is automatically head-on, so it always gives a σ bond.
Key relation H(1s¹) + H(1s¹) → H₂ (σ1s–1s), with the potential-energy curve reaching a minimum at r = 74 pm and bond energy ≈ 435 kJ mol⁻¹. The minimum exists because attraction (each nucleus to the other's electron) grows faster than repulsion as the atoms approach — until the nuclei get too close, when nucleus–nucleus repulsion takes over and the curve turns up again.
Solution
1. Starting atoms: each hydrogen atom has the configuration 1s¹ — one half-filled spherical s orbital, which is the requirement for VBT overlap.
2. Approach: as HA and HB come closer, two attractive forces (nucleus A – electron B, and nucleus B – electron A) and two repulsive forces (nucleus–nucleus, electron–electron) act simultaneously.At large separation the attractions dominate, so the potential energy falls.
3. Overlap: at r = 74 pm the two 1s orbitals overlap head-on; the electrons pair with opposite spins (Pauli), and the system reaches its energy minimum.
4. Result: the energy released, 435 kJ mol⁻¹, is the bond-dissociation energy of H₂; 74 pm is the H–H bond length. Because the overlap is axial and symmetric about the bond line, the bond formed is a sigma (σ) bond, specifically σs–s.
5. Other s–s examples and the contrast: the H–H bond in H₂ is the standard one; s–p overlap gives H–Cl (σs–p) and p–p axial overlap gives Cl–Cl (σp–p). Overlap strength runs s–s < s–p < p–p because directional p orbitals concentrate electron density along the axis.

⚡ JEE Trick Answer every "explain the bond in XY" VBT question with the same four-beat template: write the configurations → identify the half-filled orbitals → name the overlap type (s–s / s–p / p–p) → name the bond (σ or π) and quote length + energy. Trap: writing that hydrogen atoms "share electrons to complete the octet" — H needs a duplet (2 electrons), not an octet, and VBT wants the language of orbital overlap, not of Lewis sharing.
Key point: two half-filled 1s orbitals of H atoms overlap head-on at 74 pm, the electrons pair with opposite spins, and a σ(s–s) bond is formed with the release of 435 kJ mol⁻¹.
2Explain in brief the need for the concept of hybridisation. Give reasons for the need of hybridisation.Why hybridisation is neededWhy plain atomic orbitals fail and hybrid orbitals are needed (VBT)🟡 Similar—
Concept Plain VBT with unmixed atomic orbitals makes two predictions that experiment flatly contradicts. First, s, p and d orbitals have different energies and different shapes, so the bonds they form ought to be inequivalent — yet all four C–H bonds in CH₄ are measured to be identical in length and strength. Second, p orbitals lie at 90° to one another, so CH₄ ought to show 90° bond angles — yet the measured angle is 109°28′. Hybridisation is the repair: mix the orbitals first, then bond, and both problems disappear at once.
Definition Hybridisation = mixing of atomic orbitals of the same atom having nearly equal energy to give an equal number of new orbitals of equal energy and identical shape. The new orbitals are called hybrid orbitals; they are dumb-bell shaped with one large lobe and one small lobe, which is exactly what makes them better at overlapping.
Solution
1. To explain equivalent bonds. Carbon's valence orbitals are 2s and 2p — different in energy and shape. Without mixing, CH₄ would have three "p-type" bonds and one "s-type" bond. Hybridisation makes all four sp³ orbitals identical, so all four C–H bonds are identical.
2. To explain the observed geometry and bond angles. Pure p orbitals are mutually perpendicular (90°), but CH₄ is measured at 109°28′, NH₃ at 107° and H₂O at 104.5°. Only a tetrahedral set of sp³ hybrids reproduces these numbers.The hybrid angle follows cos α = −(1/m): sp → 180°, sp² → 120°, sp³ → 109°28′ — all confirmed experimentally.
3. To explain the observed valency. Ground-state carbon is 1s²2s²2p² with only two unpaired electrons, predicting a divalent carbon (CH₂). Promotion of one 2s electron to the empty 2p, followed by hybridisation, gives four unpaired electrons and the observed tetravalency.
4. To give stronger bonds. A hybrid orbital is dumb-bell shaped with one large lobe pointing at the partner atom, so it overlaps far more effectively than a spherical s or a symmetric p orbital. Greater overlap ⇒ shorter bond length and higher bond energy.This is also why bond strength rises with s-character: sp (50% s) > sp² (33.33% s) > sp³ (25% s).
5. To give definite spatial orientation. Hybrid orbitals point in fixed, well-defined directions, which is precisely what allows a molecule to have a predictable geometry at all.

3Explain the geometry of the methane molecule on the basis of sp³-hybridisation.sp³ · CH₄ geometryMethane — sp³ hybridisation and the tetrahedral shape of CH₄🟢 Exact—
Concept Methane is the standard worked example of sp³ hybridisation because it is the "clean" case: four bond pairs and zero lone pairs, so the molecular shape is identical to the electron geometry and the ideal angle is not distorted at all. The explanation has three compulsory stages — excitation (to get four unpaired electrons), hybridisation (to make them equivalent), and overlap (to form the bonds).
Key relation Steric number Q = bond pairs + lone pairs = 4 + 0 = 4 ⇒ sp³; angle from cos α = −(1/m) = −1/3 ⇒ α = 109°28′. m = 3 because three p orbitals are mixed. The result 109°28′ (= 109.5°) is the tetrahedral angle, and it matches the measured H–C–H angle exactly.
Solution
1. Ground state of carbon: 1s² 2s² 2px¹ 2py¹ 2pz⁰ — only two unpaired electrons, which would wrongly predict CH₂.
2. Excited state: one 2s electron is promoted to the empty 2pz, giving 1s² 2s¹ 2px¹ 2py¹ 2pz¹ — now four unpaired electrons, i.e. tetravalent carbon.The promotion energy is small (2s and 2p are close in energy) and is more than repaid by the energy released in forming two extra C–H bonds.
3. Hybridisation: the one 2s and the three 2p orbitals mix to give four identical sp³ hybrid orbitals, each with 25% s and 75% p character, each carrying one electron.
4. Compute the geometry from the steric number, not from memory: bond pairs = 4, lone pairs = 0 ⇒ Q = 4 ⇒ four electron domains ⇒ they point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron. With no lone pair to squeeze them, the angle takes its ideal value: cos α = −1/3 ⇒ α = 109°28′.
5. Overlap: each sp³ hybrid overlaps head-on with the half-filled 1s orbital of a hydrogen atom, giving four identical σ(sp³–s) bonds of length 109 pm. Hence CH₄ is a regular tetrahedron, non-polar overall because the four equal bond dipoles cancel.

4What is sp-hybridisation?sp (collinear) hybridisationsp, sp² and sp³ hybridisation explained from scratch🟡 Similar—
Concept sp-hybridisation — also called collinear or diagonal hybridisation — is the mixing of one s and one p orbital of the same atom to give two identical sp hybrid orbitals. Because only one p orbital is used up, two p orbitals are left untouched, and those left-over p orbitals are what go on to make π bonds. That is why every sp-hybridised centre carries either two double bonds or one triple bond.
Definition & relation 1 s orbital + 1 p orbital → 2 sp hybrid orbitals at 180°; s-character = 50%, p-character = 50%; angle from cos α = −(1/m) = −1/1 = −1 ⇒ α = 180°. Two hybrids on a sphere repel until they are exactly opposite, so the arrangement is linear. The 50% s-character makes sp the most electronegative and shortest-bonded of the three carbon hybrids.
Solution
1. Definition: the mixing of one s and one p orbital of nearly equal energy to give two new orbitals of equal energy and identical shape, called sp hybrid orbitals.
2. Geometry from the steric number: an sp centre has Q = σ + lp = 2 + 0 = 2 ⇒ two electron domains ⇒ linear, bond angle 180° (cos α = −1 ⇒ α = 180°).
3. Leftovers: two unhybridised p orbitals remain, perpendicular to the molecular axis and to each other; they form the π bonds.In C₂H₂ they give the two π bonds of the C≡C triple bond; in CO₂ they give one π to each oxygen.
4. Standard example — BeF₂ (the book's own): Be ground state 1s²2s² has no unpaired electron; on excitation it becomes 1s²2s¹2p¹, the 2s and 2p mix to give two sp hybrids at 180°, and each overlaps the half-filled 2p orbital of a fluorine ⇒ F–Be–F is linear.

3Which of the following statements is true about hybridisation? (a) The hybridised orbitals have different energies for each orbital (b) The number of hybrid orbitals is equal to the number of atomic orbitals that are hybridised (c) Hybrid orbitals form multiple bonds (d) The orbital with large energy difference undergo hybridisationRules of hybridisationCompanion MCQ — spotting the wrong statement about hybridisation🟡 SimilarbNumber of hybrid orbitals = number of atomic orbitals hybridised
Concept Hybridisation is a linear combination of atomic orbitals, and the mathematics of linear combinations forces one unbreakable bookkeeping rule: n orbitals in must give n orbitals out. Every other property in the options is a genuine postulate of the theory but has been deliberately stated backwards. The three false statements each attack a different pillar — degeneracy of the products, the σ-only role of hybrids, and the comparable-energy requirement of the reactants — so working through them is effectively a revision of the whole postulate list.
Statement Pauling's postulates: n orbitals in → n hybrids out; hybrids are degenerate (identical energy and shape); only orbitals of comparable energy mix; hybrid orbitals form σ bonds and hold lone pairs only. π bonds are made exclusively by unhybridised p (or d) orbitals overlapping sideways — this is why π bonds never appear in the steric number.
Solution
1. (a) "different energies for each orbital" — FALSE. The whole purpose of hybridisation is to produce a set of equivalent, degenerate orbitals. The four sp³ hybrids of carbon are identical, which is why all four C−H bonds in CH₄ have the same length and strength.
2. (b) "number of hybrid orbitals = number of atomic orbitals hybridised" — TRUE. s + p → 2 sp; s + 2p → 3 sp²; s + 3p → 4 sp³; s + 3p + d → 5 sp³d; s + 3p + 2d → 6 sp³d². Orbital count is conserved exactly.This is not chemistry-specific — it follows from the fact that a linear combination of n independent functions spans an n-dimensional space.
3. (c) "hybrid orbitals form multiple bonds" — FALSE. Hybrid orbitals point along the internuclear axes and can only overlap head-on ⇒ σ bonds. The extra bonds of a double or triple bond are π bonds from unhybridised p orbitals (e.g. in C₂H₂ the two sp hybrids give the σ framework; the two π bonds come from the untouched py and pz).
4. (d) "orbital with large energy difference undergo hybridisation" — FALSE. The requirement is exactly the opposite: only orbitals of nearly equal (comparable) energy mix effectively. This is why 2s mixes with 2p but not with 3p, and why hybridisation involving d orbitals is restricted to periods 3 and beyond where nd and (n)s, (n)p lie close.
5. Only statement (b) survives ⇒ answer (b).

Concept The steric number rule Q = σ + lp converts each statement into arithmetic, and three of the four survive immediately. The interesting one is (b), and it fails on the word "must". Nitrogen has an odd number of valence electrons (5), so it can end up with a single unpaired electron that is neither a bond nor a lone pair — and a lone electron still occupies an electron domain. A nitrogen with two σ bonds and no lone pair may therefore carry an odd electron, pushing Q to 3 and the hybridisation to sp². The classic counter-example is the NO₂ radical, bent at about 134° and sp², whereas NO₂⁺ with the same two σ bonds and no odd electron is linear and sp.
Key relation Q = σ bonds + lone pairs + single unpaired electrons. A half-filled orbital still repels its neighbours, so it counts as a domain — though it repels less strongly than a full lone pair, which is why NO₂'s angle (134°) sits between NO₂⁺ (180°) and NO₂⁻ (115°).
Solution
1. (a) 3 σ + 1 lp ⇒ Q = 4 ⇒ sp³. TRUE. This is NH₃, NF₃, NCl₃, PH₃-type nitrogen — pyramidal, ≈ 107°.
2. (b) 2 σ + 0 lp ⇒ "must be sp". FALSE. It is sp only if nothing else occupies a domain. In the NO₂ radical nitrogen has two σ bonds, no lone pair, but one odd electron ⇒ Q = 3 ⇒ sp², bent (≈ 134°). The word "must" makes the statement unsafe.Compare the pair directly: NO₂⁺ (2 σ, nothing else) is sp and linear at 180°; NO₂• (2 σ + 1 odd electron) is sp² and bent. Same σ count, different hybridisation.
3. (c) 3 σ + 0 lp ⇒ Q = 3 ⇒ sp². TRUE. Realised in NO₃⁻ and in the nitrogen of HNO₃ — planar, 120°, with the left-over p orbital carrying the delocalised π system.
4. (d) 4 σ + 0 lp ⇒ Q = 4 ⇒ sp³. TRUE. This is NH₄⁺ and any quaternary ammonium R₄N⁺ — tetrahedral, 109°28′. Four σ bonds exhaust nitrogen's capacity, so no ambiguity is possible here.

Concept Each option welds two claims together with "as well as", so an option is true only if both halves hold. That structure is the whole game: (b) and (d) each pair a correct trend with a second trend that runs in a different order, and one wrong half sinks the option. Option (a) is a pure symmetry question — the bond type depends only on how many nodal planes contain the chosen axis. 10(One or more than one option may be correct) Which statements correctly describe the need for hybridisation as explained in the theory? (a) Hybridisation gives orbitals of equal energy and definite spatial orientation (b) Hybridisation increases repulsive forces and therefore decreases bond strength (c) Hybridisation allows the formation of stronger σ-bonds due to directional character (d) Hybridisation helps an atom achieve the specific geometry required for bond formationWhy hybridiseHybridisation part 1 — why the concept is needed at all🟡 Similara, c, dall except (b)
Concept Hybridisation was invented to repair a specific failure of simple valence-bond theory: pure atomic orbitals predict the wrong angles and unequal bonds. Carbon's 2s and three 2p orbitals would give one weak bond from a spherical s orbital and three bonds at 90° from mutually perpendicular p orbitals, whereas methane demonstrably has four identical bonds at 109°28′. Mixing the orbitals fixes all three defects at once — it makes the bonding orbitals degenerate, gives them definite directions, and reshapes each one into a large lobe pointing at its partner, which increases overlap and therefore bond strength. Since hybrid orbitals arrange themselves to be as far apart as possible, hybridisation minimises repulsion rather than increasing it, which is exactly where statement (b) goes wrong.
Statement Purpose of hybridisation: equal energy + definite direction + better overlap + correct geometry. Overlap is proportional to the size of the lobe pointing along the bond; a hybrid orbital is strongly lopsided (one big lobe, one small), so its overlap — and hence bond strength — beats that of a pure s or pure p orbital.
Solution
1. (a) TRUE. All hybrids of a set are degenerate and identical in shape, and each points in a fixed direction. This is why the four C−H bonds of CH₄ are indistinguishable — an observation pure s/p orbitals cannot explain.
2. (b) FALSE. Hybrid orbitals adopt the arrangement of maximum mutual separation (180°, 120°, 109°28′), which minimises electron-pair repulsion; and because overlap improves, bond strength increases. The statement reverses both halves of the truth.If hybridisation really weakened bonds, molecules would simply not hybridise — the process is adopted precisely because the energy released by stronger, better-directed bonds exceeds the small promotion/mixing cost.
3. (c) TRUE. A hybrid orbital is highly directional, with one enlarged lobe aimed at the partner atom. Greater head-on overlap ⇒ stronger σ bonds. This is also why bond strength rises with s-character: sp (50%) > sp² (33.3%) > sp³ (25%).
4. (d) TRUE. The number of hybrid orbitals fixes their spatial arrangement and hence the molecular geometry — 2 ⇒ linear, 3 ⇒ trigonal planar, 4 ⇒ tetrahedral, 5 ⇒ trigonal bipyramidal, 6 ⇒ octahedral. Without hybridisation the predicted angle for CH₄ would be 90°, not the observed 109°28′.
