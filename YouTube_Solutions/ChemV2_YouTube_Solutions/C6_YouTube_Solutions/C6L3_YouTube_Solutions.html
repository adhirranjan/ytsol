1The mathematical form of the first law of thermodynamics when heat (q) is supplied and W is work done by the system is: (Level-1)

Concept The lecture's boxed statement of the first law is ΔU=q+w, built on the convention that lower-case w is positive when work is done ON the system (compression) and negative when the system does work on its surroundings (expansion). This question deliberately hands you a capital W defined the opposite way — as the work done BY the system, which is the convention used in most physics/engineering textbooks. So the question isn't really testing the law itself, it is testing whether you can translate between the two bookkeeping conventions without getting the sign backwards.
Key relation w (on system) = -W (by system). Whatever energy the system spends pushing outward (W, by-system convention) is exactly the energy the surroundings are NOT doing on it, so the two bookkeeping numbers are equal in size and opposite in sign. Substituting into the lecture's own formula ΔU = q + w gives ΔU = q - W.
Solution
1. Start from the lecture's own boxed first-law statement: ΔU = q + w, with w positive when work is done ON the system.This is the anchor equation printed at the top of this very lecture page — every sign-convention question in this book is answered by returning to it.
2. The question defines W as work done BY the system — the reverse of w. So w = -W.
3. Substitute: ΔU = q + (-W) = q - W.
4. Test it physically on expansion: a gas that expands does positive work BY itself (W > 0), and expansion should always cool/drain the system unless heat is pumped in — ΔU = q - W correctly subtracts that drained energy.
5. Test it on compression: the surroundings do work ON the gas, so W (by-system) is negative; ΔU = q - W = q - (negative) = q + (positive), correctly adding energy — consistent with intuition.
6. Eliminate the distractors: (a) ΔU=q+W silently reuses the on-system sign for a by-system W — the classic trap. (c) and (d) additionally flip the sign of q, which no version of the first law does.
7. Hence the correct statement, with W explicitly defined as work done BY the system, is ΔU = q - W — option (b).

Concept PV work is mechanical work traced back to its definition, force × displacement, applied to a piston. A gas held in a cylinder by a frictionless, weightless piston of cross-sectional area A pushes the piston outward by a small distance dℓ whenever it expands; multiplying the force the gas exerts by that displacement, and recognising that force = pressure × area, converts the mechanical definition into a pressure-volume statement.
Formula dw = F·dℓ = (P·A)·dℓ = P·(A·dℓ) = P dV, integrated to w = P(V2-V1) = PΔV, and finally given the sign that marks energy leaving the system: W = -PexΔV. A·dℓ is exactly the extra sliver of volume the gas has swept out, so P·A·dℓ and P·dV are the same quantity written two ways.
Solution
1. Set up the picture: a gas of volume V and pressure P (slightly greater than the external pressure) is enclosed by a piston of area A; the external pressure pushing back is Pex.
2. As the gas expands, the piston moves outward through a small displacement dℓ, doing work against the external pressure: dw = force × displacement = (Pex·A)·dℓ.
3. Recognise that A·dℓ is exactly the infinitesimal increase in volume, dV, so dw = Pex·dV.
4. Integrate between the initial and final volumes to get the total work for a finite change: w = ∫V1V2 Pex dV = Pex(V2-V1) = PexΔV (valid because Pex is held constant throughout).
5. Apply the sign convention: when the gas expands it does work ON the surroundings, so this work must count as energy LEAVING the system, giving it a negative sign in ΔU = q + w: W = -PexΔV.
6. Check the limiting case: if the gas is compressed, ΔV is negative, so W comes out positive — correctly signalling that the surroundings are adding energy to the system.

2Explain sign convention of work during expansion and compression. OR Explain +W and -W.Sign convention for work — why expansion is negative, compression is positiveFirst Law of Thermodynamics : sign convention for WORK🟡 Similar—
Concept Sign convention is just a bookkeeping choice for tracking whether energy is entering or leaving the system, and this lecture fixes it from the system's point of view: anything that ADDS energy to the system is positive, anything that REMOVES energy is negative. Work fits into that scheme by asking who is doing the pushing — if the surroundings push in on the system (compression), energy is being deposited into the system; if the system pushes out on the surroundings (expansion), energy is leaving.
Key relation Work done ON the system = W positive; Work done BY the system = W negative. This is the mirror image of the heat convention (q positive when absorbed, negative when evolved) — both quantities are signed by the same rule: energy gained by the system is positive.
Solution
1. Picture compression: the surroundings push a piston inward, squeezing the gas into a smaller volume. The surroundings are doing the work, and that work ends up as extra internal energy of the gas — the system GAINS energy, so W is taken as positive.
2. Picture expansion: the gas itself pushes the piston outward. Whatever energy the gas spends shoving the piston leaves the system as work delivered to the surroundings — the system LOSES energy, so W is taken as negative.
3. Connect this to the algebra of ΔV: expansion means V2 > V1, so ΔV > 0; with W = -PexΔV, a positive ΔV automatically forces W negative — the formula's minus sign is not arbitrary, it encodes exactly this physical rule.
4. Similarly, compression means ΔV < 0, so W = -PexΔV comes out positive automatically — the same single formula correctly signs both cases without needing to remember two separate rules.
5. Apply it inside the first law: ΔU = q + w. A compression (+w) and heat absorption (+q) both raise ΔU; an expansion (-w) and heat release (-q) both lower it — the convention keeps every term consistently "gain = positive."

 expansion     system pushes OUT        system LOSES energy     W = -ve

⚡ JEE Trick Reduce the whole convention to one sentence: "whoever loses energy gets the minus sign." Apply it uniformly to both q and w and you never need to memorise the rule as a separate fact — you can always re-derive it from "does the system gain or lose energy here?"
