1A block hangs from a string; the string applies force T on the block. Draw its reaction force.Newton's third law / action–reactionNewton's 3rd law — action–reaction pairs🟡 Similar—
Concept This is a pure Newton's third law question. The string pulls the block up with force T. The third law says every force is one half of a pair: if body A pushes/pulls body B, then B pushes/pulls A with a force of the same magnitude, opposite direction, of the same type, along the same line of action, and at the same instant. The single most important rule: the two members of a pair never act on the same body. So the reaction to "string on block" must be a force that acts on the string, not on the block.
Formula F⃗(A on B) = − F⃗(B on A) — equal magnitudes in newtons (N), opposite directions. The minus sign is the whole content of the law: same size, reversed direction. Here A = string, B = block, and the magnitude is T (in N).
Solution
1. Name the given (action) force precisely by its two bodies: it is the force of the string on the block, magnitude T, directed vertically upward.Third-law bookkeeping only works if you always say "force of X on Y" — naming it just "T" is what makes students pick the wrong partner.
2. Swap the two bodies to build the reaction: force of the block on the string.Reversing the roles of the two interacting bodies is the mechanical recipe for finding a reaction partner.
3. Give it the same magnitude and the opposite direction: magnitude T (N), directed vertically downward, applied at the point where the string is tied to the block, along the same vertical line.
4. Check it is a genuine pair: two different bodies (string, block) √, same type of force (contact/tension) √, same line √, equal magnitude T √, opposite sense √.
5. Reject the classic trap: the block's weight mg (in N) is not the reaction to T.mg also acts on the block (same body as T), so it cannot be T's partner. mg is the force of the Earth on the block; its reaction is the force of the block on the Earth, mg upward, applied at the Earth's centre.

⚡ JEE Trick To find any reaction force, just read the action out loud as "force of A on B" and swap A and B. The answer is always the same number with the arrow flipped, drawn on the other body. Trap: if your "action–reaction pair" ever appears on the same free-body diagram, it is wrong — that is a balanced pair (like T and mg when the block is in equilibrium), not a third-law pair. Balanced forces can be unequal the moment the block accelerates; third-law pairs are equal always, even in free fall.
Key point: the reaction is the force of the block on the string — magnitude T, directed vertically downward, acting at the block–string junction on the string. (It is not the weight mg, which acts on the block itself.)
2A player takes 0.1 s catching a ball of mass 150 g moving at 20 m/s. Find the force imparted on the hands.Impulse–momentum / forcePlayer catches 150 g ball at 20 m/s in 0.1 s🟢 Exact30 N
Concept Catching a ball is a pure impulse–momentum problem, not an "F = ma" problem — you are never told an acceleration. The hands bring the ball from 20 m/s to rest, so the ball loses all its momentum. Newton's second law in its original form says force equals the rate of change of momentum; the same momentum change spread over a longer time needs a smaller force — exactly why a fielder pulls his hands back. The traps are forgetting the final velocity is zero and leaving the mass in grams.
Formula Favg = Δp ÷ Δt = m(vf − vi) ÷ t (newton). This is F = dp/dt averaged over the catch; the force opposes the motion, so we take magnitudes. Data: m = 150 g = 0.15 kg, vi = 20 m/s, vf = 0, t = 0.1 s.
Solution
1. Convert units: m = 150 g = 0.15 kg.SI force needs kilograms — leaving grams gives 30000, the classic slip.
2. Initial momentum: pi = m vi = 0.15 × 20 = 3 kg·m/s.
3. Final momentum: the ball is caught, so vf = 0 ⇒ pf = 0.
4. Change of momentum: Δp = pf − pi = 0 − 3 = −3 kg·m/s (magnitude 3, directed against the ball's motion).
5. Average force: F = |Δp| ÷ t = 3 ÷ 0.1 = 30 N.
6. By Newton's third law the ball pushes back on the hands with the same 30 N — this is the force "imparted on the hands".
 m = 0.15 kg   v: 20 -> 0 m/s   t = 0.1 s
 p_i = 0.15 x 20 = 3 kg m/s
 p_f = 0
 |dp| = 3 kg m/s
 F = |dp| / t = 3 / 0.1 = 30 N      <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Read the force as Δp ÷ Δt, never as "ma" — no acceleration is given. One line: F = mv/t = (0.15)(20)/(0.1) = 30 N. Trap: forgetting to divide by t (leaving 3 N), or keeping m = 150 (giving 30000 N). Physics point: a bigger catch time t means a smaller force — that is the whole idea behind "giving" with the ball.
Answer: 30 N.
3Bullets of 0.03 kg hit a plate at 200/s with velocity 50 m/s and reflect back at 30 m/s. Find the average force on the plate.Average force from momentum changeBullets 0.03 kg at 200/s, 50 m/s, rebound 30 m/s — average force🟢 Exact480 N
Concept A steady stream of bullets strikes a plate, so the average force equals the total momentum delivered per second. Each bullet arrives with momentum into the plate and rebounds, so its velocity reverses direction. When a velocity reverses, the two speeds add in the momentum change — this is the single biggest trap: students write (50 − 30) instead of (50 + 30).
Formula Per bullet: |Δp| = m(vhit + vback) (kg·m/s). Average force from n bullets per second: F = n × |Δp| (N). Force = momentum change per unit time; n bullets each deliver |Δp|, so multiply. Data: m = 0.03 kg, vhit = 50 m/s, vback = 30 m/s, n = 200 s⁻¹.
Solution
1. Take "into the plate" as positive. Incoming momentum of one bullet: m vhit = 0.03 × 50 = +1.5 kg·m/s.
2. After rebound the bullet moves out, so its momentum is negative: −m vback = −0.03 × 30 = −0.9 kg·m/s.
3. Change of momentum of one bullet: Δp = (−0.9) − (+1.5) = −2.4 kg·m/s (magnitude 2.4).The reversal is why the numbers add: 0.03(50 + 30) = 2.4.
4. This is the impulse the plate gives each bullet; by Newton's third law each bullet gives the plate 2.4 kg·m/s.
5. With n = 200 bullets per second: F = 200 × 2.4 = 480 N.

 rate n = 200 /s
 F = n |dp| = 200 x 2.4 = 480 N        <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For a rebounding stream, add the two speeds; for bullets that embed (stop dead) you would use only vhit. Trap: writing 0.03(50 − 30) = 0.6 ⇒ 120 N — that would be for a bullet whose speed merely drops without reversing. The reversal here nearly doubles the answer.

4Block of mass m on a smooth 53° incline, F = mg applied horizontally. Find net force, acceleration and normal reaction.Horizontal force on inclineHorizontal force on a block on an inclined plane🟡 Similarmg/5, g, 7mg/5 ⚠
Concept A block rests on a smooth (frictionless) incline of 53°, and a horizontal force F = mg is applied pushing it toward the uphill side. Because the force is horizontal but the surface is tilted, F must be resolved into components along and perpendicular to the incline — and, crucially, its perpendicular component presses the block into the surface, so it increases the normal reaction above the usual mg cosθ. Choosing incline-aligned axes (rather than horizontal/vertical) makes the block's acceleration lie entirely along one axis, which is what keeps the algebra short.
Formula Along the incline (taking up the slope as positive): Fnet = F cosθ − mg sinθ (N). Perpendicular to the incline (no motion, so net = 0): N − mg cosθ − F sinθ = 0 (N). Then a = Fnet ÷ m (m s⁻²). The block cannot leave or sink into the surface, so its acceleration perpendicular to the incline is zero — that is what makes the perpendicular equation an equilibrium equation. Data: θ = 53°, so sin 53° = 0.8 and cos 53° = 0.6; F = mg (N); take g = 10 m s⁻². Smooth ⇒ friction = 0.
Solution
1. List the three forces on the block: weight mg (N) vertically down; normal N (N) perpendicular to and away from the incline surface; applied F = mg (N) horizontal, pointing toward the uphill side.Smooth surface ⇒ no friction force, so these three are all of them.
2. Resolve the weight onto incline axes: along the slope = mg sinθ = mg(0.8) = 0.8 mg (N), directed down the slope; perpendicular = mg cosθ = mg(0.6) = 0.6 mg (N), pressing into the surface.
3. Resolve the horizontal force F onto the same axes: along the slope = F cosθ = mg(0.6) = 0.6 mg (N), directed up the slope; perpendicular = F sinθ = mg(0.8) = 0.8 mg (N), pressing into the surface.A horizontal push toward the hill has an uphill component (F cosθ) and also squeezes the block against the incline (F sinθ) — note the sin and cos swap over compared with the weight, because F is perpendicular to mg.
4. (i) Net force along the incline = F cosθ − mg sinθ = 0.6 mg − 0.8 mg = −0.2 mg N.The minus sign means it points down the slope: the uphill push 0.6 mg loses to gravity's 0.8 mg pull down the slope. So Fnet = 0.2 mg N, directed down the incline.
5. (ii) Acceleration: a = Fnet ÷ m = (0.2 mg) ÷ m = 0.2 g = 0.2 × 10 = 2 m s⁻², directed down the incline.The mass cancels — the answer 0.2g is independent of m, which is a good sign the algebra is right.
6. (iii) Normal reaction, from the perpendicular equation N = mg cosθ + F sinθ = 0.6 mg + 0.8 mg = 1.4 mg N.Both the weight and the applied force push the block into the surface, so they add; N here is larger than the plain mg cosθ = 0.6 mg you would get with no F.
7. Dimensional check: 0.2 mg and 1.4 mg are (kg)(m s⁻²) = N ✓; a = 0.2g is m s⁻² ✓.

⚡ JEE Trick On an incline, gravity gets (sin, cos) but a horizontal force gets (cos, sin) — the two swap, because F is at 90° to mg. Memorise 53° as the "0.8–0.6" angle (3–4–5 triangle: sin53 = 0.8, cos53 = 0.6, and sin37 = 0.6, cos37 = 0.8) — ICAD/JEE use 37°/53° precisely so the arithmetic stays exact. Trap: writing N = mg cosθ out of habit — any force with a component into the surface changes N, and here it climbs to 1.4 mg. Second trap: F = mg does not mean the block is in equilibrium; since cos53 < sin53, the block still slides down. (Equilibrium would need F = mg tan53 = 1.33 mg.)
Answer: (i) Fnet = 0.2 mg N directed down the incline; (ii) a = 0.2 g = 2 m s⁻² down the incline (g = 10 m s⁻²); (iii) N = 1.4 mg N.
5A particle of mass 2 kg at rest; a force changing with time (F–t graph) acts on it. Find the velocity after 10 s.Area under F–t graph → velocityParticle of mass 2 kg at rest, force changes with time🟢 Exact50 m s⁻¹
Concept When a force varies with time, you cannot use F = ma with a single number. The right tool is the impulse–momentum theorem: the area under the F–t graph equals the change in momentum. Since the particle starts from rest, that area divided by the mass gives the final velocity directly. The trap is to read a force value off the graph and plug it in; only the enclosed area matters.
Formula ∫F dt = area under F–t = Δp = m(v − 0) ⇒ v = (area) ÷ m (m/s). Impulse is the time-integral of force; for straight-line graphs it is just the geometric area, no calculus needed. Data: m = 2 kg, starts at rest, read the area up to t = 10 s.
Solution
1. State the theorem: impulse = area under the F–t curve = Δp.
2. Read the area from the graph geometrically. The shaded region up to t = 10 s is a triangle of base 10 s and peak height 20 N.Break any F–t graph into triangles/rectangles and add — this is the whole method.
3. Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 10 × 20 = 100 N·s.
4. Impulse–momentum: Δp = 100 = m v = 2 × v (starts from rest, so pi = 0).
5. Solve: v = 100 ÷ 2 = 50 m/s.
6. Units check: N·s ÷ kg = (kg·m/s²·s)/kg = m/s ✓.
 impulse = area under F-t = change in momentum

 dp = m v  ->  100 = 2 x v
 v = 100 / 2 = 50 m/s              <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Area under F–t = impulse = Δp; area under a–t = Δv. Don't integrate — read triangles and rectangles straight off the axes. Trap: using the slope of the F–t line, or plugging the peak force 20 N into F = ma (would give a = 10 and a wrong velocity). Only the enclosed area counts.

1We never use Newton's first law directly for studying motion. Why should it be studied?Significance of Newton's first lawNewton's First Law of Motion — Class 11🟡 Similar—
Concept The question is really asking: if we always compute with F⃗ = ma⃗, what work is the first law still doing? The answer is that the first law is not a special case of the second law — it is logically prior to it. It does two jobs the second law cannot do for itself: it gives a qualitative definition of force (force is whatever changes a state of rest or uniform motion), and it defines the class of reference frames — inertial frames — in which F⃗ = ma⃗ is even allowed to be written. Without the first law, the second law is circular: it would be a formula with no independent statement of when it holds.
Key relation First law: if F⃗net = 0 (N), then v⃗ = constant (m s⁻¹) — a body at rest stays at rest and a body in uniform motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed. Setting F⃗net = 0 in F⃗ = ma⃗ gives a⃗ = 0, i.e. v⃗ = constant — which looks like the first law, and that is exactly the trap; the first law's real content is the assertion that frames exist in which this is true.
Solution
1. It defines force qualitatively. The first law tells us what a force is — the external agency needed to change a body's state of rest or of uniform straight-line motion.The second law only says how much acceleration a force produces; it presupposes that we already know what a force is.
2. It defines the inertial frame. An inertial frame is defined as one in which the first law holds — a frame in which a body with no net force really does keep constant velocity.This is the law's deepest job: F⃗ = ma⃗ is only valid in inertial frames, so without the first law we would not know in which frames we may apply the second and third laws at all.
3. It kills the Aristotelian error. It establishes that no force is needed to maintain uniform motion — force is needed only to change motion.Everyday experience (a pushed box stops) suggests motion needs a sustaining force; the first law says the box stops only because friction is an unbalanced external force.
4. It introduces inertia. It names the property — inertia, measured by the mass m (kg) — by which matter resists any change in its state of motion.That is why the first law is also called the law of inertia.
5. It is the logical foundation, not a corollary. Although a⃗ = 0 drops out of F⃗ = ma⃗ when F⃗net = 0, the first law is not merely that special case, because the second law needs the first law's frame-definition before it can be stated without circularity.

⚡ JEE Trick The one-line answer examiners want: "The first law defines force and defines the inertial frame in which the second law is valid." Trap: answering "it is just F = ma with F = 0" scores nothing — that reverses the logic and misses that NLM-II presupposes NLM-I. Remember: NLM-I is qualitative (what force is + where the laws work), NLM-II is quantitative (how much).
Key point: Newton's first law must be studied because it is logically prior to the second law — it defines force qualitatively, defines the inertial frames in which F⃗ = ma⃗ is valid, introduces inertia (mass), and establishes that uniform motion needs no force. It is the foundation of the second and third laws, not a corollary of them.
2Distinguish between inertial and non-inertial frames of reference.Inertial vs non-inertial framesInertial vs non-inertial frames of reference🟢 Exact—
Concept A frame of reference is the coordinate system (plus clock) from which motion is measured. The dividing question is whether Newton's first law holds in it. An inertial frame is one that is unaccelerated: a body with no net force stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion. A non-inertial frame is accelerating (linearly or rotating): in it, free bodies appear to accelerate with no real force, so the first law fails unless you invent a pseudo-force.
Formula Inertial: Fnet = m a holds as written. Non-inertial frame accelerating with a0: Fnet + Fpseudo = m a′ with Fpseudo = − m a0. The pseudo-force is a bookkeeping term that restores F = ma inside an accelerating frame; it has no real agent (no third-law partner).
Solution
1. Inertial frame: a frame moving with constant velocity (zero acceleration). Newton's three laws are valid exactly.
2. Non-inertial frame: a frame that is accelerating or rotating. Newton's laws are not valid unless pseudo-forces are added.
3. Pseudo-force: absent in inertial frames; required (magnitude m·a0, opposite to the frame's acceleration) in non-inertial frames.Centrifugal force and the "backward push" in an accelerating bus are pseudo-forces.
4. Third-law test: every force in an inertial frame has a real reaction; a pseudo-force has none — the cleanest way to tell them apart.
5. Examples: the ground/lab is inertial to good approximation; an accelerating car, a merry-go-round, and (strictly) the rotating Earth are non-inertial.
 property           inertial frame     non-inertial frame
 acceleration       zero               non-zero (linear/rotating)
 Newton's laws      valid              invalid without correction
 pseudo-force       not needed         needed  (= - m a0)
 example            distant stars      accelerating bus, turntable
⚡ JEE Trick One-line test: release a body with no real force acting — if it stays put or moves uniformly, the frame is inertial; if it "spontaneously" accelerates, it is non-inertial. Any frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial. Trap: calling a pseudo-force "real" — it vanishes the moment you switch to an inertial frame and has no reaction partner.
Answer: An inertial frame is unaccelerated and obeys Newton's laws directly; a non-inertial frame is accelerating/rotating and needs a pseudo-force (−m a0) for F = ma to work.
3Ten identical masses joined by 10 strings; topmost held, system accelerated up at g/2. Tension in the 6th string from the top?Tension in a chain of massesTensions for masses pulled upward by a string🟡 Similar1/200 ⚠
Concept Ten identical masses m hang one below the other from 10 strings, and the whole column is hauled upward with acceleration a = g/2. Any string in such a chain must do exactly two things for everything hanging below it: hold up its weight, and supply the extra force needed to accelerate it upward. So the tension in the k-th string depends only on how many masses hang below that string. The trick is to cut the chain at the string in question and treat everything below the cut as one single body — that removes all the internal strings from the problem in one stroke.
Formula For a sub-system of total mass Mbelow (kg) accelerating upward at a (m s⁻²), Newton's second law along the vertical gives T − Mbelow g = Mbelow a ⇒ T = Mbelow(g + a) (N). Upward T is positive, downward weight is negative, and the net must equal (mass)(upward acceleration). Here a = g/2, so g + a = g + g/2 = 3g/2 (m s⁻²). Data: 10 masses, each m (kg); strings light (massless) so tension is uniform along each string.
Solution
1. Fix the labelling: string 1 is the topmost (held), and string k sits directly above mass k, for k = 1 to 10.10 strings and 10 masses: string 1 → mass 1 → string 2 → mass 2 → ... → string 10 → mass 10.
2. Count what hangs below the 6th string: since string 6 sits just above mass 6, it carries masses 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 = 5 masses.Number carried by string k = 10 − k + 1 = 11 − k; for k = 6 this is 11 − 6 = 5. This counting step is where nearly all marks are lost.
3. Total mass below the cut: Mbelow = 5 × m = 5m kg.

4Are there situations where Newton's laws cannot be applied? Is there an alternative?Limits of Newton's laws / pseudo forcePseudo force in a non-inertial frame🟡 Similar—
Concept Yes — Newton's laws are not universal. They have a domain of validity, and outside it they fail. The three escape routes to know are: (1) in a non-inertial (accelerating) frame the laws appear to break, and the fix is to add a pseudo-force; (2) at speeds close to the speed of light, Newtonian mechanics is replaced by special relativity; (3) at atomic/sub-atomic scales it is replaced by quantum mechanics. The one the question is really after (this is a Newton's-first-law chapter) is the non-inertial frame + pseudo-force case.
Key relation In a frame accelerating with acceleration a⃗frame (m s⁻²), Newton's second law is repaired by adding a fictitious force to every body of mass m (kg): F⃗pseudo = − m a⃗frame (N), after which F⃗real + F⃗pseudo = m a⃗(relative to the frame). The minus sign means the pseudo-force points opposite to the frame's acceleration; its magnitude is m|a⃗frame| in newtons. A pseudo-force has no reaction partner and no physical agent — so Newton's third law does not apply to it.
Solution
1. Where the laws fail: non-inertial frames. Newton's laws hold only in inertial frames. In an accelerating frame a body can be seen to accelerate with no real force acting, contradicting the first law.Example: a bus starts forward with acceleration a; to a passenger inside (the accelerating frame), a ball on the floor rolls backward although nothing pushes it.
2. Alternative 1 — switch frames. Simply describe the motion from an inertial frame (the ground) and the laws work again unmodified.From the ground, the ball is not accelerating backward at all — it is simply staying put by inertia while the bus moves forward under it. No paradox.
3. Alternative 2 — keep the frame, add a pseudo-force. If we insist on working in the accelerating frame, we restore F⃗ = ma⃗ by adding F⃗pseudo = −m a⃗frame (N) to every body.For the bus: a⃗frame is forward, so each body of mass m feels a pseudo-force of magnitude ma backward — which is exactly what "throws the passenger backward" as seen from inside.
4. Familiar examples of pseudo-forces: centrifugal force (mν²/r, N, outward, in a rotating frame) and the Coriolis force; also the apparent weight change in an accelerating lift.
5. Other limits of validity. (i) For v comparable to c (≈ 3×10⁸ m s⁻¹) Newtonian mechanics fails and special relativity takes over — only F⃗ = dp⃗/dt survives, with p⃗ = γmv⃗. (ii) At atomic and sub-atomic scale, quantum mechanics replaces it, since position and momentum cannot be simultaneously definite (uncertainty principle).

1Two bodies P (M) and Q (m), M > m, distance d apart; equal force applied so they approach. Where do they hit?Equal force, unequal massTwo bodies under the same force — acceleration & distance🟡 Similarbnearer to P
Concept Two bodies of different masses M and m (with M > m) receive the same force and start from rest, moving toward each other from a separation d. Under equal forces the accelerations are unequal: a = F/m, so the lighter body Q accelerates more, and therefore covers more ground before they meet. They collide nearer to whichever body travelled less — the heavier one, P. This is really the statement that inertia (mass) decides the response to a given force.
Formula Acceleration: a = F ÷ mass (m s⁻²). Distance from rest in time t: s = ½ a t² (m), since u = 0. Both bodies are released at the same instant and collide at the same instant, so t is identical for both — and with s = ½at², equal t makes s directly proportional to a, and hence inversely proportional to the mass. Data: masses M and m with M > m, same force F (N), both from rest, initial gap d (m).
Solution
1. Find each acceleration from the same force F: aP = F/M and aQ = F/m (m s⁻²).
2. Compare them: since M > m, dividing F by the larger number gives the smaller result ⇒ aP < aQ. The lighter body Q accelerates faster.
3. Both start from rest (u = 0) and travel for the same time t until they meet, so s = ½at² gives sP = ½(F/M)t² and sQ = ½(F/m)t².
4. Take the ratio — the ½, F and t² all cancel: sP ÷ sQ = (F/M) ÷ (F/m) = m ÷ M.Since m < M, this ratio is less than 1 ⇒ sP < sQ: the heavy body P covers the shorter distance.
5. Locate the meeting point: with sP + sQ = d and sP < sQ, the collision happens closer to P's starting position ⇒ nearer to P, option (b).
6. Exact position (for completeness): sP = d m/(M+m) and sQ = d M/(M+m) (m). Since m < M, sP < d/2 — P has not even reached the midpoint.This is exactly the centre of mass of the two-body system: equal and opposite forces mean the centre of mass never moves, and they must meet there.
7. Sanity check with numbers: M = 3 kg, m = 1 kg, d = 4 m ⇒ sP = 4(1)/4 = 1 m and sQ = 4(3)/4 = 3 m. They meet 1 m from P and 3 m from Q — clearly nearer P ✓.
