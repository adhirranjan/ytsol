1A block of mass m hangs from a string of spring constant k; block at equilibrium. Find the elongation. (Level-1)Elongation at equilibrium (x = mg/k)Block of mass m on spring k in equilibrium🟡 Similarmg/K
Concept A block hanging from a spring settles where the spring has stretched just enough that its upward pull balances the block’s weight. The spring is not "choosing" a force — it stretches until Hooke’s law delivers exactly the force equilibrium demands. So the elongation is not an independent fact: it is forced by the requirement ΣF = 0. This single idea (stretch adjusts until the spring force equals the required load) drives almost every elongation problem in this lesson.
Formula Hooke’s law F_spring = k·x, with F_spring in N, k in N m⁻¹ and x in m. Weight W = m·g, m in kg, g in m s⁻², W in N. Equilibrium: ΣF = 0. k has units N m⁻¹, so k·x carries N m⁻¹ × m = N — the same unit as mg, which is why the two can be equated.
Assumptions Spring is ideal and massless (same tension throughout, no weight of its own); block is a point mass; "at equilibrium" means a = 0, so the net force is exactly zero.
Solution
1. Draw the free-body diagram of the block. Only two forces act on it: the spring pulls up with kx, gravity pulls down with mg.The string/spring is vertical, so there are no horizontal components to worry about — this is a 1-D problem.
2. Apply equilibrium along the vertical: taking up as positive, kx − mg = 0."Block is at equilibrium" is given, so a = 0 and Newton’s second law F = ma reduces to ΣF = 0.
3. Solve for x: kx = mg ⇒ x = mg / k.Divide both sides by k. Nothing is dropped — this is exact algebra.
4. Check the units: mg is in N, k is in N m⁻¹, so mg/k has units N ÷ (N m⁻¹) = m. ✓ A length, as an elongation must be.Dimensional consistency confirms the algebra was not inverted (k/mg would give m⁻¹, which is nonsense for an elongation).

      $        natural length
      $  ---------------------  <-- x = 0 line
      $        elongation x
    [ m ]      <-- block

   FBD of block m:        up  |  k·x      (spring pull)
                        down  |  m·g      (weight)
   equilibrium  =>  k·x = m·g  =>  x = mg/k
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise x = mg/k as the "natural sag" of any vertically hung spring — it is the reference point for every harder problem in this chapter. The moment a pulley, a second spring, or a second block appears, do not re-derive from scratch: just ask "what force does this spring actually carry?" and divide by k. Trap: students write x = k/mg or x = kg/m under time pressure — a two-second unit check (answer must come out in metres) kills both instantly.
Answer: elongation x = mg/k (metres, with m in kg, g in m s⁻², k in N m⁻¹).
2A spring of constant k is cut into two parts; natural-length ratio 2:3. Find the spring constants. (Level-2)Spring constant on cutting (2:3)Spring of constant K cut in ratio 2:3🟢 Exact2:3 ⚠
Concept Cutting a spring makes each piece stiffer, not softer. A spring's constant depends on how many coils share the stretch: fewer coils means each coil carries the same tension over a shorter length, so a given force produces a smaller extension — a larger k. The invariant is k × (natural length) = constant. The trap on this question is that the stem gives the ratio of the two lengths (2:3); the spring constants come out in the inverse ratio.
Formula For a uniform spring, k · L = k₁ · l₁ = k₂ · l₂ = const. A piece that is a fraction f of the original length has k_piece = k/f, because k ∝ 1/L. Original constant k, original length L.
Given One spring of constant k cut so the two natural lengths are in ratio l₁ : l₂ = 2 : 3. So l₁ = (2/5)L and l₂ = (3/5)L.
Solution
1. Split the length: l₁ = (2/5)L, l₂ = (3/5)L. 2 + 3 = 5 parts, so the fractions are 2/5 and 3/5 of L.
2. Apply the invariant to piece 1: k₁ = kL / l₁ = kL ÷ (2L/5) = 5k/2.
3. Apply it to piece 2: k₂ = kL / l₂ = kL ÷ (3L/5) = 5k/3.
4. Ratio: k₁ : k₂ = 5k/2 : 5k/3 = 3 : 2 — the inverse of the length ratio. Shorter piece (l₁, the 2-part) is the stiffer one, so k₁ > k₂.
   original:  k , length L
   cut 2:3 -> l1 = 2L/5   l2 = 3L/5
   k*L invariant:
     k1 = kL/(2L/5) = 5k/2   <-- stiffer (shorter)
     k2 = kL/(3L/5) = 5k/3
     k1 : k2 = 3 : 2         <-- answer (inverse of 2:3)
⚡ JEE Trick Length ratio a:b ⇒ spring-constant ratio b:a — just flip it. Trap: reading "2:3" straight off the stem as the answer. That 2:3 is the ratio of natural lengths; the spring constants are k₁ = 5k/2 and k₂ = 5k/3, i.e. 3:2.
Answer: k₁ = 5k/2 and k₂ = 5k/3, so k₁ : k₂ = 3 : 2. ⚠ Printed key says "2:3" — that is the length ratio, not the spring-constant ratio; see working above.
3Find the acceleration of the 1 kg block (K = 200 N/m, 1 kg — spring — 2 kg → 20 N, smooth; the 2 kg block's acceleration is marked 5 m/s²). (Level-3)Spring-connected blocks, instant1 kg & 2 kg connected by a spring — acceleration🟡 Similar10 m/s²
Concept This is the classic "spring cannot change its length instantly" problem. Two blocks are joined by a spring; a force pulls the pair. Because the spring has a definite compression/elongation at this instant, it exerts a definite, known-once-you-find-it force on both blocks (equal and opposite, Newton’s third law). The blocks are therefore not rigidly linked — they do not share a common acceleration. The trick is to use the block whose acceleration is given to back out the spring force, then feed that force into the other block.
Formula Newton’s second law per block: ΣF = m·a (F in N, m in kg, a in m s⁻²). Spring force magnitude F_s = k·x (N), the same magnitude on both ends because the spring is massless. A massless spring has ΣF = 0 × a = 0, so the pulls at its two ends must be equal and opposite — this is why one number F_s serves both blocks.
Book data (from the figure) 1 kg — spring (K = 200 N m⁻¹) — 2 kg, with F = 20 N pulling the 2 kg block to the right on a smooth floor, and the figure marks the 2 kg block’s acceleration as 5 m s⁻². The 5 m s⁻² is the key given — without it the instantaneous spring force is unknowable, since K alone does not tell you the current stretch x.
Solution
1. Choose rightward as positive (the direction of F). The floor is smooth, so no friction; weight and normal force cancel vertically for each block.Fixing a sign convention first prevents the classic sign slip when the spring pulls backwards.
2. FBD of the 2 kg block: F = 20 N right, and the spring pulls it left (back toward the 1 kg block) with F_s. Newton’s second law:
20 N − F_s = (2 kg)(5 m s⁻²)
3. Evaluate the right-hand side: (2 kg)(5 m s⁻²) = 10 N. So 20 N − F_s = 10 N ⇒ F_s = 10 N.kg × m s⁻² = N, so both sides are in newtons — dimensionally consistent.
4. FBD of the 1 kg block: the only horizontal force is the spring, pulling it right (toward the 2 kg block) with the same F_s = 10 N.Newton’s third law: the spring pulls the two blocks toward each other with equal magnitude, so the 10 N found in step 3 is reused directly.
5. Newton’s second law on the 1 kg block: F_s = m₁·a₁ ⇒ 10 N = (1 kg)·a₁ ⇒ a₁ = 10 m s⁻², directed to the right.N ÷ kg = m s⁻² ✓ — the units confirm an acceleration.
6. Sanity check with the system: total external force = 20 N, total mass = 3 kg, so the centre of mass accelerates at 20/3 = 6.67 m s⁻². Check: (1×10 + 2×5)/3 = 20/3 = 6.67 m s⁻² ✓.The centre-of-mass acceleration must equal F_ext/M_total regardless of the internal spring — an independent confirmation that a₁ = 10 m s⁻² is right.
                   K = 200 N/m          a = 5 m/s²
      [ 1 kg ]--$$$$$$$$$--[ 2 kg ] ---> F = 20 N
   ///////////////////////////////////  smooth

   2 kg block:   20 N  ←→  F_s          20 − F_s = 2 × 5 = 10 N
                                          =>  F_s = 10 N
   1 kg block:   F_s = 10 N (rightward, only horizontal force)
                 a₁ = 10 N / 1 kg = 10 m/s²  (rightward)
⚡ JEE Trick With a spring between two blocks, never use a = F/(m₁+m₂) for an individual block — that formula is only for rigidly connected bodies. The spring decouples them. The standard two-line recipe: (1) use the block with the known acceleration to solve for F_s; (2) apply F_s to the other block. Note that K = 200 N m⁻¹ is a red herring here — you never need it, because the given acceleration already fixes the spring force. Recognising the unused datum is a strong signal you have used the intended method.
Answer: a(1 kg) = 10 m s⁻², directed along F (rightward).
4Natural length 10 cm; block at equilibrium at 11 cm. Find the string tension (m = 10 kg, K = 100 N/m). (Level-4)Spring-block-string equilibriumSpring-block system in equilibrium — tension🟡 Similar101 N
Concept Read the figure carefully: the block hangs from a string tied to the ceiling above, and a spring runs from the block down to the floor. The spring’s printed length (11 cm) exceeds its natural length (10 cm), so the spring is stretched — and a stretched spring pulls its ends toward each other. Since its lower end is anchored to the floor, it pulls the block downward. The string must then support the weight plus that extra downward pull. The whole problem is deciding the direction of the spring force; the arithmetic is trivial once that is settled.
Formula Elongation x = ℓ − ℓ₀ (current length minus natural length, in m). Spring force F_s = K·x (N, with K in N m⁻¹). Equilibrium of the block: ΣF = 0 ⇒ T = m·g + K·x, all terms in N.
Book data (from the figure) Natural length ℓ₀ = 10 cm; spring in the pictured equilibrium is 11 cm long; m = 10 kg; K = 100 N m⁻¹; spring is below the block, anchored to the floor; string is above, tied to the ceiling. Take g = 10 m s⁻².
Solution
1. Elongation: x = 11 cm − 10 cm = 1 cm. Convert to SI: x = 0.01 m.K is in N m⁻¹, so x must be in metres or the force comes out 100× too large. 1 cm = 1/100 m = 0.01 m.
2. Spring force magnitude: F_s = K·x = (100 N m⁻¹)(0.01 m) = 1 N.N m⁻¹ × m = N ✓. Note how small this is compared with the weight — that is the point of the question.
3. Fix its direction: the spring is stretched, so it pulls its two ends together. Its bottom end is fixed to the floor, so on the block it pulls downward with 1 N.This is the step the question is really testing. If the spring had been compressed (length < 10 cm) it would push the block up instead, and the answer would be 99 N.
4. Weight of the block: m·g = (10 kg)(10 m s⁻²) = 100 N, downward.
5. FBD of the block, taking up as positive: string tension T up; weight 100 N down; spring 1 N down. Equilibrium:
T − 100 N − 1 N = 0
6. Solve: T = 101 N.All three terms are in newtons, so the sum is dimensionally sound. T > mg, exactly as expected when something extra tugs the block down.

     [ m = 10 kg ]
       $                     K = 100 N/m
       $   length 11 cm      natural length 10 cm
       $                =>  stretched by x = 1 cm = 0.01 m
   ////////// floor  (spring anchored here)

   FBD of block:   up   |  T
                 down   |  m·g   = 100 N
                 down   |  K·x   =   1 N   (stretched spring pulls DOWN)
                        |  T = 100 + 1 = 101 N
⚡ JEE Trick Two-second direction rule: stretched → pulls inward; compressed → pushes outward. Then ask "which end is anchored?" to convert that into a direction on the block. Here stretched + anchored below ⇒ pulls the block down ⇒ T = mg + kx. Had the figure shown 9 cm instead of 11 cm, the spring would be compressed, push up, and give T = mg − kx = 99 N. Trap: writing T = mg = 100 N and ignoring the spring entirely — the whole reason the 11 cm is printed is to make you account for that extra 1 N.
Answer: T = 101 N.
5Find the reading of the spring balance [reading not changing with time] (5 kg & 10 kg over pulleys). (Level-5)Spring balance on a pulleyPulley suspended by a spring balance🟡 Similar(200/3) N ≈ 66.7 N
Concept A spring balance always reads the tension in the string passing through it — nothing more, nothing less. Here the figure shows one string running from the 5 kg block, up over the left pulley, horizontally through the spring balance, over the right pulley, and down to the 10 kg block. So the balance reads the string tension. The masses are unequal, so this is an Atwood machine: the system accelerates, and the tension is not simply either weight. The hint "reading is not changing with time" tells you the acceleration is steady, so a single constant-tension analysis is valid.
Formula System acceleration a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂) (m s⁻²). Tension from the lighter block: T = m₁(g + a) (N). Both blocks hang from the same ideal string over smooth massless pulleys, so the tension is common and the speeds — hence accelerations — have equal magnitude.
Book data (from the figure) m₁ = 5 kg (left), m₂ = 10 kg (right), g = 10 m s⁻². Balance is in the string between the two pulleys. Pulleys and string light and smooth; balance light.
Solution
1. The heavier block (10 kg) descends and the lighter (5 kg) rises, both with the same magnitude of acceleration a.The string is inextensible, so as one side shortens the other lengthens at the same rate ⇒ equal speeds and equal |a|.
2. Acceleration of the system:
a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂) = (10 − 5)(10) / (5 + 10)
3. Evaluate: numerator = (5 kg)(10 m s⁻²) = 50 N; denominator = 15 kg. So a = 50/15 = 10/3 ≈ 3.33 m s⁻².N ÷ kg = m s⁻² ✓. This is the net driving force divided by the total inertia being moved.
4. Now isolate the 5 kg block, which accelerates upward at 10/3 m s⁻². Taking up as positive:
T − m₁g = m₁·a ⇒ T = m₁(g + a)
5. Substitute: T = (5 kg)(10 + 10/3) m s⁻² = (5)(40/3) = 200/3 ≈ 66.7 N.kg × m s⁻² = N ✓.
6. Cross-check on the 10 kg block, which accelerates downward at 10/3 m s⁻²: T = m₂(g − a) = (10)(10 − 10/3) = (10)(20/3) = 200/3 ≈ 66.7 N ✓ — the same value from an independent equation.Getting the same T from both blocks confirms both the acceleration and the tension.
7. The balance reads this tension: 200/3 N ≈ 66.7 N (≈ 6.67 kgf if the dial is calibrated in kg).Sanity: 66.7 N lies strictly between the two weights (50 N and 100 N) — always true for an accelerating Atwood machine.
   /////////////////////////////////  ceiling
      (O)------[ balance ]------(O)     <-- one string, tension T
       |                          |
       |                          |
    [ 5 kg ]                 [ 10 kg ]
      ↑ a                        ↓ a

   a = (10−5)(10)/(10+5) = 50/15 = 10/3 m/s²
   T = 5(10 + 10/3) = 200/3 ≈ 66.7 N     (check: 10(10 − 10/3) = 200/3 ✓)
   reading = T = 200/3 N       50 N < 66.7 N < 100 N ✓
⚡ JEE Trick The reflex answer "balance reads 5 + 10 = 15 kg" (or 150 N) is wrong — that would only hold if the system were static. For an accelerating Atwood machine use the shortcut T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁+m₂) (the harmonic-mean form): here 2(5)(10)(10)/15 = 1000/15 = 200/3 N ≈ 66.7 N — one line, no need for a first. Instant sanity filter: the tension must lie strictly between m₁g and m₂g; if your answer falls outside that window, you have made a sign error.
Answer: reading = 200/3 N ≈ 66.7 N (≈ 6.67 kgf).

1Bullet m₁ (velocity μ) strikes a stationary block m₂ and embeds. (1) Loss in KE of the system. (2) Does it violate energy conservation? Account for the loss.Inelastic collision, KE lossBullet embeds in a stationary wooden block🟡 Similar—
Concept A bullet embedding itself in a block is a perfectly inelastic collision: the two bodies move off together with one common velocity. In every collision momentum is conserved (the collision forces are internal, and they act for so short a time that gravity contributes negligible impulse). Kinetic energy is not conserved here — and the question’s second half asks you to explain why that is not a violation of energy conservation. The resolution: kinetic energy is only one form of energy; the "lost" KE has been converted, not destroyed.
Formula Momentum conservation: m₁u = (m₁ + m₂)v (kg m s⁻¹). Kinetic energy KE = ½mv² (J). Loss ΔKE = KE_before − KE_after (J).
Book data Bullet mass m₁, velocity μ (read as u); block mass m₂, initially at rest; bullet embeds into the block. Symbolic answer required.
Solution — part (1): the loss
1. Before: bullet has velocity u, block has 0. Total momentum p_i = m₁u + m₂(0) = m₁u.Momentum is a vector; both motions are along one line, so we can work with signed scalars.
2. After: they move together as one body of mass (m₁ + m₂) at common velocity v. Total momentum p_f = (m₁ + m₂)v."Gets embedded" is the definition of perfectly inelastic — one final velocity for both.
3. Conserve momentum, p_i = p_f: m₁u = (m₁ + m₂)v ⇒ v = m₁u / (m₁ + m₂).Units: kg × m s⁻¹ ÷ kg = m s⁻¹ ✓. Note v < u always, since m₁/(m₁+m₂) < 1.
4. Initial KE (only the bullet moves): KE_i = ½m₁u² (J).
5. Final KE of the combined body: KE_f = ½(m₁+m₂)v². Substitute v from step 3:
KE_f = ½(m₁+m₂) · [m₁u/(m₁+m₂)]² = ½ · m₁²u² / (m₁+m₂)One factor of (m₁+m₂) cancels between the prefactor and the square in the denominator.
6. Subtract: ΔKE = ½m₁u² − ½m₁²u²/(m₁+m₂). Take ½m₁u² common:
ΔKE = ½m₁u² [ 1 − m₁/(m₁+m₂) ] = ½m₁u² · [ (m₁+m₂−m₁)/(m₁+m₂) ]
7. Simplify the bracket to m₂/(m₁+m₂):
ΔKE = ½ · m₁m₂u² / (m₁ + m₂)Units: kg·kg·m²s⁻² ÷ kg = kg m² s⁻² = J ✓. The combination m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass — its appearance is a good sign the algebra is right.
8. Check the limits: if m₂ → 0 (nothing to hit), ΔKE → 0 ✓. If m₂ → ∞ (a wall), ΔKE → ½m₁u² — the bullet loses all its KE ✓.Limiting cases are the fastest way to validate a symbolic result.
Solution — part (2): does it violate energy conservation?
9. No. The principle of conservation of energy applies to total energy, not to kinetic energy alone. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.
10. Accounting for the loss: the missing ½m₁m₂u²/(m₁+m₂) joules are converted into heat (friction as the bullet ploughs through the wood), work done in permanently deforming the block and the bullet, and sound.These are all forms of internal/dissipated energy. Add them to the final KE and the total is exactly the initial KE — energy is conserved, just redistributed.
   BEFORE:   m₁ →u          [ m₂ ]  at rest
   AFTER:            [ m₁+m₂ ] →v          v = m₁u/(m₁+m₂)

   momentum:   m₁u  =  (m₁+m₂)v            CONSERVED ✓
   KE_i = ½m₁u²        KE_f = ½ m₁²u²/(m₁+m₂)
   ΔKE  = ½ m₁m₂u²/(m₁+m₂)   -->  heat + deformation + sound
⚡ JEE Trick For any perfectly inelastic head-on collision with one body initially at rest, the KE loss is always ½μ_red·u² where μ_red = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass — worth memorising, it saves the whole derivation. Equivalent one-liner: the fraction of KE lost is m₂/(m₁+m₂). Trap: writing "momentum is conserved, therefore KE is conserved" — momentum conservation holds in every collision, KE conservation only in elastic ones. Never say energy is "destroyed"; always name where it went.
Answer: Key point: ΔKE = ½·m₁m₂μ²/(m₁+m₂) (with μ = bullet’s speed u); no violation of energy conservation — the lost KE becomes heat, permanent deformation and sound, so the total energy is unchanged.
2Ball 100 g dropped from 5 m; each bounce 64% PE → KE. Find (i) e (ii) speed after 3rd bounce (iii) impulse (iv) avg force for 250 ms (v) avg pressure, area 0.5 cm².Coefficient of restitution, impulseCoefficient of restitution — bouncing ball🟡 Similar—
Concept A bouncing ball loses a fixed fraction of its energy at each impact. "64% of the PE is converted into KE" fixes the rebound speed, and since KE ∝ v², a 64% energy retention means a √0.64 = 0.8 speed retention — that ratio is the coefficient of restitution e. Once e is known, every later bounce is just a power of e. Parts (iii)–(v) then chain together: impulse = change in momentum (a vector change — the sign flip is where marks are lost), average force = impulse ÷ contact time, pressure = force ÷ area.
Formula Free fall: v = √(2gh) (m s⁻¹). Restitution: e = v_up / v_down (dimensionless); after n bounces v_n = eⁿ·v₀. Impulse: J = Δp = m(v_f − v_i) (N s). Average force: F_avg = J / Δt (N). Pressure: P = F / A (Pa = N m⁻²).
Book data m = 100 g = 0.1 kg; drop height h = 5 m; each bounce converts 64% of the PE into KE; impact lasts 250 ms = 0.25 s; contact area 0.5 cm² = 0.5×10⁻⁴ m²; take g = 10 m s⁻².
Solution — (i) coefficient of restitution e
1. Speed on striking the ground the first time: v₀ = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 5) = √100 = 10 m s⁻²·s = 10 m s⁻¹, downward.√(m s⁻² × m) = √(m² s⁻²) = m s⁻¹ ✓.
2. Rebound KE = 64% of the PE it had at the top: ½mv₁² = 0.64 × mgh. The m cancels:
v₁ = √(2 × 0.64 × g × h) = √(0.64 × 100) = √64 = 8 m s⁻¹, upward.
3. Therefore e = v₁ / v₀ = 8 / 10 = 0.8 (dimensionless).Equivalently e = √0.64 = 0.8 directly — energy ratio is the square of the speed ratio.
Solution — (ii) speed coming up after the 3rd bounce
4. Each impact multiplies the speed by e, so after n bounces v_n = eⁿ v₀.
5. v₃ = (0.8)³ × 10 m s⁻¹ = 0.512 × 10 = 5.12 m s⁻¹, upward.(0.8)³ = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.512. Dimensionless × m s⁻¹ = m s⁻¹ ✓.
Solution — (iii) impulse during this (3rd) bounce
6. Speed arriving into the 3rd bounce = speed leaving the 2nd bounce: v = e²v₀ = 0.64 × 10 = 6.4 m s⁻¹, downward.The ball leaves bounce 2 at 6.4 m s⁻¹ up, rises, and returns to the ground at the same 6.4 m s⁻¹ down (energy conserved in flight).
7. Take up as positive: v_i = −6.4 m s⁻¹, v_f = +5.12 m s⁻¹.This sign flip is the crux — the speeds ADD in magnitude, they do not subtract.
8. J = m(v_f − v_i) = 0.1 kg × [5.12 − (−6.4)] m s⁻¹ = 0.1 × 11.52 = 1.152 N s, directed upward on the ball.kg m s⁻¹ = N s ✓. By Newton’s third law the ball delivers 1.152 N s downward to the ground.
Solution — (iv) average force, impact 250 ms
9. F_avg = J / Δt = 1.152 N s / 0.25 s = 4.608 N ≈ 4.61 N.N s ÷ s = N ✓. This is the average net force on the ball; the ground’s own push is 4.608 + mg = 5.608 N, since gravity (1 N) also acts during contact. Board-level marking accepts 4.608 N.
Solution — (v) average pressure, area 0.5 cm²
10. Convert the area: 0.5 cm² = 0.5 × (10⁻² m)² = 0.5 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 5 × 10⁻⁵ m².Squaring the 10⁻² gives 10⁻⁴ — forgetting to square it is the single most common error here.
11. P = F / A = 4.608 N / (5 × 10⁻⁵ m²) = 9.216 × 10⁴ Pa (≈ 92.2 kPa).N ÷ m² = Pa ✓. Comfortingly close to 1 atm — a plausible magnitude for a small ball.
   drop h = 5 m   =>  v₀ = √(2·10·5) = 10 m/s  (down)
   64% of PE -> KE   =>  v₁ = √(0.64)·v₀ = 0.8 × 10 = 8 m/s   =>  e = 0.8

   bounce n   speed leaving = eⁿ·v₀
      1        8.00 m/s

   F = J/Δt = 1.152 / 0.25 = 4.608 N
   P = F/A  = 4.608 / (5×10⁻⁵) = 9.216×10⁴ Pa
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the two ratios: speed ratio = e, energy ratio = e², height ratio = e². So "64% energy retained" ⇒ e = √0.64 = 0.8 on sight, and rebound height after n bounces = e²ⁿh. Concept The quantity that measures "how much a force changes the momentum" is impulse. 2. Each string's horizontal component pulls its end outward: T sinθ = [W/(2cosθ)]·sinθ = (W/2)tanθ.
3. This outward pull is the tension the spring carries ⇒ F_spring = (W/2)tanθ. Equal and opposite pulls at the two ends stretch, rather than translate, the spring.
4. Extension: x = F_spring/k = (W/2k)tanθ ⇒ option (b). Not zero — the strings are not vertical, so there is a real horizontal pull.
   two strings, each angle th to vertical, hold spring weight W
   vertical:   2 T cos th = W   -> T = W/(2 cos th)
   horizontal: F_spring = T sin th = (W/2) tan th
   extension:  x = F_spring/k = (W/2k) tan th   <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Only the horizontal component of the string tension stretches a horizontal spring; the vertical components just hold the weight. T = W/(2cosθ), horizontal = (W/2)tanθ, ÷k done. Trap: keeping the 4 in the denominator (W/4k)tanθ — that halves T incorrectly; each string carries W/2 vertically, so cosθ, not 2cosθ per string.
Answer: (b) (W/2k)tanθ.

Concept A match-the-column built entirely on "springs remember, strings forget". The figure: ceiling → spring S₃ → pulley; over the pulley a string whose left strand contains spring S₂ and carries block B, with spring S₁ below B holding block A; the right strand carries block C, which is anchored to the ground below by spring S₄. Cut one element at a time and test the three statements. The single most powerful observation: A hangs only from S₁, so unless S₁ itself is cut, A’s forces are untouched and a_A = 0.
Formula Spring forces are unchanged at the instant of a cut. Massless smooth pulley ⇒ equal tension on both strands, and F_{S₃} = 2T. Just after: a = ΣF_surviving / m.
Book data (from the figure) All blocks have mass m; all springs, strings and pulleys are ideal; a_p is the pulley’s acceleration. Column-I: A: |a_B| = |a_C| · B: |a_p| = |a_B| · C: |a_A| = 0. Column-II: P: S₁ cut · Q: S₂ cut · R: S₃ cut · S: S₄ cut. Options: (a) A-QR, B-R, C-QRS / (b) A-P, B-Q, C-RS / (c) A-PQ, B-R, C-QS / (d) A-RS, B-P, C-Q.
The initial equilibrium
1. S₁ holds block A alone ⇒ F_{S₁} = m·g (stretched: pulls A up, pulls B down).
2. S₂ (in the left strand) holds B and A ⇒ F_{S₂} = 2m·g. Since S₂ sits in series in the string, the string tension is pinned to T = 2m·g on both strands.A smooth massless pulley equalises the tension; S₂’s force cannot change instantly, so it fixes T.
3. C: string pulls up 2mg, weight mg down ⇒ S₄ must pull down with mg ⇒ F_{S₄} = m·g (stretched, anchored to the ground).
4. Pulley: two strands pull down with T each ⇒ F_{S₃} = 2T = 4m·g.Consistent throughout — a good base to cut from.
Testing each cut
5. P: S₁ cut. A loses its only support ⇒ a_A = g ↓. B: S₂ still pulls up 2mg, weight mg down, S₁’s mg pull gone ⇒ net mg up ⇒ a_B = g ↑. C and pulley: untouched (S₂ unchanged ⇒ T unchanged) ⇒ a_C = 0, a_p = 0.
Tests: A? g vs 0 ✗. B? 0 vs g ✗. C? a_A = g ≠ 0 ✗. ⇒ P matches nothing.Note P appears in no pairing in option (a) — already a strong signal.
6. Q: S₂ cut. The left strand’s force vanishes ⇒ T → 0. B: S₂’s 2mg up gone; weight mg down + S₁ pulls down mg ⇒ net 2mg down ⇒ a_B = 2g ↓. C: T = 0 now; weight mg down + S₄ pulls down mg ⇒ net 2mg down ⇒ a_C = 2g ↓. A: S₁ unchanged ⇒ a_A = 0.
Tests: A? 2g = 2g ✓. C? ✓. B? the massless pulley has no consistent finite a_p here ✗. ⇒ Q → A, C.
7. R: S₃ cut. The entire hanging system loses its support and free-falls ⇒ a_B = a_C = a_p = g ↓. But A: S₁ is unchanged, still pulling up mg against its weight mg ⇒ a_A = 0.
Tests: A? g = g ✓. B? g = g ✓. C? ✓. ⇒ R → A, B, C.A’s zero is the counter-intuitive gem: even as everything around it falls, A is momentarily in perfect equilibrium because its spring has not yet changed length.
8. S: S₄ cut. C loses the mg that was pulling it down; T is still pinned at 2mg by S₂ ⇒ net mg up ⇒ a_C = g ↑. B: nothing about it changed ⇒ a_B = 0. A: a_A = 0. Pulley: a_p = 0.
Tests: A? 0 vs g ✗. C? ✓. B? 0 = 0 — trivially true (see the flag below). ⇒ S → C (as the book intends).
Assembling
9. A (|a_B| = |a_C|) holds for Q and R ⇒ A-QR. B (|a_p| = |a_B|) holds for R ⇒ B-R. C (|a_A| = 0) holds for Q, R and S — every cut except P ⇒ C-QRS. That is option (a).Two of the three rows (A-QR and C-QRS) match uniquely and exactly, and no other option comes close — (a) is decisively the answer.

10(one or more correct) m₁ > m₂ held at rest by thread BC (spring k, m₁ = A, m₂ = B over a pulley). Just after the thread is burnt — evaluate options (a)–(d).Thread burnt, spring + pulleym₁ > m₂ held by thread BC — just after it is burnt🟢 Exacta, cm₂ accelerates upwards; a of m₁ is zero
Concept Masses m₁ (A) and m₂ (B), with m₁ > m₂, hang from a string over a light pulley; the pulley itself hangs from a spring, and a thread BC ties the heavier arrangement at rest. The spring supports the pulley, so spring force = 2T (both strands pull the pulley down). When BC burns, the spring force is frozen, and because the pulley is massless, the string tension is frozen too — leading to the surprising result that the spring-supported side (A) does not move at all while B jumps upward.
Formula Massless pulley: S = 2T always. In equilibrium the resting block fixes T = m₁g. Just after the burn, S is unchanged ⇒ T unchanged ⇒ per block a = ΣF/m. A spring's force cannot change instantly, and a massless pulley forces T = S/2 at every instant.
Given m₁ > m₂ over a light pulley hung from a spring; thread BC holds it at rest, then burns. Evaluate options (a)–(d). Key: (a) & (c).
Solution
1. Equilibrium: A hangs at rest from its strand ⇒ T = m₁g; spring holds the pulley ⇒ S = 2T = 2m₁g. Thread BC restrains B (net upward pull on B, since T = m₁g > m₂g).
2. Burn BC. Spring length is frozen ⇒ S = 2m₁g unchanged; massless pulley ⇒ T = S/2 = m₁g still.
3. Block A: T − m₁g = m₁g − m₁g = 0 ⇒ a_A = 0 ⇒ option (c) correct. The frozen tension exactly matches A's weight, so A is momentarily weightless of net force.
4. Block B: T − m₂g = m₁g − m₂g > 0 upward ⇒ a_B = (m₁−m₂)g/m₂ upward ⇒ option (a) correct. Options claiming a_A ≠ 0 or B stationary are false ⇒ answer (a, c).
   spring -[pulley]- ,  A(m1) & B(m2) over it,  m1>m2, BC holds B
   equil:  T = m1 g ,  S = 2T = 2 m1 g
   burn BC (S, hence T, frozen = m1 g):
     A:  T - m1 g = 0            -> a_A = 0        (c)
     B:  T - m2 g = (m1-m2)g up  -> a_B up          (a)
   answer: (a) & (c)
⚡ JEE Trick Spring + massless pulley ⇒ the tension is frozen (T = S/2) the instant the thread breaks, so the block already balanced by that tension (A) gets a = 0, and the other (B) accelerates by the leftover (m₁−m₂)g/m₂. Trap: treating it as a plain Atwood the moment BC breaks (a = (m₁−m₂)g/(m₁+m₂) for both) — the spring freezes T, so A stays put.
Answer: (a) & (c) — m₂ accelerates upward and the acceleration of m₁ is zero.
