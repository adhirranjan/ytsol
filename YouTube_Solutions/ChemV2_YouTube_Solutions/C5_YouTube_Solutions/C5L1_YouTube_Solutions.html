1Which of the following is NOT a strong inter-atomic bond according to the theory? (a) Ionic bond (b) Covalent bond (c) Metallic bond (d) Vander Waals InteractionClassification of bondsThe weakest among the following is — ionic, covalent, metallic bond or van der Waals🟢 ExactdVander Waals Interaction
Concept The chapter opens by splitting every chemical bond into two families using bond energy as the yardstick. Strong (inter-atomic) bonds are the ones that actually build a molecule out of atoms — they involve real redistribution of valence electrons and carry roughly 200 kJ/mol. Weak (inter-molecular) forces act between already-formed molecules, involve no electron transfer or sharing at all, and carry only about 2.42 kJ/mol — nearly a hundred times less. So the question is really "which one is inter-molecular?"
Key relation Strong bond (inter-atomic, ≈200 kJ/mol) = Ionic + Covalent + Co-ordinate + Metallic. Weak bond (inter-molecular, ≈2.42 kJ/mol) = Hydrogen bond + van der Waals bond. The four strong ones all redistribute valence electrons — transfer (ionic), sharing (covalent), one-sided sharing (co-ordinate) or delocalisation (metallic). The two weak ones are purely electrostatic attractions between dipoles.
Solution
1. Sort each option into the classification tree given in the theory:
  • (a) Ionic — complete transfer of e⁻, e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ ⇒ strong, inter-atomic.
  • (b) Covalent — sharing of an electron pair ⇒ strong, inter-atomic.
  • (c) Metallic — valence electrons delocalised over kernels ⇒ strong, inter-atomic.
  • (d) van der Waals — instantaneous-dipole attraction between molecules; no electron is transferred or shared.This is the only one listed on the "WEAK BOND (inter molecular)" branch of the classification chart.
2. Therefore the odd one out — the bond that is not a strong inter-atomic bond — is van der Waals, option (d).

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the tree as 4 strong + 2 weak: strong = I·C·C·M (Ionic, Covalent, Co-ordinate, Metallic); weak = H·V (Hydrogen, van der Waals). 2Which pair of elements is most likely to form an ionic bond according to the theory? (a) Two group-17 elements (b) Group-1 element and group-17 element (c) Group-14 element and group-15 element (d) Two group-16 elementsWho forms ionic bondsThe pair of elements which on combination is most likely to form an ionic compound is🟢 ExactbGroup-1 element and group-17 element
Concept An ionic bond needs a complete transfer of electrons, and that only happens when one partner gives electrons away almost for free and the other grabs them hungrily. The theory states it as a distance rule: the greater the separation of the two elements in the periodic table, the greater the ionic character. Group 1 sits at the extreme left (most electropositive, lowest ionisation enthalpy) and group 17 at the extreme right (most electronegative, most negative electron gain enthalpy) — the maximum possible horizontal separation, hence the maximum electronegativity difference.
Key relation ionic character ∝ ΔEN = |EN(A) − EN(B)|, and by convention ΔEN > 1.7 ⇒ predominantly ionic. Group 1 has EN ≈ 0.8–1.0 and group 17 has EN ≈ 2.8–4.0, so ΔEN ≈ 2–3 — comfortably ionic. Two atoms of the same group give ΔEN = 0, i.e. a purely covalent bond.
Solution
1. Test option (b) — the electron bookkeeping works out perfectly for a 1 : 1 transfer:Na (2,8,1) loses exactly one electron to reach the Ne core 2,8; Cl (2,8,7) gains exactly one to reach the Ar core 2,8,8. Both partners end up with a noble-gas octet in a single step.
2. Eliminate (a) Two group-17 elements: both need to gain an electron, neither will donate — they share instead, giving Cl₂ (covalent, ΔEN = 0).
3. Eliminate (d) Two group-16 elements: identical reasoning — both want 2 electrons; O₂ is covalent.
4. Eliminate (c) Group-14 + group-15: carbon-family elements would have to lose or gain 4 electrons and nitrogen-family 3; the ionisation enthalpy needed for a 4+ ion is prohibitive, so these bond covalently (e.g. in C₃N₄, CN⁻).

 gp16 + gp16  O +O       0            neither donates                covalent
 gp14 + gp15  C +N      ~0.5          transfer of 4 e⁻ impossible    covalent
 gp1  + gp17  Na+Cl      3.0−0.9=2.1  Na 2,8,1 → 2,8   (−1 e⁻)       IONIC   <-- answer (b)
                                      Cl 2,8,7 → 2,8,8 (+1 e⁻)
⚡ JEE Trick For "which pair is most ionic", just compute the periodic-table distance: pick the pair that is farthest apart (leftmost metal + rightmost non-metal). Caesium fluoride, CsF, is the most ionic binary compound known for exactly this reason. Trap: options pairing two elements of the same group look symmetric and tempting, but ΔEN = 0 makes them the least ionic possible.
Answer: (b) Group-1 element and group-17 element.
3Ionic bond formation is favoured when: (a) The metal has high ionisation enthalpy and low lattice energy (b) The non-metal has low electron gain enthalpy (c) The metal has low ionisation enthalpy and the non-metal has high electron gain enthalpy (d) Both atoms have equal electronegativityFactors favouring ionic bondIonisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy and electronegativity for JEE🟡 SimilarcThe metal has low ionisation enthalpy and the non-metal has high electron gain enthalpy
Concept Ionic bond formation is a three-step energy accounting problem, and the bond forms only if the net energy change is negative (energy released). Step 1 costs energy (ionisation), step 2 pays some back (electron gain), step 3 pays back a lot (lattice formation). To make the sum favourable you want the cost small and the payback large — i.e. low ionisation enthalpy for the metal, highly negative electron gain enthalpy for the non-metal, and high lattice enthalpy for the crystal.
Key relation The three steps written in the theory: A(g) + IE → A⁺(g) + e⁻ (absorbed), B(g) + e⁻ → B⁻(g) + EG (released), A⁺(g) + B⁻(g) → AB(s) + LE (released). Overall lowering of energy requires |EG| + |LE| > IE. Since LE is by far the biggest term, a high lattice enthalpy is what usually rescues the balance.
Solution
1. Write the requirement for each factor separately: IE low (cheap to make the cation), EGE high/most negative (lots of energy back on making the anion), LE high (lots of energy back on stacking the lattice).
2. Check option (c) against all three: it demands low IE for the metal ✓ and high EGE for the non-metal ✓ — both correct.
3. Kill (a): high IE makes the cation expensive and low lattice energy removes the payback — this is the recipe for no bond.
4. Kill (b): low electron gain enthalpy means little energy released when the anion forms, again working against the bond.Recall the theory's ordering — anion formation tendency Cl⁻ > F⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ — the species with the most exothermic EGE forms the anion most readily.
5. Kill (d): equal electronegativity ⇒ ΔEN = 0 ⇒ no driving force for transfer at all; that produces a pure covalent bond, the exact opposite of ionic.

 (ii) B(g) + e⁻ → B⁻(g) + EG       RELEASED    large  ⇒ HIGH electron gain enthalpy
 (iii)A⁺(g)+ B⁻(g) → AB(s) + LE    RELEASED    large  ⇒ HIGH lattice enthalpy

4Among the following, which compound will have the strongest ionic character? (a) KCl (b) RbCl (c) LiCl (d) CsClCation size vs ionic characterFast rule for picking the compound with the greatest ionic character🟡 SimilardCsCl
Concept All four compounds are M⁺Cl⁻, so the anion and both charges are held constant — only the cation size changes. Fajans' rules say a small, highly charged cation polarises the anion's electron cloud, dragging electron density back into the internuclear region and injecting covalent character. A big cation has low charge density, polarises feebly, and lets the anion keep its own electrons — so the bond stays cleanly ionic. Going down group 1 the cation grows, so ionic character grows.
Key relation Polarising power ∝ charge / (radius)² = φ (ionic potential); covalent character ∝ polarising power; therefore ionic character ∝ cation size (charge fixed). Ionic radii: Li⁺ 76 pm < Na⁺ 102 < K⁺ 138 < Rb⁺ 152 < Cs⁺ 167 pm. Electronegativity falls the same way: Li 1.0 > K 0.8 > Rb 0.8 > Cs 0.79, so ΔEN with Cl (3.0) is largest for Cs.
Solution
1. Note the common anion Cl⁻ and the common charge +1 — the comparison reduces to one variable, r⁺.
2. Order the cations by size: Li⁺ < K⁺ < Rb⁺ < Cs⁺.
3. Order the polarising power (inverse of size): Li⁺ ≫ K⁺ > Rb⁺ > Cs⁺, so LiCl is the most covalent of the four.
4. Invert to get ionic character: CsCl > RbCl > KCl > LiCl — the strongest ionic character belongs to CsCl, option (d).Cross-check with ΔEN: CsCl gives 3.0 − 0.79 = 2.21, LiCl gives 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0 — the same verdict.

 KCl        K⁺        138        1/138²              2.18       ↑
 RbCl       Rb⁺       152        1/152²              2.18       ↑
 CsCl       Cs⁺       167        1/167² = lowest     2.21    GREATEST   <-- answer (d)

⚡ JEE Trick When every option is MX with the same X and the same charge, do not think — just pick the compound with the biggest cation for "most ionic" and the smallest cation for "most covalent". Trap: LiCl is deliberately placed to catch students who remember "Li is the most reactive-looking alkali metal"; reactivity has nothing to do with it, and LiCl is in fact the least ionic here (it even dissolves in organic solvents like ethanol and acetone).

5A bond is most likely to form when: (a) Repulsive forces dominate and energy increases (b) Attractive forces dominate and energy decreases (c) Both attractive and repulsive forces are equal (d) Electrons cannot redistribute between atomsEnergetics of bond formationChemical bond formation takes place when: energy is absorbed / forces of attraction overcome...🟢 ExactbAttractive forces dominate and energy decreases
Concept When two atoms approach, four electrostatic interactions switch on at once: nucleus-A ↔ electron-B and nucleus-B ↔ electron-A (attractive), plus nucleus ↔ nucleus and electron ↔ electron (repulsive). Whether a bond forms is decided by the net of these four. If attraction wins, the total potential energy of the pair falls below the energy of the two isolated atoms, and that energy difference is released to the surroundings — bond formation is therefore always exothermic. If repulsion wins, the energy rises and the atoms simply bounce apart.
Key relation Stability ∝ 1 / Energy — the lower the energy, the more stable the system. E(molecule) < E(separate atoms) ⇒ bond forms, ΔE released. The theory states it directly: "a molecule will only be formed if it is more stable and has a lower energy than the individual atoms", and "decrease in energy is proportional to strength of the bond".
Solution
1. List the four interactions as the atoms approach and take the net result.
2. If the net result is attraction, the system slides down the potential-energy curve to a minimum at the bond length r₀ — energy decreases, a bond forms. That is option (b).
3. Eliminate (a): if repulsion dominated, E would rise; a higher-energy arrangement is less stable and the atoms separate again.
4. Eliminate (c): equal attraction and repulsion gives zero net force and zero energy change — no driving force, hence no bond.
5. Eliminate (d): the theory defines a chemical bond as "the union of two or more atoms involving redistribution of electrons"; if electrons cannot redistribute, by definition no bond can form.
 interactions on approach          sign
 nucleus_A  ↔  electron_B          attractive  (−)
 nucleus_B  ↔  electron_A          attractive  (−)
 nucleus_A  ↔  nucleus_B           repulsive   (+)
 electron_A ↔  electron_B          repulsive   (+)
 -------------------------------------------------
 net attractive  ⇒  E_molecule < E_atoms  ⇒  ΔE released (exothermic)
                 ⇒  bond FORMS, bond energy = depth of the well    <-- answer (b)
 net repulsive   ⇒  E rises  ⇒  no bond
⚡ JEE Trick Bond formation is always exothermic and bond breaking always endothermic — one sentence that answers a whole family of MCQs (including L4-Q9 and L5-Q1 in this same lecture). Add the proportionality "more energy released = stronger bond = more stable molecule". Trap: option (c) sounds like the equilibrium bond length (where the net force is zero) — but at r₀ the energy is at its minimum, i.e. it has already decreased; "equal forces" is not the criterion for whether a bond forms.
Answer: (b) Attractive forces dominate and energy decreases.

1What is a chemical bond?Definition of chemical bondChemical bonding for JEE — what a bond is and why atoms combine🟡 Similar—
Concept A board answer for "what is a chemical bond" must contain three ingredients, not one: (i) the force statement — what physically holds the atoms together; (ii) the mechanism statement — that the union involves a redistribution of electrons; and (iii) the energy statement — that the process lowers the energy of the system and is therefore exothermic. Most students write only (i) and lose the other two marks. Adding the proportionality "more decrease in energy ⇒ stronger bond" shows you understand why molecules exist at all.
Definition A chemical bond is the force that acts between two or more atoms and holds them together as a stable molecule. Equivalent wording used in the theory: "it is the union of two or more atoms involving redistribution of electrons among them; this process is accompanied by a decrease in energy." Bond strength ∝ decrease in energy.
Solution
1. Force definition: a chemical bond is the attractive force that acts between two or more atoms and holds them together as a stable molecule.
2. Mechanism: it is the union of two or more atoms involving a redistribution of electrons — either complete transfer (ionic), sharing (covalent), one-sided sharing (co-ordinate) or delocalisation (metallic).
3. Energetics: the process is accompanied by a decrease in energy; energy is released, so bond formation is exothermic.A molecule forms only if it is more stable and lower in energy than the separate atoms — this is why, except for the inert gases, no element exists as free atoms under ordinary conditions.
4. Strength rule: the decrease in energy is proportional to the strength of the bond — the greater the decrease, the stronger the bond and the more stable the molecule relative to its atoms.
5. Classification (worth adding): strong inter-atomic bonds (≈200 kJ/mol) — ionic, covalent, co-ordinate, metallic; weak inter-molecular bonds (≈2.42 kJ/mol) — hydrogen bond and van der Waals bond.

⚡ JEE Trick Answer definition questions with the force + mechanism + energy triple; each phrase is a separate mark in a board scheme. Keep the two energy numbers (200 vs 2.42 kJ/mol) handy — they convert the same definition into an MCQ answer about which bond is strongest. Trap: writing only "attraction between two atoms" omits the electron redistribution and the energy lowering, which is where most of the marks live.

2Explain the formation of ionic bond in the molecule of calcium chloride.Formation of CaCl₂Formation of calcium chloride CaCl₂ — ionic bond, step by step🟢 Exact—
Concept Calcium chloride is the standard example of a 1 : 2 ionic compound, and it is chosen because the electron bookkeeping does not balance one-to-one. Ca must shed two electrons to reach an argon core, but each Cl can accept only one. Charge conservation therefore forces two chlorine atoms per calcium — and that, not any "molecular" arrangement, is where the formula CaCl₂ comes from. Note carefully: CaCl₂ is an empirical formula describing a 3-D lattice; ionic compounds have no molecular formula.
Key relation Ca (2,8,8,2) → Ca²⁺ (2,8,8) + 2e⁻ and 2 × [Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8)], giving Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → CaCl₂. Both product ions have the argon configuration 2,8,8. Electrovalency of Ca = 2 (two electrons lost); electrovalency of Cl = 1 (one electron gained).
Solution
1. Write the configurations. Ca (Z = 20) = 2,8,8,2; Cl (Z = 17) = 2,8,7.
2. Decide the direction of transfer. Ca is a group-2 metal with low ionisation enthalpy — it loses its two 4s electrons. Cl is a group-17 non-metal with a highly negative electron gain enthalpy — it gains one electron.
3. Balance the electrons. Ca releases 2 e⁻ but each Cl absorbs only 1 e⁻, so one Ca needs two Cl atoms. Ca donates one electron to each chlorine.
4. Form the ions. Ca²⁺ = 2,8,8 (argon configuration); each Cl⁻ = 2,8,8 (argon configuration). Every species now has a complete octet.Ca²⁺ is 20 protons with 18 electrons ⇒ +2; each Cl⁻ is 17 protons with 18 electrons ⇒ −1. Net charge = (+2) + 2(−1) = 0, so the crystal is electrically neutral.
5. Form the bond. The Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are then held by the electrostatic force of attraction — the ionic (electrovalent) bond. Because that force acts equally in all directions, the ions stack into a 3-D crystal lattice, and the energy released per mole of lattice is the lattice energy.
6. State the electrovalencies: electrovalency of Ca = 2, electrovalency of Cl = 1; hence the empirical formula CaCl₂ (also obtainable by the criss-cross rule from Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻).

 Cl (1st)  2, 8, 7         + 1 e⁻     2, 8, 8        −1       Ar
 Cl (2nd)  2, 8, 7         + 1 e⁻     2, 8, 8        −1       Ar
 ------------------------------------------------------------------

 charge balance   :  (+2) + 2(−1) = 0                   ✓

3Explain the formation of ionic bonds on the basis of ionization enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy.IE, EGE and lattice enthalpyIonic bond, ionisation enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy — Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure🟢 Exact—
Concept Ionic bond formation is not a single event but an energy balance sheet with three entries. Making the cation costs energy (ionisation enthalpy, IE). Making the anion returns some (electron gain enthalpy, EG). Assembling the gaseous ions into a crystal returns a large amount (lattice enthalpy, LE). The compound exists only if the books close with a net release — "overall lowering of energy". The reason group-1 chlorides form so readily while, say, a hypothetical CCl₄⁴⁻ salt does not, is entirely visible in this arithmetic.
Key relation (i) A(g) + IE → A⁺(g) + e⁻ — energy absorbed. (ii) B(g) + e⁻ → B⁻(g) + EG — energy released. (iii) A⁺(g) + B⁻(g) → AB(s) + LE — energy released. Net condition: |EG| + |LE| > IE. Lattice enthalpy is by far the largest term, which is why an "expensive" cation such as Mg²⁺ or Al³⁺ is still worth making: its higher charge multiplies the lattice enthalpy.
Solution
1. Ionisation enthalpy (metal). Defined as the energy required to remove an electron from the outermost orbit of an isolated gaseous atom to form the cation — energy is absorbed. Lesser IE ⇒ greater tendency to form the cation ⇒ greater tendency to form an ionic compound.Cation formation tendency follows Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺ (removing successive electrons costs more each time) and Cs⁺ > Rb⁺ > K⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺ (IE falls down the group as the valence electron gets farther from the nucleus).
2. Electron gain enthalpy (non-metal). Defined as the energy released when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom to form the anion. A higher (more negative) value ⇒ greater tendency to form the anion ⇒ greater tendency to form an ionic compound.Anion formation tendency follows Cl⁻ > F⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ and F⁻ > O²⁻ > N³⁻ — note that Cl beats F because F's tiny 2p shell suffers strong inter-electronic repulsion.
3. Lattice enthalpy (the crystal). The energy released when one mole of crystal lattice is formed from isolated gaseous ions. Higher lattice energy ⇒ greater stability and strength of the ionic compound; a more negative lattice enthalpy favours formation.
4. Combine. Add the three: the bond forms only if the total is exothermic, i.e. the energy given back by steps (ii) and (iii) exceeds the energy paid out in step (i).
5. Conclusion. An ionic bond is favoured by low ionisation enthalpy of the metal, high (highly negative) electron gain enthalpy of the non-metal and high lattice enthalpy of the resulting crystal — precisely the situation for a group-1/group-2 metal with a group-16/group-17 non-metal.

Trap: quoting F as having the highest electron gain enthalpy. The book's own ordering is Cl > F > Br > I — chlorine, not fluorine, tops the list.
Key point: ionic bonding needs low ionisation enthalpy (cheap cation), high electron gain enthalpy (energy-rich anion) and high lattice enthalpy (energy-rich crystal), so that |EG| + |LE| > IE and the overall process releases energy.
4Distinguish between Ionic bond and Covalent bond.Ionic vs covalentDifference Between Ionic Bond and Covalent Bond - Nature of Chemical Bond - Chemistry Class 11🟢 Exact—
Concept The whole distinction flows from one difference in electron bookkeeping — transfer versus sharing — and everything else (directionality, physical state, melting point, conductivity, solubility, formula type) is a downstream consequence. Answer this question as a table with the cause in the first row and the consequences below it, and each row scores. The controlling variable is the electronegativity difference: large ΔEN forces transfer, small ΔEN permits sharing.
Key relation Ionic: A → A⁺ + e⁻ ; B + e⁻ → B⁻ ; A⁺ + B⁻ (electrostatic), requires ΔEN > 1.7. Covalent: A· + ·B → A:B (one electron contributed by each, the pair shared), typical of ΔEN < 1.7. Because a shared pair is localised along the A–B axis, a covalent bond is directional; because an electrostatic field is spherically symmetric, an ionic bond is non-directional.
Solution
1. Mechanism: ionic = complete transfer of one or more electrons; covalent = mutual sharing of an electron pair.
2. Formed between: ionic = metal + non-metal (large ΔEN, e.g. group 1/2 with group 16/17); covalent = non-metal + non-metal (small ΔEN).
3. Nature of the force: ionic = electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, equal in all directions ⇒ non-directional; covalent = overlap of orbitals along a fixed axis ⇒ directional, so covalent compounds have definite shapes and show isomerism.
4. Physical state and melting/boiling points: ionic compounds are hard, crystalline, brittle solids with high m.p./b.p. because of the strong electrostatic lattice; covalent compounds are usually gases, liquids or soft solids with low m.p./b.p. because only weak inter-molecular forces have to be overcome.
5. Electrical conductivity: ionic compounds do not conduct as solids (ions locked in the lattice) but conduct in the fused state and in aqueous solution (free ions); order solid < fused < aqueous. Covalent compounds are non-conductors in every state — there are no free ions.
6. Solubility: ionic compounds are highly soluble in polar solvents such as water and poorly soluble in non-polar solvents such as benzene; covalent compounds are the other way round.Concrete test from the theory: NaCl forms a true solution in water but only a colloid in benzene, and NaCl gives a white AgCl precipitate with AgNO₃ (free Cl⁻ ions) whereas covalent CCl₄ does not.
7. Formula: ionic compounds have only an empirical formula (NaCl describes a lattice ratio, not a molecule); covalent compounds have genuine molecular formulas.

Concept Fajans' rules describe how covalent character creeps into an ionic bond through polarisation. Two properties matter: the cation's polarising power (how hard it pulls on the anion's cloud, ∝ charge/size, so small cations polarise strongly) and the anion's polarisability (how easily its cloud deforms, so large anions deform easily). Maximum covalent character therefore comes from a small cation with a large anion. Turn both around — large cation, small anion — and you get the least polarisation and hence the most ionic bond.
Key relation covalent character ∝ (polarising power of cation) × (polarisability of anion) ∝ (q⁺/r⁺) × r⁻. So ionic character ∝ r⁺ / (q⁺ · r⁻). Metals that hold their electrons tightly (Au, Pt, Hg) form covalent rather than ionic compounds.
Solution
1. (a) Low ionisation enthalpy ✓ — the theory says "lesser ionisation energy implies greater tendency to form cation implies greater tendency to form ionic compound".
2. (b) High electron gain enthalpy ✓ — "higher value of negative electron gain enthalpy implies greater tendency to form anion implies greater tendency to form ionic compound".
3. (c) High lattice enthalpy ✓ — "higher lattice energy implies greater the stability or strength of ionic compound; higher negative value of lattice enthalpy favours ionic compound formation".
4. (d) Strongly held outermost electrons ✗ — this says the metal resists ionisation, i.e. IE is high, which directly contradicts (a). A metal that will not give up its electrons cannot form a cation, so no ionic bond forms.Cross-check with the theory's ordering Cs⁺ > Rb⁺ > K⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺: caesium holds its 6s electron most loosely and is therefore the best cation former.
5. Correct set = a, b, c.
 factor                                          direction wanted   in the ledger      verdict
 (a) metal ionisation enthalpy LOW               low               A(g)+IE→A⁺(g)+e⁻    ✓
 (b) non-metal electron gain enthalpy HIGH       high              B(g)+e⁻→B⁻(g)+EG    ✓
 (c) lattice enthalpy HIGH                       high              A⁺+B⁻→AB(s)+LE      ✓
 (d) metal holds outer e⁻ STRONGLY               = high IE          contradicts (a)     ✗

 worked example (NaCl, kJ/mol)
   +496  ionise Na            (low IE helps make this small)
   −349  Cl gains an electron (high EGE makes this large)
   −788  lattice formation    (high LE makes this large)
   ------
   −641  net, exothermic ⇒ ionic bond forms
 ⇒ correct options : a, b, c
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the triple LOW IE · HIGH EGE · HIGH LE — it answers Suggested-Q3, L3-Q3, L4-Q8, L4-Q10 and this question, five items from one line. In multiple-correct format, scan for the option that restates an earlier option in reverse; here (d) is (a) turned upside down, and two mutually contradictory options can never both be correct. Trap: (d) sounds like a virtue — "strongly held" suggests stability — but stability of the atom is the enemy of ionic bond formation.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c) — low ionisation enthalpy, high electron gain enthalpy and high lattice enthalpy. (d) contradicts (a) and is false.
