1Which vdW constant represents the effect of intermolecular attractions? (a/b/R/T)Meaning of aA-CONST🟡 Similaraconstant a (intermolecular attraction)
Concept The van der Waals equation patches the ideal-gas law for the two ways a real gas departs from ideality. (i) Molecules attract one another, so the wall is struck a little softer than kinetic theory predicts — this is repaired by the pressure term an²/V². (ii) Molecules occupy space, so the free volume is less than V — repaired by the volume term nb. Constant a is therefore the "stickiness" of the gas, and b is its "bulkiness"; R and T are not gas-specific correction constants at all.
Formula (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. Here a carries units atm L² mol⁻² and scales the attraction correction; b carries units L mol⁻¹ and scales the size correction. Only the term multiplied by a describes intermolecular attraction, so a is the constant asked for.
Solution
1. Read off what each symbol does in the equation:a → pressure (attraction) correction; b → volume (size) correction; R → universal gas constant; T → temperature variable.
2. Intermolecular attraction lives only in the term an²/V², whose gas-specific constant is a.
3. Eliminate the rest: b measures molecular volume, R is the same for every gas, T is a state variable — none of them encodes attraction. So the answer is (a).
 constant   what it corrects       physical meaning         units
 a          pressure (an²/V²)      strength of attraction   atm·L²·mol⁻²
 b          volume  (nb)           molecular size / volume  L·mol⁻¹
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise "a = Attraction, b = Bulk." A larger a ⇒ stronger forces ⇒ higher critical temperature ⇒ the gas liquefies more easily — a is the single most-tested van der Waals constant. Trap: R looks like a "constant" too, but it is universal, not a property of the particular gas.
Answer: (a) constant a — it scales the intermolecular-attraction (pressure) correction.
2b = 4× actual volume of 1 mol; molecular radius r — which expression is b?Excluded volumeA-EXCL🟡 Similarb4 × (4/3)πr³NA
Concept Constant b (the "co-volume") is the volume that one mole of molecules denies to the others because two hard spheres cannot interpenetrate. It is a famous result that this excluded volume is four times the actual volume of the molecules themselves — not one times. So the question is really "actual molar volume of the spheres, multiplied by 4."
Formula Volume of one spherical molecule = (4/3)πr³; for one mole multiply by NA; then the excluded (co-)volume is 4× that: b = 4 × (4/3)πr³ × NA. The factor 4, derived below, is what distinguishes the correct option from the "1×" distractor.
Solution
1. Actual volume of a single molecule (a sphere of radius r) = (4/3)πr³.
2. Per mole, multiply by Avogadro's number: molar volume of the molecules = (4/3)πr³·NA.
3. Derive the factor 4: the centres of two molecules of radius r cannot come closer than 2r, so each pair excludes a sphere of radius 2r:excluded (pair) volume = (4/3)π(2r)³ = 8 × (4/3)πr³ — eight molecular volumes.
4. That 8× belongs to two molecules, so per molecule the excluded volume is 8/2 = 4 molecular volumes.
5. Hence b = 4 × (4/3)πr³ × NA, which is option (b).

 excluded sphere:  (4/3)π(2r)³ = 8·(4/3)πr³   (per pair)
 per molecule:     8 / 2 = 4 molecular volumes
 per mole:         b = 4·(4/3)πr³·N_A
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the headline number: b = 4 × (molar volume of the molecules). From a measured b you can back out the molecular radius via (4/3)πr³ = b/(4NA). Trap: option "1 × (4/3)πr³NA" forgets the factor 4 — the single commonest error on this question.
Answer: (b) 4 × (4/3)πr³NA — four times the actual molar volume of the spheres.

Concept In the van der Waals equation the bracket (P + an²/V²) is the pressure a gas would exert if there were no attractions — i.e. the "ideal" kinetic pressure on the wall. The measured (observed) pressure P is smaller, because attractions pull the molecules inward and soften each wall impact. To recover the ideal-gas pressure from the observed one you simply add back the lost pressure, the correction term an²/V². Constant b (finite size) plays no part in the pressure correction, so it is a distractor here.
Formula Pideal = Pobs + an²/V², with a in atm L² mol⁻², V in L, n in mol. The an²/V² term is exactly the pressure "stolen" by attractions; adding it undoes that loss. Given n = 1, a = 3.6, V = 4 L, Pobs = 9.5 atm.
Solution
1. Compute the pressure-correction term: an²/V² = 3.6 × 1² ÷ 4² = 3.6 ÷ 16 = 0.225 atm.n = 1 so n² = 1; V² = 16.
2. Add it to the observed pressure: Pideal = 9.5 + 0.225 = 9.725 atm ≈ 9.8 atm.The ideal pressure exceeds the observed one because attractions were reducing the real pressure.
3. Note b = 0.04 is unused — the size correction affects volume, not this pressure calculation.
 P_obs        = 9.5   atm
 an²/V²       = 3.6/16 = 0.225 atm
 P_ideal      = 9.5 + 0.225 = 9.725 ≈ 9.8 atm
⚡ JEE Trick "Ideal pressure from observed" ⇒ add a/V²; "observed pressure from ideal" ⇒ subtract a/V². Spot the direction from which pressure is larger — the ideal (attraction-free) pressure is always the bigger of the two. Trap: don't waste time on b in a pure pressure-correction problem; it only enters through (V − nb).
Answer: (a) 9.8 atm (Pobs + a/V² = 9.5 + 0.225).
4At moderate pressure Z < 1 — which vdW explanation is correct?Compressibility factor Z<1A-REALZ🟡 SimilarbAttractive forces dominate as a/V² reduces the effective pressure.
Concept The compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT measures how far a gas strays from ideal (where Z = 1). At moderate pressures the molecules are close enough that attractions matter but not so close that their finite size matters — so the gas is more compressible than ideal and Z drops below 1. In van der Waals language, the attraction term an²/V² lowers the effective (measured) pressure, which is exactly what pushes Z under 1.
Formula Z = PV/nRT; Z < 1 ⇒ attractions dominate; Z > 1 ⇒ molecular size/repulsion dominates. Attractions reduce P below the ideal value, so PV < nRT and Z < 1.
Solution
1. Set the reference: an ideal gas has Z = 1 at every P and T.
2. At moderate pressure the dominant real-gas effect is intermolecular attraction; the size term nb is still tiny beside V.Molecules are near enough to pull on each other but the free volume is barely reduced.
3. Attraction lowers the wall pressure by an²/V², so PV < nRT ⇒ Z < 1 — the gas is easier to compress than an ideal gas.
4. This is precisely option (b): attractive forces dominate as a/V² reduces the effective pressure.
 regime            dominant effect     Z
 low  P            almost ideal        ≈ 1
 moderate P        attraction (a/V²)   < 1   ← this question
 very high P       size / repulsion(b) > 1
⚡ JEE Trick One-line rule: Z < 1 → attractions (a) win; Z > 1 → size (b) wins. H₂ and He, having almost no attraction, show Z > 1 at essentially all pressures. Trap: a dip below 1 does not mean the gas is "ideal at that point" — it is the maximum deviation caused by attractions.
Answer: (b) Attractive forces dominate as a/V² reduces the effective pressure (so Z < 1).

1Explain liquefaction of gases and critical constantsLiquefaction / critical constantsA-LIQ🟡 Similar—
Concept A gas liquefies when its molecules are brought close enough for their mutual attractions to overcome their thermal (kinetic) energy. Two things help: lowering the temperature (less kinetic energy to escape the attractions) and raising the pressure (pushing molecules together). But cooling is decisive: above a certain temperature no amount of pressure can produce a liquid. That threshold is the critical temperature, and it, with the critical pressure and volume, makes up the critical constants.
Key relation Tc = 8a/(27Rb), Pc = a/(27b²), Vc = 3b. All three follow from the van der Waals equation's point of inflection at the critical point (∂P/∂V = ∂²P/∂V² = 0).
Solution
1. Critical temperature Tc: the temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone. Above Tc only a "gas/supercritical fluid" exists.Larger attractions (larger a) raise Tc, so such gases liquefy more readily.
2. Critical pressure Pc: the pressure needed to liquefy the gas exactly at Tc — the minimum pressure that produces liquid at the critical temperature.
3. Critical volume Vc: the molar volume of the substance at Tc and Pc, where liquid and vapour become indistinguishable (one phase).
4. Andrews' CO₂ isotherms show the picture: below Tc a horizontal step marks gas↔liquid coexistence; at Tc that step shrinks to a single point (the critical point); above Tc the isotherms are smooth with no liquefaction.
 constant   formula        physical meaning
 T_c        8a/27Rb        above it, no liquefaction by pressure
 P_c        a/27b²         pressure to liquefy at T_c
 V_c        3b             molar volume at the critical point
⚡ JEE Trick "Easy to liquefy" ⇔ "high Tc" ⇔ "large a" (strong attractions): NH₃, CO₂, SO₂ liquefy easily; H₂, He, N₂ do not. Remember Zc = PcVc/RTc = 3/8 = 0.375 for every van der Waals gas.
Key point: a gas liquefies only below its critical temperature Tc = 8a/27Rb; the critical constants (Tc, Pc = a/27b², Vc = 3b) fix the point where liquid and vapour merge into one phase.

1The van der Waals equation for n moles is …vdW equation formA-DERIV🟡 Similarc(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT
Concept The n-mole van der Waals equation is the ideal-gas law with two corrections. Pressure is corrected upward by an²/V² (attractions lower the measured P), and volume is corrected downward by nb (molecules occupy space). The scaling with n matters: the attraction term goes as n² (it depends on the square of concentration), while the size term goes as n.
Formula (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. The pressure correction ∝ (density)² = (n/V)² ⇒ an²/V²; the volume correction ∝ number of molecules ⇒ nb.
Solution
1. Start from ideal gas PV = nRT and add attraction: the true wall pressure is P + an²/V².Attraction between molecules reduces the impact pressure by an amount proportional to (n/V)².
2. Subtract the excluded volume: the space actually available to move in is V − nb, not V.
3. Combine: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT — option (c).
4. Reject the distractors: any option using n (not n²) in the pressure term, or +nb instead of −nb, has the wrong scaling or wrong sign.
 correction     ideal   →   van der Waals
 pressure       P       →   P + a n²/V²   (attraction)
 volume         V       →   V − n b       (molecular size)
 result:        (P + a n²/V²)(V − n b) = nRT
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the scaling: pressure term carries n², volume term carries n. For 1 mole it collapses to the familiar (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT. Trap: options that write an/V² (only one power of n) or (V + nb) are the standard traps.
Answer: (c) (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.
2At high T & low P, the vdW equation reduces to …Limiting behaviour → idealA-REALZ🟡 SimilaraPV = nRT
Concept A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure. At low pressure the molecules are far apart, so the space they occupy (nb) is negligible beside the huge volume V; at high temperature their kinetic energy swamps the weak attractions, so an²/V² is negligible beside P. With both corrections vanishing, van der Waals collapses back to PV = nRT.
Formula Start from (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. As P→low, V→large ⇒ an²/V² → 0 and nb ≪ V. Dropping both small terms leaves PV = nRT.
Solution
1. Low pressure ⇒ large molar volume ⇒ the size term nb is tiny next to V, so (V − nb) ≈ V.
2. High temperature ⇒ large kinetic energy ⇒ attractions negligible, and with large V the term an²/V² → 0, so (P + an²/V²) ≈ P.an²/V² falls off as 1/V², so it dies quickly as V grows.
3. Substitute both approximations: (P)(V) = nRT ⇒ PV = nRT — option (a).
 term            at high T, low P        →
 an²/V²          → 0   (V large)         drop
 nb              ≪ V   (V large)         drop
 result:         P · V = nRT   (ideal)
⚡ JEE Trick "Ideal-gas limit" = high T + low P (large volume). This is also why H₂, He look nearly ideal at ordinary conditions — small a and b. Trap: high pressure does the opposite — there the nb term grows and Z rises above 1.
Answer: (a) PV = nRT.
