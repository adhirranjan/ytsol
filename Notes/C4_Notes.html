L-1

Development of the Periodic Table & Modern Classification

Early attempts

  • Lavoisier: simplest split into metals (tend to lose e⁻, e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻) and non-metals (tend to gain e⁻, e.g. F + e⁻ → F⁻). Could not place metalloids.
  • Döbereiner's Triads (1817): groups of three like elements where the atomic weight of the middle ≈ average of the other two (e.g. Cl 35.5, Br 80, I 127 → (35.5+127)/2 = 81.2 ≈ 80). Failed for most elements.
  • Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865): arranged by increasing atomic mass; every 8th element resembled the 1st (like musical octaves). Valid only up to Ca; broke down when noble gases were later found.
  • Lothar Meyer (1869): plotted atomic volume vs atomic weight — alkali metals sit on the peaks, halogens on the ascending part, metalloids & transition metals at the bottom. Physical properties are a periodic function of atomic weight.
  • Mendeleev (1869): "physical & chemical properties are a periodic function of atomic weight." First systematic table — 7 periods, 8 groups (A & B subgroups); left gaps & predicted eka-elements. Anomalies: Ar–K, Co–Ni pairs (mass order wrong), isotopes, H placement.

Modern (long form) periodic table

  • Moseley's modern periodic law: properties are a periodic function of the atomic number (Z), not atomic weight — this fixed the Ar–K and Co–Ni anomalies and gave each isotope one place.
  • Long form: 7 periods, 18 groups. Period number = principal quantum number of the valence shell.
  • Blocks (by orbital receiving the last electron): s-block (gp 1–2, ns¹⁻², soft reactive metals), p-block (gp 13–18, ns²np¹⁻⁶, metals + metalloids + non-metals), d-block / transition (gp 3–12, (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns⁰⁻², coloured ions, variable valency), f-block / inner-transition (lanthanoids 4f, actinoids 5f, (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴(n−1)d⁰⁻¹ns²).
Predicting group, period & block from the configuration
Period = highest principal quantum number n. Block = subshell of the last electron. Group: s-block → (valence e⁻); p-block → 10 + (valence e⁻); d-block → (electrons in (n−1)d + ns); f-block → always 3 (III B).
💡 Handy factsLongest group = group 3 / III B (32 elements, incl. 14 lanthanoids + 14 actinoids); longest period = 6th (32 elements). Most gaseous elements sit in the 2nd period (N, O, F, Ne). Only 6 liquid elements (Cs, Fr, Ga, Hg, Br + element 112); Br is the only liquid non-metal. Transuranics (Z > 92) are all radioactive & man-made; first man-made element = Tc, first man-made lanthanoid = Pm.
⚠ TrapZn, Cd, Hg have (n−1)d¹⁰ns² (completely filled d in all oxidation states) so they are not typical transition elements. A "transition element" must have a partially-filled d-subshell in the atom or in one of its ions.

L-2

Effective Nuclear Charge, Shielding & Atomic Radius

Effective nuclear charge & shielding

  • An outer electron feels attraction from the nucleus minus repulsion from inner electrons. The net pull is the effective nuclear charge Z* = Z − σ.
  • Screening / shielding effect: inner electrons "screen" the valence electron from the full nuclear charge. More inner electrons → more shielding → smaller Z*.
Slater's shielding constant σσ = 0.35 × (other e⁻ in ns,np group) + 0.85 × (e⁻ in (n−1) shell) + 1.00 × (e⁻ in (n−2) & inner) [1s uses 0.30]
Worked σ (from the module)
₇N (1s²)(2s,2p)⁵, valence in 2p: σ = 0.35×4 + 0.85×2 = 3.10, Z* = 7 − 3.10 = 3.90.
₃₀Zn for a 4s electron: σ = 0.35×1 + 0.85×18 + 1.00×10 = 25.65, Z* = 4.35; for a 3d electron: σ = 1.00×18 + 0.85×9 = 21.15... note d-rules: electrons left of nd count 1.00 → Z* = 8.85.

Atomic radius & its four types

  • Hard to measure directly (no sharp boundary), so it is taken as half the internuclear distance (bond length) between two like bonded atoms.
  • Covalent radius (for non-metals) = ½ × internuclear distance of two covalently-bonded like atoms; e.g. Br–Br = 2.284 Å → rBr = 1.142 Å. For a given element: single > double > triple bond radius (C: 0.77, 0.67, 0.60 Å).
  • van der Waals radius (noble gases / non-bonded atoms) = ½ the distance between adjacent non-bonded atoms in the solid; ≈ 2 × covalent radius (largest).
  • Metallic (crystal) radius = ½ distance between adjacent metal atoms in the close-packed lattice.
  • Ionic radius — cation/anion (covered in L-3).
radius order & heteronuclear bondvan der Waals > metallic > covalent · Schomaker–Stevenson: dA–B = rA + rB − 0.09(χA − χB)
💡 TrendAcross a period atomic radius decreases (Zeff rises, same shell); down a group it increases (extra shells outweigh the rising nuclear charge).

L-3

Ionic Radius, Factors & Isoelectronic Species

  • Cationic radius < parent atom: losing electrons keeps the same protons acting on fewer electrons (Zeff ↑), and often the whole outer shell is removed. Size of cation ∝ 1/Zeff. e.g. Fe > Fe²⁺ > Fe³⁺; Mn > Mn²⁺ > … > Mn⁷⁺.
  • Anionic radius > parent atom: extra electrons increase inter-electron repulsion & screening, lowering Zeff, so the cloud expands. e.g. for F: Zeff falls from 9/9 = 1 (F) to 9/10 = 0.9 (F⁻), hence F⁻ > F.
  • Factors on atomic size: (i) number of shells (more shells → bigger) and (ii) nuclear charge (more Z → smaller).
  • Variation of ionic radii follows the atomic radii: decreases across a period, increases down a group.

Isoelectronic species

  • Species with the same number of electrons. Among them, radius ∝ 1/(nuclear charge): more protons pull the same electron cloud in tighter.
  • e.g. O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ (all 10 e⁻; Z = 8, 9, 11, 12) → Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻.
key relationscation < atom < anion · isoelectronic: ionic radius ∝ 1 / nuclear charge
★ s-block size mapSmallest = Li (top of group 1); largest = Cs (bottom). Down each group the radius grows (Li→Cs, Be→Ba); across, group 1 > group 2.

Book Example · L-3
Why is the radius of a cation always smaller and that of an anion always larger than the parent atom?
In a cation the unchanged nuclear charge acts on fewer electrons, pulling them closer, so the ion shrinks.
In an anion the same nuclear charge acts on more electrons; each is held less tightly and the electron cloud expands.
Cation < atom < anion — governed by the electron-to-proton ratio (Zeff).
L-4

Ionisation Enthalpy (IE)

  • Definition: minimum energy to remove an electron from the valence shell of an isolated gaseous atom. Always endothermic (positive).
  • Successive IE: IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃ … — after each removal the cation's Zeff rises, so the next electron is harder to pull out. (Values in kJ mol⁻¹, kcal/g-atom or eV/atom; derived from Born–Haber cycles.)
successive ionisationM(g) → M⁺ + e⁻ (IE₁); M⁺ → M²⁺ + e⁻ (IE₂); M²⁺ → M³⁺ + e⁻ (IE₃) with IE₃ > IE₂ > IE₁

Factors affecting IE

  • Effective nuclear charge: IE ∝ Zeff.
  • Atomic size: bigger atom → weaker pull → lower IE.
  • Subshell: for a given shell s < p < d < f in energy (s closest to nucleus), so IE order of removal is s > p > d > f — why IE₁(B) < IE₁(Be).
  • Stable configuration: half-filled / fully-filled configs are extra stable → higher IE. So IE₁(N) > IE₁(O), but IE₂(O) > IE₂(N).
  • Shielding: more screening → lower IE.

Trends & the classic exceptions

  • Across a period IE generally increases (Zeff ↑); down a group it decreases (size ↑).
  • Group 2 → 13 dip (Be→B, Mg→Al, Ca→Ga): the electron leaves an easier np orbital instead of the tightly held ns².
  • Group 15 → 16 dip (N→O, P→S, As→Se): the half-filled np³ is stable, so IE of group 15 exceeds group 16.
  • These dips shrink from period 2 → 4 (d-electron shielding). Also Pd→Ag drops (Pd's 4d¹⁰5s⁰ electron is closer/harder than Ag's 5s¹).
⚠ Trap2nd IE of a group-1 metal is huge (removing from a noble-gas core), while the 2nd IE of a group-2 metal is modest — a big jump in successive IE reveals the number of valence electrons (hence the group).

L-5

Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegH)

  • Definition: energy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). Usually negative (energy released) — most negative for halogens (ns²np⁵ wanting a noble-gas shell).
  • Noble gases: filled ns²np⁶ — an added electron must enter the next shell, so ΔegH is large positive.
  • Successive ΔegH: the 1st is generally negative, but the 2nd (adding to an already-negative ion) is positive — energy is needed to overcome repulsion. e.g. O + e⁻ → O⁻ is −141 kJ, O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻ is +780 kJ.

Factors & trends

  • Atomic size ↑ → less negative; nuclear charge ↑ → more negative; stable half/fully-filled shells (gp 2, 15, 18) → low or positive.
  • Across a period ΔegH becomes more negative; down a group it becomes less negative.
  • Key exception: 2nd-period N, O, F are less negative than the 3rd-period P, S, Cl below them — the small n = 2 shell crowds the incoming electron (extra repulsion). Hence Cl has the most negative ΔegH of all elements, and among halogens the order is F < Cl (Cl more negative).
💡 EGE ≠ ENElectron gain enthalpy is a property of an isolated atom gaining an electron; electronegativity is the pull of a bonded atom on a shared pair.

L-6

Electronegativity (EN)

  • Definition: the tendency of a bonded atom to attract the shared pair of electrons towards itself. It is a relative (unitless) property, not directly measurable.
  • Pauling scale (from bond energies): χA − χB = 0.208√Δ, where Δ = EA–B − √(EA–A·EB–B). Fluorine is the reference at χ = 4.0 (most electronegative element).
  • Mulliken scale (from atomic constants): χM = (IE + EA)/2. Mulliken values ≈ 2.8 × Pauling values.
the two scalesPauling: Δχ = 0.208√Δ · Mulliken: χ = (IE + EA)/2 · χPauling ≈ χMulliken / 2.8

Factors & periodic trend

  • Smaller size / higher Zeff → higher EN; higher s-character of the hybrid orbital (sp > sp² > sp³) and higher oxidation state → higher EN.
  • Across a period EN increases; down a group it decreases. F is the most electronegative; the least electronegative (excluding inert gases) are the heavy alkali metals (Cs, Fr).

Applications

  • Nature of a bond: large Δχ → ionic; small Δχ → covalent. Percentage ionic character rises with Δχ.
  • Bond length of a polar bond (Schomaker–Stevenson): dA–B = rA + rB − 0.09(χA − χB).
  • Metallic vs non-metallic character and acidic vs basic oxides: low EN → metallic / basic oxide; high EN → non-metallic / acidic oxide.
⚠ TrapElectronegativity is not a fixed atomic constant — it depends on the atom's hybridisation, oxidation state and the atoms it is bonded to. ΔegH (a single-atom energy) is the closest measurable cousin, but the two are conceptually different.
