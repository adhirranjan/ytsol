Concept A point in the x–y plane is fixed by an ordered pair (x, y): x is the signed perpendicular distance from the y-axis (positive to the right), y is the signed perpendicular distance from the x-axis (positive upwards). The two axes cut the plane into four quadrants, and the signs of x and y alone decide which quadrant a point falls in. Concept The straight-line distance between two points is nothing but the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal gap Δx and the vertical gap Δy. Dropping a horizontal from one point and a vertical from the other builds that triangle automatically, so Pythagoras' theorem gives the distance directly. Because both gaps get squared, the order in which you subtract makes no difference — distance is always positive.
Formula d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²], with x, y in grid units and d in the same units of length. Squaring kills the sign of each gap, so d ≥ 0 always; the square root restores the unit from unit² back to unit.

1. (i) (−2, 0) & (1, −1): Δx = 1 − (−2) = 3 units, Δy = −1 − 0 = −1 unit.

Concept The slope m of a straight line measures its steepness as "rise over run," and it is linked to the line's inclination θ — the angle measured anti-clockwise from the positive x-axis — by m = tanθ. Because tanθ is positive for acute θ and negative for obtuse θ, the sign of the slope tells you which way the line leans: up to the right, or down to the right. Concept The mid-point of a segment is the point exactly halfway along it, so each of its coordinates is simply the ordinary average of the corresponding coordinates of the two endpoints. This works because moving from A to B changes x by a fixed amount Δx; going only halfway therefore changes x by Δx/2, which lands you at (x₁ + x₂)/2. The same argument applies independently to y, which is why the x and y calculations never mix.
Formula M = ( (x₁ + x₂)/2 , (y₁ + y₂)/2 ), all coordinates in the same length units. It is the section formula with ratio 1 : 1 — averaging is just "divide the segment equally."
Solution
1. (i) (3, −2) & (4, 6): average x and y separately.

  • x = (2 + 3)/2 = 5/2 = 2.5 ; y = (−2 + 4)/2 = 2/2 = 1 → M = (2.5, 1)

  • x = (0 + 4)/2 = 2 ; y = (0 + (−1))/2 = −0.5 → M = (2, −0.5)When one endpoint is the origin, the mid-point is just half the other endpoint's coordinates.

5Find the centroid of an equilateral triangle whose vertices are (0, 0), (1, 0), (1/2, √3/2)Centroid formulaCentroid of a triangle given the vertices · Centroid when vertices are given — solved🟡 Similar(1/2, 1/(2√3))
Concept The centroid G of a triangle is the common intersection point of its three medians, and it is also the triangle's centre of mass if the triangle is a uniform lamina — which is exactly why physics cares about it. Its coordinates are the plain average of the three vertices' coordinates, because the centroid divides each median in a 2 : 1 ratio and that ratio makes the averages work out. Assumption: the lamina is uniform (constant surface density), so the geometric centroid coincides with the centre of mass.
Formula G = ( (x₁ + x₂ + x₃)/3 , (y₁ + y₂ + y₃)/3 ), with all coordinates in the same length units. Average of three points, exactly as the centre of mass of three equal point masses placed at the vertices — and a uniform triangular plate has its CM at the same place.
Solution
1. List the vertices: (x₁, y₁) = (0, 0); (x₂, y₂) = (1, 0); (x₃, y₃) = (1/2, √3/2).These three are indeed the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 1 unit — the apex height √3/2 is the standard (√3/2)×side.
2. Average the x-coordinates: x_G = (0 + 1 + 1/2)/3 = (3/2)/3 = 1/2 = 0.5 units.
3. Average the y-coordinates: y_G = (0 + 0 + √3/2)/3 = (√3/2)/3 = √3/6 ≈ 0.289 units.√3 ≈ 1.732, so √3/6 = 1.732/6 = 0.2887.

2In a Cartesian coordinate system, the point of intersection of x-axis and y-axis is called? (Origin)Cartesian basicsThe point of intersection of the axes is called the______🟢 ExactbOrigin
Concept The Cartesian system is built from two mutually perpendicular number lines — the x-axis and the y-axis. The single point where they cross is the origin, written O, and it acts as the zero reference from which every coordinate is measured. In physics this is exactly the "choice of origin" you make when setting up a frame of reference: positions are meaningless until this reference point is fixed.
Formula O ≡ (0, 0) — the origin is the unique point whose x-coordinate and y-coordinate are both zero (in whatever length units the axes carry: m, cm, or plain units). Being on the y-axis forces x = 0; being on the x-axis forces y = 0; the intersection must satisfy both simultaneously.
Solution
1. Every point on the y-axis has x = 0 (it is zero units to the left or right of the y-axis).
2. Every point on the x-axis has y = 0 (it is zero units above or below the x-axis).
3. The point of intersection lies on both axes at once, so it must satisfy both conditions: x = 0 and y = 0.Only one point in the whole plane can do this — the axes are straight lines and cross exactly once.
4. That point (0, 0) is called the origin, denoted O.
5. It is the reference from which all distances are measured: the distance of any point (x, y) from O is √(x² + y²), which correctly gives 0 for O itself.

The x-coordinate (abscissa) is the shortest distance from the y-axis, and the y-coordinate (ordinate) is the shortest distance from the x-axis. "Shortest" means measured along the perpendicular, and the sign records which side of the axis the point lies on, so a single pair (x, y) locates the point uniquely and unambiguously.
Formula For P ≡ (x, y): |x| = perpendicular distance of P from the y-axis and |y| = perpendicular distance of P from the x-axis, both in length units. 4The plane is divided into four quadrants numbered I, II, III, IV in? (anti-clockwise)QuadrantsOrigin, quadrants & plotting · Origin, axes & quadrants explained🟡 SimilarcAnti-clockwise
Concept The two axes cut the plane into four regions called quadrants. The numbering is not random: starting from the region where both coordinates are positive (upper right), the quadrants are numbered I, II, III, IV going anti-clockwise. This matches the universal mathematical convention that positive angles are measured anti-clockwise from the +x-axis, so quadrant number and angle range stay in step.
Formula I: (+, +), 0°–90°   II: (−, +), 90°–180°   III: (−, −), 180°–270°   IV: (+, −), 270°–360°. Sweeping anti-clockwise from the +x-axis, the angle increases steadily, and the quadrant number increases with it — the two conventions are deliberately aligned.
Solution
1. Start in the region right of the y-axis and above the x-axis: both coordinates positive, (+, +) → this is Quadrant I.
2. Rotate anti-clockwise by 90°: now left of the y-axis but still above the x-axis, (−, +) → Quadrant II.
3. Continue anti-clockwise: left and below, (−, −) → Quadrant III.
4. Continue once more: right and below, (+, −) → Quadrant IV, and a further 90° returns to Quadrant I.Four 90° steps complete the 360° turn, confirming the cycle closes correctly.
5. So the numbering runs anti-clockwise, consistent with measuring θ anti-clockwise from the +x-axis.A clockwise numbering would make quadrant II the (+, −) region and break the link with the standard angle convention.

Concept A straight line can be written in several equivalent standard forms, and each is tailored to the data you happen to have: a point plus a slope, two points, or two intercepts. 3. Equal positive intercepts — the intercept form x/a + y/b = 1 with b = a becomes x/a + y/a = 1.
