1 (i)Fill in the blank: The ionization constant of water (Kw) is a product of the concentration of ______ ions and ______ ions in water.Ionic product of waterWhat is Kw? (Ionic Product of Water)🟢 Exacthydrogen (H⁺) and hydroxide (OH⁻)
Concept Water ionises very slightly into H+ and OH−. The product of those two concentrations is a constant at any given temperature, and that constant is Kw.
Key relation Kw = [H+][OH−] = 10−14 at 25 °C Water itself is left out because its concentration, 55.55 M, is effectively constant.
Solution
1. Water ionises as H2O ⇌ H+ + OH−, so K = [H+][OH−]/[H2O].
2. The concentration of water is 1000/18 = 55.55 M and barely changes, so it is absorbed into the constant.
3. That gives K×[H2O] = Kw = [H+][OH−], the ionic product of water.
4. So the blanks are hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions.
5. Experimentally Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C, so in pure water [H+] = [OH−] = 10−7 M.
6. The relation holds in every aqueous solution, not just pure water — adding acid lowers [OH−] by exactly as much as it raises [H+].
7. That is what makes the whole pH scale work: knowing one ion concentration always fixes the other.

 K x [H2O] = Kw = [H+][OH-]

⚡ JEE Trick Kw = [H+][OH−] applies to any aqueous solution, acidic or basic. Given either ion concentration you can always get the other by dividing into 10−14.
Answer: hydrogen (H+) ions and hydroxide (OH−) ions.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: The value of Kw ______ with an increase in temperature.Kw and temperatureIonic Product of Water (Kw) | Full Derivation, Numericals & Concept Explained | Chapter 9🟡 Similarincreases
Concept The dissociation of water is endothermic, so heating drives it forward. More ions means a larger product, so Kw increases with temperature.
Key relation H2O ⇌ H+ + OH−, ΔH > 0 ⇒ Kw rises with T Le Chatelier applied to an endothermic equilibrium.
Solution
1. Splitting a water molecule requires breaking an O–H bond, so the ionisation is endothermic.
2. By Le Chatelier, heating an endothermic equilibrium drives it forward, producing more H+ and OH−.
3. Both concentrations rise, so their product Kw increases. That is the blank — and the lecture states it directly.
4. Numerically: Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C, rises to 5.5×10−14 at 50 °C, and reaches 10−12 at 90 °C.
5. A crucial consequence: the neutral pH is not always 7. At 90 °C neutral water has [H+] = 10−6, so its pH is 6.
6. The water is still neutral, because [H+] still equals [OH−] — it is the number 7 that is tied to 25 °C, not neutrality itself.
7. For the same reason pH + pOH = 14 only at 25 °C; in general it equals pKw.

 25 C : Kw = 1e-14 , neutral pH = 7
 50 C : Kw = 5.5e-14
 90 C : Kw = 1e-12  , neutral pH = 6

 pH + pOH = pKw , which is 14 only at 25 C
⚡ JEE Trick Neutral means [H+] = [OH−], not pH = 7. Away from 25 °C the neutral pH moves, and several questions in this lecture turn on exactly that.

1 (iii)Fill in the blank: The pH scale is a logarithmic scale used to measure the ______ of a solution.Meaning of the pH scalepH Scale, Relation Between pH and pOH - Equilibrium | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6 | CBSE🟢 Exactacidity (or alkalinity)
Concept The pH scale compresses the enormous range of possible [H+] into a small set of numbers. What it measures is the acidity — equivalently the alkalinity — of a solution.
Key relation pH = −log[H+]; low pH acidic, high pH basic Introduced by Sørensen precisely to avoid writing numbers like 0.0000001.
Solution
1. Hydrogen-ion concentrations in ordinary solutions span from about 1 M down to 10−14 M — fourteen orders of magnitude.
2. Sørensen introduced the logarithmic pH scale to compress that range: pH = −log[H+].
3. What the number reports is the acidity of the solution, or equivalently its alkalinity. That is the blank.
4. Item 3(B)→(p) of this Quick Recap says the same: the pH scale “measures the acidity or alkalinity of a solution”.
5. The reading at 25 °C: pH = 7 neutral, pH < 7 acidic, pH > 7 basic.
6. Because the scale is logarithmic, each whole unit is a tenfold change in [H+] — pH 3 is ten times as acidic as pH 4, not a third more.
7. That tenfold-per-unit rule is what Level-4 Q10 and Level-5 Q8 of this lecture both test.
8. Note what the scale does not report. pH measures the concentration of free hydrogen ions, not the total amount of acid present: a 0.1 M solution of acetic acid and a 0.0013 M solution of HCl have the same pH, because only the free H+ counts. Strength and concentration are separate quantities, and the pH scale reads only their combined effect.

1 (iv)Fill in the blank: A solution with a pH less than 7 is considered ______, while a solution with a pH greater than 7 is considered ______.Acidic and basic rangesPH and POH | Ionic Product of Water | Chemistry🟡 Similaracidic; basic (alkaline)
Concept The two halves of the scale. Below 7 the hydrogen ions outnumber the hydroxides — acidic; above 7 the reverse — basic.
Key relation pH < 7 ⇒ [H+] > 10−7 > [OH−]; pH > 7 ⇒ the reverse The crossover sits at 7 because that is where the two are equal at 25 °C.
Solution
1. In pure water at 25 °C, [H+] = [OH−] = 10−7 M, giving pH = 7 — neutral.
2. Adding acid raises [H+] above 10−7, so pH falls below 7 and the solution is acidic. That is the first blank.
3. Adding base raises [OH−], which by Kw forces [H+] below 10−7, so pH rises above 7 and the solution is basic. That is the second blank.
4. The lecture summarises it as pH = 7 = pOH neutral, pH < 7 or pOH > 7 acidic, pH > 7 or pOH < 7 basic — all at 25 °C.
5. Note the qualifier: the boundary is at 7 only at 25 °C. At 90 °C neutral water has pH 6, so a solution of pH 6.5 there is actually basic.
6. The definition that never moves is the comparison itself: acidic means [H+] > [OH−], whatever the temperature.
7. Everyday anchors: gastric juice around 1.7, lemon juice around 2.5, pure water 7, household ammonia around 11.
 [H+] > [OH-]  ->  pH < 7  ->  ACIDIC
 [H+] = [OH-]  ->  pH = 7  ->  neutral
 [H+] < [OH-]  ->  pH > 7  ->  BASIC

 the number 7 is tied to 25 C
 the comparison [H+] vs [OH-] is not
⚡ JEE Trick Define acidic by comparing [H+] with [OH−], not by comparing pH with 7. The comparison survives a temperature change; the number 7 does not.
Answer: acidic; basic (alkaline).
1 (v)Fill in the blank: The pH of a mixture of a strong acid and a strong base depends on the ______ of the two.Mixing acid and baseclass 11-Ionic equilibrium.How to find PH of strong acid and base.(NEET/JEE)🟡 Similarrelative amounts
Concept Mixing a strong acid with a strong base does not automatically give a neutral solution. Whichever is in excess survives, so the pH depends on the relative amounts.
Key relation n(H+) vs n(OH−): whichever is larger sets the pH Only exactly equal amounts of the two produce a neutral mixture.
Solution
1. Neutralisation is H+ + OH− → H2O, consuming the two ions one for one.
2. If the moles supplied are equal, everything is consumed and the mixture is neutral at pH 7.
3. If the acid is in excess, the leftover H+ makes the solution acidic; if the base is in excess, the leftover OH− makes it basic.
4. So the pH depends on the relative amounts of the two — the blank.
5. Item 3(E)→(t) of this Quick Recap states the same thing, and item 2(v) tests it as a false statement.
6. “Amounts” means moles of H+ and OH−, which brings in both the volume and the basicity or acidity — 0.01 M H2SO4 supplies twice the H+ of 0.01 M HCl.
7. Level-3 Q2 and Q4 and Level-5 Q3, Q6, Q7 and Q9 of this lecture are all worked instances of this one idea.
8. Note that “amounts” must be counted in moles of the reacting ions, not in volumes or molarities. Equal volumes of equimolar H2SO4 and NaOH leave the acid in twofold excess, because the acid is dibasic — a case Level-5 Q7 of this lecture works through in normality to avoid exactly that trap.
 H+  +  OH-  ->  H2O      one for one

 n(H+) = n(OH-)  ->  neutral, pH 7
 n(H+) > n(OH-)  ->  acidic
 n(H+) < n(OH-)  ->  basic

 amounts = molarity x volume x basicity
⚡ JEE Trick Always work in millimoles for these mixtures: molarity times volume in mL. Subtract, divide by the total volume, and the pH follows in one line.
Answer: relative amounts of the two.

2 (ii)True or false: Kw remains constant with changes in temperature.Kw and temperatureIonic Product of Water (Kw) | Full Derivation, Numericals & Concept Explained | Chapter 9🟡 SimilarFalse Kw rises with temperature
Concept Kw is an equilibrium constant, and every equilibrium constant depends on temperature. Since water’s ionisation is endothermic, Kw rises on heating — so the statement is false.
Key relation endothermic ⇒ K rises with T; Kw goes 10−14 (25 °C) → 10−12 (90 °C) A hundredfold change over 65 degrees is far from constant.
Solution
1. Kw is an equilibrium constant, and no equilibrium constant is independent of temperature.
2. Water’s ionisation is endothermic, so by the van’t Hoff rule Kw increases with temperature.
3. The statement claims it is constant, so it is false.
4. The lecture states it plainly: “Dissociation of water is endothermic, so on increasing temperature, Kw increases.”
5. The size of the change is large: 10−14 at 25 °C, 5.5×10−14 at 50 °C, 10−12 at 90 °C — a hundredfold rise.
6. What is constant is Kw across different solutions at a fixed temperature: acidic, basic or neutral, the product [H+][OH−] is the same.
7. That is probably the true fact the statement is a corruption of — constant with composition, not with temperature.

⚡ JEE Trick Two different constancies get confused here. Kw is the same in every solution at one temperature, and different at every temperature. Questions exploit the gap between those two statements.

Concept At 25 °C a neutral solution has [H+] = 10−7, so pH = 7. The statement is true under the standard conditions assumed throughout the lecture.
Key relation neutral ⇒ [H+] = [OH−] = √Kw = 10−7 at 25 °C The number 7 follows from Kw = 10−14, not from the definition of neutrality.
Solution
1. A neutral solution is one in which [H+] = [OH−].
2. Since their product is Kw, each equals √Kw = √(10−14) = 10−7 M at 25 °C.
3. So pH = −log(10−7) = 7, and the statement is true.
4. The lecture’s summary agrees: pH = 7 = pOH means neutral, at 25 °C.
5. The caveat, worth stating: the number 7 depends on Kw, so it holds only at 25 °C. At 90 °C, Kw = 10−12 and neutral water has pH 6.
6. Neutrality itself never moves — it always means [H+] = [OH−]. Only its numerical label shifts.
7. Since the whole lecture works at 25 °C unless stated otherwise, the statement is taken as true here.

Concept The statement takes the concentration straight to a pH without noticing that NaOH supplies hydroxide, not hydrogen ions. The correct answer is 13.
Key relation [OH−] = 0.1 ⇒ pOH = 1 ⇒ pH = 14 − 1 = 13 Going through pOH is the safe route for any base.
Solution
1. NaOH is a strong base and dissociates completely: 0.1 M NaOH gives [OH−] = 0.1 M.
2. So pOH = −log(0.1) = 1.
3. Then pH = 14 − pOH = 14 − 1 = 13, not 1. The statement is false.
4. The error is transparent once seen: 0.1 gives “1” on the p-scale, but that 1 is the pOH.
5. A sanity check settles it instantly: NaOH is a base, so its pH must be well above 7. A pH of 1 would make it strongly acidic.

7. Item 3(D)→(s) of this Quick Recap makes the general point: the pH of a strong base is greater than 7.
