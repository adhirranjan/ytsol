Concept The range R is the horizontal distance a ground-launched projectile covers before returning to launch height. Horizontal motion is force-free, so the horizontal speed u cosθ stays constant; the projectile is airborne for the time of flight T fixed entirely by the vertical motion. Range is (constant horizontal speed) × (flight time), so the correct option must be (i) dimensionally a length and (ii) maximal at θ = 45°.

2. Flight time from the vertical axis: T = 2u sinθ ÷ g (s).The body climbs for u sinθ/g and falls for the same time back to y = 0.

5. Check the maximum: sin2θ = 1 at θ = 45°, giving Rmax = u²/g, the standard result.

2A ball is thrown with initial velocity 100 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. How far from the throwing point will the ball hit? (g=10 m/s²)Horizontal range (numeric)A projectile launched at ground level with speed 50.0 m/s at 30.0° above horizontal · Ex-73: body projected 30 m/s at 30° with horizontal — height, time, range🟡 Similar500√3 m ⚠
Concept "How far from the throwing point will the ball hit" — for a ball thrown from ground level over level ground, the landing point is the horizontal range R. Projectile motion splits cleanly into two independent 1-D motions: horizontally there is no force, so ux stays constant; vertically gravity acts with constant a = −g. The ball is in the air only as long as the vertical motion allows, so R = (constant horizontal speed) × (time of flight), and that product collapses to the single range formula.
Assumptions Ground is level (launch height = landing height = 0 m), air resistance is neglected, and g = 10 m s⁻² as given. The book writes "g = 10 m/s" — a misprint for m s⁻²; the value 10 is what is intended.
Formula R = u² sin2θ / g — R in m, u = launch speed in m s⁻¹, θ = launch angle (degrees), g = 9.8 or 10 m s⁻². Units: (m s⁻¹)² ÷ (m s⁻²) = m² s⁻² × s² m⁻¹ = m ✓ — dimensionally a length, as a range must be. Built from T = 2u sinθ / g (s) and R = ux·T = (u cosθ)·T (m).
Solution
1. List the data with units: u = 100 m s⁻¹, θ = 30°, g = 10 m s⁻².
2. Resolve the launch velocity into components:ux = u cos30° = 100 × 0.8660 = 86.60 m s⁻¹ (constant); uy = u sin30° = 100 × 0.500 = 50.0 m s⁻¹ (decelerated by g).
3. Time of flight from the vertical motion: T = 2uy ÷ g = (2 × 50.0 m s⁻¹) ÷ (10 m s⁻²) = 100 ÷ 10 = 10.0 s.Up-and-down are symmetric: it takes uy/g = 5.0 s to rise and another 5.0 s to fall back to launch height. Units: (m s⁻¹)/(m s⁻²) = s ✓.
4. Horizontal distance = constant speed × time: R = ux × T = 86.60 m s⁻¹ × 10.0 s = 866 m.No horizontal force ⇒ uniform velocity ⇒ plain distance = speed × time.
5. Cross-check with the one-line range formula: R = u² sin2θ ÷ g = (100)² × sin60° ÷ 10 = 10000 × 0.8660 ÷ 10 = 866 m ✓ — same answer, so the two routes agree.
6. Exact surd form: R = 10000 × (√3/2) ÷ 10 = 500√3 m ≈ 866 m.

Concept Two launches at the same speed hit the same range only when their angles are complementary — θ and 90°−θ — because R ∝ sin2θ and sin2(90°−θ) = sin2θ. Their times of flight differ (one carries sinθ, the other cosθ), and this problem asks you to prove their product equals 2R/g — a compact identity that ties the two flights to their shared range.
Formula t₁ = 2u sinθ/g, t₂ = 2u sin(90°−θ)/g = 2u cosθ/g (s); R = u² sin2θ/g (m). t₂ uses sin(90°−θ) = cosθ; the shared range guarantees the two flights are the complementary pair.
Solution
1. First flight time: t₁ = 2u sinθ ÷ g.
2. Same speed, angle 90°−θ (the only other angle giving the same R): t₂ = 2u cosθ ÷ g.
3. Multiply: t₁t₂ = (2u sinθ/g)(2u cosθ/g) = 4u² sinθ cosθ / g².
4. Use 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ: t₁t₂ = 2u² sin2θ / g².Just the double-angle identity, nothing more.
5. From R = u² sin2θ/g we get u² sin2θ = Rg; substitute: t₁t₂ = 2(Rg)/g² = 2R/g. ∎
6. Numerical check (u = 20, θ = 30°, g = 10): t₁ = 2.0 s, t₂ = 3.46 s, product = 6.93; and 2R/g = 2 × 34.6 ÷ 10 = 6.93 ✓.

The last one is dimension-safe (s² = m ÷ (m s⁻²) ✓) and lets you get one flight time from the other plus the range without touching θ. Concept Same speed a at complementary angles θ and (π/2−θ) gives the same range but different heights: the steep launch reaches H₁, the shallow one H₂. Because H uses sin²(angle), the two heights carry sin²θ and cos²θ, whose product folds neatly back into the range. The question wants R written using only H₁ and H₂.

5. Limits: θ = 90° (straight up) gives the longest T = 2u/g; θ = 0° gives T = 0 — both physically correct, ruling out the cosθ options.

3If speed is the same but angle changes from θ to 90°−θ, what happens to time of flight?Complementary angles — time of flightTwo bodies projected at angle θ and (90°−θ) with same speed — ratio of time of flight🟡 SimilarcRemains the same ⚠
Concept This is the heart of the whole lesson: for complementary angles θ and 90°−θ at the same speed, the range is identical but the time of flight is not. The reason is which trig function each depends on. Range carries sin2θ, and sin2(90°−θ) = sin(180°−2θ) = sin2θ — unchanged. Time of flight carries sinθ, and sin(90°−θ) = cosθ — a different number unless θ = 45°. So the steep partner stays up longer while covering the same ground.
Formula T = 2u sinθ / g (s) with u in m s⁻¹, g in m s⁻². For the partner angle: T′ = 2u sin(90°−θ)/g = 2u cosθ / g (s). Units: (m s⁻¹)/(m s⁻²) = s ✓ for both. Dividing gives T′/T = cosθ / sinθ = cot θ — a pure number, as a ratio must be.
Assumptions Same launch speed u, same g, level ground, no air resistance — only the angle is changed.
Solution
1. Original flight time: T = 2u sinθ ÷ g (s).From v = u − gt on the vertical axis: the body returns to launch height after 2u sinθ/g seconds.
2. New angle 90°−θ, same speed u ⇒ T′ = 2u sin(90°−θ) ÷ g = 2u cosθ ÷ g (s).The identity sin(90°−θ) = cosθ is the only step needed.
3. Take the ratio to remove u and g: T′ ÷ T = cosθ ÷ sinθ = cot θ.Both u (m s⁻¹) and g (m s⁻²) cancel, leaving a dimensionless number ✓.
4. Read off the three cases from cot θ:
  • θ < 45° ⇒ cot θ > 1 ⇒ T′ > T ⇒ the time of flight increases.
  • θ > 45° ⇒ cot θ < 1 ⇒ T′ < T ⇒ the time of flight decreases.
  • θ = 45° ⇒ cot 45° = 1 ⇒ T′ = T ⇒ unchanged (the angle is its own complement).
5. Numerical check with u = 20 m s⁻¹, g = 10 m s⁻², θ = 30° → 60°:T = 2(20)(0.500)/10 = 2.0 s; T′ = 2(20)(0.866)/10 = 3.46 s. Ratio 3.46/2.0 = 1.73 = cot30° ✓ — it went up because 30° < 45°. Meanwhile R = 400(0.866)/10 = 34.6 m for both.
6. So the one thing that is certainly false is "remains the same" (except at exactly 45°); the direction of the change is fixed by whether θ is below or above 45°.

Solution
1. Treat the bullet as launched with a large horizontal speed toward the target.
2. Time to reach the target: t = distance ÷ (u cosθ) (s) — small but non-zero.
3. In that time the bullet falls Δy = ½ g t² below the barrel's straight line.Independent of the bullet's horizontal speed, the fall is purely gravitational.
4. Aiming straight at the target ⇒ impact is Δy below it — a miss.
5. So raise the muzzle so the line of fire passes Δy above the target; gravity then curves the bullet down onto it — slightly higher than the target, option (c).
