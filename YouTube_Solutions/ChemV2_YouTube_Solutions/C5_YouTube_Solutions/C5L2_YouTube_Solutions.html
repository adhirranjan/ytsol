1Which of the following compounds contains a coordinate (dative) bond? (a) H₂ (b) O₂ (c) NH₄⁺ (d) F₂Coordinate bondFormation of NH₄⁺ and H₃O⁺ — coordinate bond worked out🟢 ExactcNH₄⁺
Concept A coordinate (dative) bond is still a covalent bond — two electrons shared between two nuclei — but the whole shared pair comes from one atom. That demands two things at once: a donor whose octet is already complete and which still carries at least one lone pair, and an acceptor that is short of a pair (an empty orbital or a bare positive centre). So the test for "does this species contain a dative bond?" is never about the formula; it is about whether some atom had to hand over a complete pair.
Key relation donor (lone pair) + acceptor (vacant orbital) → D → A the arrow always points from donor to acceptor; both electrons of the new bond were the donor's. In NH₄⁺: H₃N: + H⁺ → [H₃N→H]⁺ N in NH₃ already has a complete octet (3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair) and H⁺ is a bare proton with an empty 1s orbital and no electrons at all — a perfect acceptor.
Solution
1. Test each option for a donor–acceptor pair.
  • (a) H₂ — H·+·H, one electron from each atom. Pure non-polar covalent, contribution 1 + 1, so no dative bond.
  • (b) O₂ — O=O, each O supplies 2 electrons to the double bond. Equal contribution, no dative bond.
  • (c) NH₄⁺ — three N–H bonds are ordinary covalent (1 e⁻ from N, 1 e⁻ from H), but the fourth H arrives as H⁺ with zero electrons; N donates its lone pair in full. That fourth bond is a coordinate bond. ✓
  • (d) F₂ — F–F, one electron from each. No dative bond.
2. Confirm the bookkeeping on nitrogen. Free N is 1s²2s²2p³ ⇒ 5 valence electrons; in NH₄⁺ all 8 electrons around N are bonding, none is a lone pair.The lone pair that was on NH₃ has become the fourth bond pair — that disappearance is the visible signature of a dative bond.
3. Note that once formed the four N–H bonds of NH₄⁺ are indistinguishable — same length, same energy. Only the origin of the electrons differed, which is exactly the point the theory note makes about coordinate bonds.

           1 x N->H              2 (N)            0 (H+)     COORDINATE   <-- answer (c)
 F2        F-F                   1                1          covalent

 N in NH4+ :  V = 5,  L = 0,  S = 8   =>  FC = 5 - 0 - 8/2 = +1  (the ion's charge sits on N)
⚡ JEE Trick Scan for a species that is one proton richer or one electron pair poorer than a neutral parent: NH₄⁺ (NH₃ + H⁺), H₃O⁺ (H₂O + H⁺), NH₃→BF₃, O₃, SO₂, HNO₃, H₂SO₄ — all contain dative bonds. Trap: the homonuclear diatomics H₂, O₂, N₂, F₂, Cl₂ can never hold a coordinate bond, because two identical atoms give no reason for one to be donor and the other acceptor.
Answer: (c) NH₄⁺ — three normal covalent N–H bonds plus one coordinate N→H bond.
2The formal charge on carbon in CO₃²⁻ is: (a) +2 (b) +1 (c) 0 (d) −1Formal charge · carbonateHow to Calculate the Formal Charges for CO3 2- (Carbonate ion)🟢 Exactc0
Concept Formal charge is a bookkeeping device, not a real charge. You pretend every bond is split exactly in half, count how many electrons that assigns to an atom, and compare with how many that atom owned when it was free. A surplus makes the formal charge negative, a shortfall makes it positive. Because it is pure bookkeeping, the answer depends only on three counts: valence electrons of the free atom, lone-pair electrons on it, and total bonding electrons around it.
Formula FC = V − L − ½S V = valence electrons of the free atom, L = non-bonding (lone-pair) electrons on that atom, S = total bonding (shared) electrons around it; ½S is the atom's half-share of every bond it makes. An equivalent and faster form is FC = V − L − (number of bonds) because each bond contributes 2 to S, half of which is 1.
Solution
1. Draw the Lewis structure of CO₃²⁻: carbon central, one C=O double bond and two C–O⁻ single bonds. Total valence electrons = 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24.The 2 extra electrons come from the 2− charge — always add one electron per unit of negative charge before distributing (Step 2 of the book's algorithm).
2. Look at carbon only. It carries no lone pair, so L = 0.
3. Count carbon's bonding electrons: one double bond (4 e⁻) + two single bonds (2 + 2 e⁻) ⇒ S = 8.
4. Apply the formula: FC(C) = 4 − 0 − ½(8) = 4 − 4 = 0. Option (c).
5. Check the whole ion adds up — the standard self-test: the doubly bonded O has FC 0, each singly bonded O has FC −1.0 + 0 + (−1) + (−1) = −2, exactly the ion's charge. If the formal charges do not sum to the overall charge, the Lewis structure or the arithmetic is wrong.
6. Eliminate: (a) +2 would need C to make only 2 bonds; (b) +1 would need 3 bonds; (d) −1 would need 5 bonds — impossible for carbon, which has no d-orbitals available.

3Which of the following is the correct increasing order of covalent character? (a) NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃ (b) AlCl₃ < MgCl₂ < NaCl (c) MgCl₂ < NaCl < AlCl₃ (d) NaCl < AlCl₃ < MgCl₂Fajan's rules · covalent characterArrange the following in the order of increasing covalent character: NaCl, MgCl₂ and AlCl₃🟢 ExactaNaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃
Concept No bond is 100% ionic. A cation sitting next to an anion pulls on the anion's outer electron cloud; the more it distorts that cloud, the more electron density is dragged into the internuclear region — and electron density between two nuclei is covalent character. Fajan's rules simply list what makes a cation a strong puller (small, highly charged, 18-electron outer shell) and what makes an anion an easy target (large, highly charged). Here the anion is Cl⁻ throughout, so the whole comparison is decided by the cation.
Key relation Polarising power of a cation φ = (ionic charge) ÷ (radius)² exactly as printed in the theory — charge in the numerator, the square of the radius below, so shrinking the cation counts for even more than charging it. And covalent character ∝ polarising power of the cation × polarisability of the anion.
Solution
1. Identify the variable: all three chlorides share Cl⁻, so anion polarisability is constant. Only the cation changes — Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺.
2. Charge rises across the series: +1 → +2 → +3. Higher charge = stronger pull.
3. Size falls across the series: Na⁺ (95 pm) > Mg²⁺ (65 pm) > Al³⁺ (50 pm).These three are isoelectronic (all 2, 8 = Ne core); as nuclear charge climbs 11 → 12 → 13 with the same electron count, the ion contracts.
4. Both effects push the same way, so polarising power increases Na⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Al³⁺ and covalent character increases NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃ — option (a).
5. Cross-check with the melting points printed in the theory: NaCl 801 °C, MgCl₂ 712 °C, AlCl₃ sublimes.Melting point falls as covalent (molecular) character rises; AlCl₃ is so covalent it exists as the discrete dimer Al₂Cl₆ and never melts normally.
6. Kill the rest: (b) is the exact reverse; (c) and (d) both place MgCl₂ out of sequence, which no trend in charge or size can justify.

⚡ JEE Trick When the anion is common, rank by cation — smaller and more highly charged wins. When the cation is common, rank by anion — bigger wins (NaF < NaCl < NaBr < NaI). Melting point is the free giveaway: it always runs opposite to covalent character. Trap: the word "increasing" — option (b) is the correct decreasing order and is the most-chosen wrong answer.
Answer: (a) NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃.
4Which of the following compounds will have the highest lattice energy and why? (a) LiI, because Li⁺ is a small cation (b) NaBr, because Na⁺ has lower charge density (c) MgF₂, because Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and F⁻ is small (d) KCl, because both ions are largeLattice energyLattice energy of ionic compounds — charge vs ionic radius🟡 SimilarcMgF₂, because Mg²⁺ has a higher charge and F⁻ is small
Concept Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions collapse into one mole of crystal — it is nothing more than Coulomb's law summed over the lattice. Two things control it: the product of the ionic charges (numerator) and the internuclear distance r₊ + r₋ (denominator). Charge dominates because it enters as a product — doubling both charges multiplies the attraction by four, whereas halving the distance only doubles it.
Formula U ∝ (z⁺ × z⁻) ÷ (r⁺ + r⁻) z⁺, z⁻ = magnitudes of the ionic charges; r⁺ + r⁻ = the cation–anion separation. Read it as "charges up, size down ⇒ lattice energy up".
Solution
1. Write the charge product for each option — the first screen, and it usually settles the question outright.
  • LiI: (1)(1) = 1   • NaBr: (1)(1) = 1   • MgF₂: (2)(1) = 2   • KCl: (1)(1) = 1
2. MgF₂ is the only one containing a doubly charged ion, so its numerator is twice everyone else's.Mg²⁺ carries +2 while Li⁺, Na⁺ and K⁺ all carry only +1.
3. Now check the denominator does not undo the gain. Mg²⁺ (65 pm) with F⁻ (136 pm) is the smallest pair in the list, r⁺ + r⁻ ≈ 201 pm.Compare LiI 60 + 216 = 276 pm, NaBr 95 + 195 = 290 pm, KCl 133 + 181 = 314 pm — iodide and bromide are enormous, which kills LiI and NaBr.
4. Both factors favour MgF₂, so it has the highest lattice energy — option (c), and the reason quoted in (c) is precisely the right one.
5. Reject the distractors on their reasons, not just their compounds: (a) Li⁺ is indeed small, but I⁻ is huge and only singly charged; (b) "lower charge density" is a reason for a low lattice energy, so the option contradicts itself; (d) large ions mean large r and therefore the lowest lattice energy — KCl is the weakest of the four.

 NaBr      1   1     1       95    195      290          1/290 = 0.0034
 MgF2      2   1     2       65    136      201          2/201 = 0.0100   <-- answer (c)
 KCl       1   1     1      133    181      314          1/314 = 0.0032
 => MgF2 wins on BOTH counts: biggest charge product AND smallest separation
⚡ JEE Trick Always compare the charge product first — it beats size in almost every JEE comparison (this is why MgO ≫ NaCl and Fe₂O₃ > FeO). Only when the charge products tie do you look at the radii. Trap: options that offer a true statement as a wrong reason, such as (a) "Li⁺ is a small cation" — correct as a fact, irrelevant to this comparison.
Answer: (c) MgF₂ — the largest charge product (2 × 1) combined with the smallest inter-ionic distance.

Concept Ozone is the case where formal charge is not optional decoration. O₃ has 18 valence electrons and you cannot give all three oxygens an octet using only neutral atoms — one oxygen must donate a whole pair to another, i.e. form a coordinate bond. The donor ends one electron short of its free-atom count (+1), the acceptor one electron rich (−1). Reading the picture therefore means counting lone pairs and bonds on each oxygen separately, never assuming symmetry.
Formula FC = V − L − ½S, with V = 6 for every oxygen. All three atoms are oxygen, so the only things that change from atom to atom are L (lone-pair electrons) and S (bonding electrons) — read them straight off the diagram.
Solution
1. Read the central O from the figure: one lone pair (2 e⁻), attached by one single and one double bond, i.e. 3 bonds = 6 bonding electrons.L = 2, S = 6 ⇒ FC = 6 − 2 − 3 = +1. It is the donor of the dative bond, so on paper it loses an electron.
2. Read the left O — the singly bonded one. To reach an octet with only one bond it must carry three lone pairs (6 e⁻) plus 1 bond (2 e⁻).L = 6, S = 2 ⇒ FC = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. It is the acceptor, so on paper it gains.
3. Read the right O — the doubly bonded one. Two lone pairs (4 e⁻) plus a double bond (4 e⁻) completes its octet.L = 4, S = 4 ⇒ FC = 6 − 4 − 2 = 0 — an ordinary, unremarkable oxygen.
4. In the order asked (left, central, right) the triple is (−1, +1, 0) — option (b).
5. 6. Remember the real molecule is the resonance hybrid: both O–O bonds are equal (128 pm, bond order 1.5) and each terminal O carries −½. The ±1 charges belong only to the individual canonical structure drawn.

1State octet rule.Octet rule — statementOctet Rule — Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure, CBSE Class 11 Chemistry🟡 Similar—
Concept The octet rule is Kössel and Lewis's answer to the question "why do atoms bond at all?". The noble gases are chemically inert, and what they share is a filled outer shell of eight electrons (ns²np⁶). Lewis proposed that every other atom bonds precisely in order to reach that same eight-electron arrangement — by losing, gaining or sharing electrons. All three bond types of this chapter fall out of that one idea: transfer gives the ionic bond, sharing gives the covalent bond, and one-sided sharing gives the coordinate bond.
Statement "Atoms combine by losing, gaining or sharing electrons so as to acquire eight electrons in their outermost (valence) shell — the stable inert-gas configuration." The eight electrons are the "octet"; hydrogen, lithium and beryllium are the standard exceptions because their nearest inert gas is helium, whose stable shell is a duplet of 2.
Solution
1. State the rule in the examiner's words — the marks sit on "eight electrons", "outermost shell" and "inert-gas configuration".
2. Name the three routes to the octet, with one example each:
  • Losing — Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8), the Ne configuration.
  • Gaining — Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8), the Ar configuration.
  • Sharing — in Cl₂ each Cl contributes one electron to a shared pair, and each then counts 8 around itself.
3. Add the sharing arithmetic explicitly, because that is what board answers usually miss: a shared pair is counted in full by both partners.In Cl₂ each atom brings 7 valence electrons; the shared pair is counted by both, so each atom reads 6 (lone) + 2 (shared) = 8.
4. Mention the deliberate exception, which earns the second mark: hydrogen needs only a duplet (2 electrons, the He configuration), and the theory note reminds you that "completion of octet is not the essential condition of a covalent bond".

 GAIN       Cl              2, 8, 7             Cl-  2, 8, 8           8
 SHARE      Cl2      :Cl. + .Cl:        :Cl : Cl:      6 lone + 2 shared = 8

 duplet exceptions:  H (1s1 -> 1s2),  Li,  Be    -> He configuration, 2 e-
⚡ JEE Trick Write the rule and one exception in the same breath — examiners nearly always follow "state the octet rule" with "give its limitations". The three standard failures to keep ready are the incomplete octet (BeCl₂ 4 e⁻, BF₃ 6 e⁻), the expanded octet (PCl₅ 10, SF₆ 12, IF₇ 14) and odd-electron molecules (NO 11, NO₂ 17). Trap: saying "atoms want 8 electrons in the atom" — it must be in the outermost shell.
Key point: atoms combine by losing, gaining or sharing electrons so as to attain eight electrons in the valence shell (the inert-gas configuration); H, Li and Be need only a duplet of 2.
2What is the significance of octet rule?Significance of the octet ruleImportance of Octet Rule — Nature of Chemical Bond, Chemistry Class 11🟢 Exact—
Concept Before Lewis, "valency" was an empirical number pulled out of formulas. The octet rule turned it into something predictive: once you accept that an atom bonds to reach eight outer electrons, you can predict how many bonds it will make, what the formula of its compound will be, whether it will form a cation or an anion, and how to draw the Lewis structure. Its significance is therefore that it converts a table of observed valencies into a single rule you can apply to a new element you have never met.
Key relation group valence = number of dots in the Lewis symbol, or 8 − (number of dots) this is the exact statement in the theory: for Na, Mg, Al the valence equals the dot count (1, 2, 3); for N, O, F, Ne it equals 8 − dots, giving (8−5) = 3, (8−6) = 2, (8−7) = 1 and (8−8) = 0.
Solution
1. It explains why atoms bond at all — the drive is to reach the stable inert-gas octet, which is the lowest-energy arrangement of the valence shell.
2. It predicts valency from the group, using the dot rule above.Group 1 → 1, group 2 → 2, group 13 → 3, group 14 → 4, group 15 → 3, group 16 → 2, group 17 → 1, group 18 → 0.
3. It predicts the formula of a compound by cross-multiplying those valencies.Na (valence 1) with O (valence 2) gives Na₂O; Mg (2) with N (3) gives Mg₃N₂ — exactly as worked out in the solved examples of this lecture.
4. It tells you which bond type to expect — a metal short of losing 1–3 electrons meets a non-metal short of gaining 1–3, so electrons transfer (ionic); two non-metals both short of gaining must share (covalent).
5. It underpins the Lewis-structure algorithm — Step 5 of the book's method is literally "distribute the remaining pairs so that each combining atom gets an octet".
6. Close by acknowledging its limits, which is what turns a 3-mark answer into a full one: it says nothing about shape or bond energy, and it fails for incomplete octets (BeCl₂, BF₃), expanded octets (PCl₅, SF₆) and odd-electron molecules (NO, NO₂).

⚡ JEE Trick The single most useful consequence: valence = dots (for groups 1, 2, 13, 14) or 8 − dots (for groups 15–18). That one line lets you write the formula of any binary compound in seconds. Trap: quoting the rule's statement when the question asks for its significance — the marks are for what the rule lets you predict, not for what it says.
Key point: the octet rule explains why atoms combine, predicts group valence (dots, or 8 − dots), lets you write the formula of a compound, tells you whether the bond will be ionic or covalent, and drives the Lewis-structure algorithm.
3Explain why the octet rule is not valid for (1) hydrogen and (2) the lithium atom.Duplet rule · exceptionsExceptions to the octet rule — duplets, incomplete and expanded octets🟡 Similar—
Concept The octet rule is really the "nearest inert gas" rule; eight is just the number that happens to fit the valence shell of Ne, Ar, Kr… But the first inert gas is helium, whose complete outer shell holds only two electrons, because the n = 1 shell contains a single 1s orbital and nothing else. Any atom whose nearest inert gas is helium therefore aims for a duplet, not an octet. That is the whole explanation for both H and Li.
Key relation Capacity of a shell = 2n² ⇒ for n = 1, capacity = 2(1)² = 2. The first shell has only the 1s orbital, which holds a maximum of 2 electrons; there is no 1p subshell, so eight electrons simply cannot be accommodated. Hence H (Z = 1): 1s¹ → 1s² (He) and Li (Z = 3): 1s²2s¹ → 1s² (He).
Solution
1. Hydrogen. Z = 1, configuration 1s¹, only one valence electron and only the n = 1 shell available.By sharing one electron (as in H₂ or H–Cl) it reaches 1s², which is the helium configuration. It is complete at 2, so demanding 8 is meaningless — the shell physically cannot hold them.
2. Show it with the H₂ count: each H brings 1 electron, the shared pair is counted by both, so each H reads 2 electrons.This is why H is always monovalent and always a terminal atom, never a central one.
3. Lithium. Z = 3, configuration 1s²2s¹ (2, 1). Its single valence electron is easily lost.Li → Li⁺ + e⁻ leaves 1s², again the helium configuration — a duplet, not an octet. Losing one electron is far cheaper than gaining seven.
4. So both atoms obey a duplet rule: hydrogen by sharing to reach 1s², lithium by losing to reach 1s².
5. Extend the same reasoning to beryllium (1s²2s², forms BeCl₂ with only 4 electrons round Be) and boron (BF₃, only 6) — the family of incomplete-octet exceptions listed in the theory.

 (Be    4   1s2 2s2             2        share 2 e-         4 e- round Be         4  incomplete)

⚡ JEE Trick Remember the slogan "He-family follows the duplet" — H, Li and Be all sit next to helium, so 2 is their magic number. Trap: writing that "hydrogen has only one electron so it cannot complete an octet" without saying why — the marking point is the 2n² capacity of the first shell / helium configuration, not merely the electron count.
Key point: the first shell holds at most 2n² = 2 electrons, so H (1s¹) and Li (1s²2s¹) attain the helium duplet — H by sharing to 1s², Li by losing its 2s electron to give Li⁺ (1s²). An octet is physically impossible for them.
4Explain the steps of writing Lewis dot structures.Lewis structure algorithmSteps to write a Lewis dot structure — CBSE class 11 walkthrough🟢 Exact—
Concept A Lewis structure is an electron budget drawn as a picture. The whole algorithm is: count how many valence electrons you are allowed to spend, decide the skeleton, spend the cheapest electrons first as single bonds, then spend the rest as lone pairs, and only if some atom is still short do you promote a lone pair into a multiple bond. Following the five steps in order guarantees you neither invent nor lose electrons — which is exactly what formal-charge questions later test.
Key relation total electrons = Σ(valence electrons of all atoms) + (negative charge) − (positive charge) add one electron for each unit of negative charge on an anion, subtract one for each unit of positive charge on a cation — Step 2 of the printed algorithm. Bookkeeping check: electrons used = 2 × (bonds) + (lone-pair electrons) must equal the total.
Solution
1. Step 1 — count. Add up the valence electrons of every combining atom.For CO: C = 2s²2p² gives 4, O = 2s²2p⁴ gives 6, total 4 + 6 = 10 electrons to spend.
2. Step 2 — adjust for charge. Add one electron per unit of negative charge; subtract one per unit of positive charge.CO₃²⁻: 4 + 18 + 2 = 24. NH₄⁺: 5 + 4 − 1 = 8. Getting this step wrong makes every later formal charge wrong.
3. Step 3 — skeleton. Write the symbols and guess the connectivity, distributing the electrons as shared pairs in proportion to the total number of bonds.
4. Step 4 — pick the central atom. Generally the least electronegative atom occupies the central position.Hence S in SO₄²⁻, C in COCl₂, P in H₃PO₄, N in HNO₃; hydrogen is never central because it can form only one bond. In an oxyacid the H atoms sit on the oxygens, not on the central atom.
5. Step 5 — place the remaining electrons. After the single bonds, give the leftover electrons out as lone pairs so that every atom reaches an octet; if an atom is still short, convert a lone pair of a neighbour into a multiple bond.In CO the single bond plus lone pairs leaves carbon at 6, so two more lone pairs of O are promoted, giving the triple bond :C≡O: and an octet on both atoms.
6. Finish by auditing with formal charges — the structure with charges closest to zero is the preferred one, and the sum of formal charges must equal the overall charge.

4Polar covalent bonds are formed between: (a) Same atoms (b) Different atoms with same electronegativity (c) Dissimilar atoms with large electronegativity difference (d) MetalsPolar covalent bondChemical bonding — polar vs non-polar covalent bonds (grade 11)🟡 SimilarcDissimilar atoms with large electronegativity difference
Concept Whether a covalent bond is polar is decided by a single number: the electronegativity difference ΔEN between the bonded atoms. If ΔEN = 0 the shared pair sits exactly midway and the bond is non-polar. If ΔEN is appreciable the pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom, which acquires a partial negative charge δ− while its partner is left δ+. The bond then has partial ionic character — it is a polar covalent bond. Only at very large ΔEN does the transfer become complete and the bond genuinely ionic.
Key relation ΔEN = 0 → non-polar covalent; ΔEN small-to-large → polar covalent (δ⁺ — δ⁻); ΔEN very large → ionic. Concept Blue vitriol is a favourite exam species because a single formula unit contains every bond type on the syllabus at once. Concept Three separate applications of one formula, deliberately chosen so that each is a different trap. In NH₄⁺ the positive charge is real and it does sit on nitrogen, because nitrogen donated a lone pair and now makes four bonds instead of three. In CO₃²⁻ the ion's 2− charge is genuine but it lives on the terminal oxygens, not on carbon. In COCl₂ the molecule is neutral and each chlorine, making one bond and holding three lone pairs, is exactly balanced. Only careful use of FC = V − L − ½B keeps these apart.
Formula FC = V − L − ½ B = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − ½ (bonding electrons). Simplify to FC = V − L − (number of bonds). Always finish by checking that Σ FC over all atoms equals the species' overall charge.
Solution
1. (i) N in NH₄⁺. Nitrogen makes four N–H bonds and keeps no lone pair: V = 5, L = 0, B = 8.
2. FC(N) = 5 − 0 − ½(8) = 5 − 4 = +1. Each hydrogen: 1 − 0 − 1 = 0, so Σ FC = +1 ✔ matches the ion's charge.Nitrogen shows +1 precisely because it donated its lone pair to H⁺ — the formal-charge signature of the coordinate bond in the ammonium ion.

8Assertion: The atoms in a covalent molecule are said to share electrons, yet some covalent molecules are polar. Reason: In a polar covalent molecule, the shared electrons spend more time on the average near one of the atoms. (a) If assertion is true but the reason is false. (b) If assertion is false but reason is true. (c) If both assertion and reason are true and the reason is the correct explanation of assertion. (d) If both assertion and reason are true but reason is not the correct explanation of assertion.Assertion–Reason · bond polarityAssertion (A): The atoms in a covalent molecule are said to share electrons, yet some covalent molecules are polar🟢 ExactcBoth assertion and reason are true and the reason is the correct explanation
Concept Assertion–Reason questions are answered in three separate passes: is the assertion true, is the reason true, and does the reason cause the assertion? Here the assertion states an apparent paradox — sharing ought to be symmetric, yet many covalent molecules have a dipole moment. The reason resolves it exactly: "sharing" never meant "sharing equally". When one atom is more electronegative, the bonding pair spends more of its time in that atom's neighbourhood, which is precisely what a partial charge separation is. Reason is not merely a true statement sitting alongside the assertion — it is the mechanism behind it.
Key relation δ⁻ on the more electronegative atom, δ⁺ on the other; μ = q × d. In HCl, ΔEN = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, so the pair sits nearer chlorine: Hδ+–Clδ− with μ = 1.03 D. In H₂ or Cl₂, ΔEN = 0 and the sharing is genuinely equal, so μ = 0.
Solution
1. Test the assertion. Covalent bonding is defined as sharing, yet HCl, H₂O and NH₃ all have measurable dipole moments (1.03 D, 1.85 D and 1.47 D). Some covalent molecules are undeniably polar ⇒ assertion true.
2. Test the reason. The more electronegative atom attracts the shared pair more strongly, so the electron density — the time-averaged position of the pair — is displaced towards it, creating δ⁻ there and δ⁺ on the partner ⇒ reason true.
3. Test the link. The assertion's paradox is "sharing yet polar". The reason dissolves it by pointing out that sharing is unequal whenever the two atoms differ in electronegativity. That is a genuine cause-and-effect explanation, not an unrelated fact ⇒ the reason is the correct explanation.Compare with a merely-true-but-unrelated reason, which would earn option (d) instead.
4. Therefore both are true and the reason explains the assertion ⇒ option (c).
5. Watch the important refinement: polar bonds do not always make a polar molecule. CCl₄, CO₂, BF₃ and SF₆ all contain polar bonds, but their symmetry cancels the vectors and μ = 0. The assertion says "some covalent molecules are polar", which correctly allows for both cases.
6. Eliminate (a) and (b), each of which brands one true statement false; eliminate (d), which accepts both statements but denies the causal link that plainly exists.

Concept Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions collapse into one mole of crystal, and it is governed by Coulomb's law: it scales with the product of the ionic charges and inversely with the inter-ionic distance. Because the charges enter as a product they dominate — doubling both charges quadruples the numerator, whereas realistic size changes alter the denominator by only tens of percent. So the strategy is always the same: sort by charge product first, and use size only to separate compounds with the same charge product.
Key relation U ∝ (z⁺ × z⁻) ÷ (r⁺ + r⁻). Radii used: K⁺ 133, Br⁻ 195, Li⁺ 60, F⁻ 136, Mg²⁺ 65, O²⁻ 140, Fe²⁺ 78, Fe³⁺ 64 pm.
Solution
1. Part (a), charge products first. KBr is (1)(1) = 1; LiF is (1)(1) = 1; MgO is (2)(2) = 4. MgO's charge product is four times either of the others, so MgO is the largest — and it also has small ions, which only reinforces the lead.
2. Separate KBr from LiF on size, since their charge products tie. r⁺ + r⁻ for KBr = 133 + 195 = 328 pm; for LiF = 60 + 136 = 196 pm. The much shorter LiF separation gives it the larger lattice energy.Experimental values confirm the ranking: KBr ≈ 671, LiF ≈ 1023, MgO ≈ 3795 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Hence increasing lattice energy is KBr < LiF < MgO.
4. Part (b). Both oxides share O²⁻, so compare the iron ions. FeO gives (2)(2) = 4; Fe₂O₃ gives (3)(2) = 6.
5. Size reinforces the charge effect: Fe³⁺ (64 pm) is smaller than Fe²⁺ (78 pm) because removing a third electron lets the nucleus contract the remainder. Numerator up, denominator down ⇒ FeO < Fe₂O₃.Measured lattice energies are roughly 3 900 kJ mol⁻¹ for FeO against about 14 800 for Fe₂O₃ — the gap is huge because the formula unit also contains five ions rather than two.

Concept NaCl and MgO are the textbook pair for isolating the charge effect, because they are structurally identical — both adopt the rock-salt lattice with 6 : 6 coordination and therefore share the same Madelung constant.
