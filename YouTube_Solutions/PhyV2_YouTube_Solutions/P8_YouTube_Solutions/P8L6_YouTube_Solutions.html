1What type of potential energy is stored in a compressed or stretched spring? (a) Gravitational potential energy (b) Mechanical energy (c) Elastic potential energy (d) Kinetic energy — (Level-1)Elastic potential energyDeriving Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring from Hooke's Law (Class 11/12, JEE)🟡 SimilarcElastic potential energy
Concept Potential energy is always named after the interaction that stores it, not after the object. A spring stores energy because its own coils are elastically deformed — the restoring force is internal to the spring, so the store is called elastic potential energy. "Gravitational" would require a change of height against Earth's pull; "kinetic" requires motion, and a compressed spring held at rest has none.
Formula Uelastic = ½ k x² — U in joules (J), k the spring constant in N/m, x the deformation from natural length in m. Compare with Ugrav = m g h — m in kg, g in m/s², h in m. Dimensional check: (N/m)(m²) = N·m = J ✓ and (kg)(m/s²)(m) = kg·m²/s² = J ✓ — both are energies, but they are stored by different interactions.
Solution
1. Identify the agent that resists the deformation. For a spring stretched or compressed by x, that agent is the spring force F = −k x (N).The minus sign says the force always points back toward the natural length — it is a restoring, conservative force, so a potential energy can be defined for it.
2. Work done on the spring to deform it by x, stored as potential energy:
  U = ∫₀ˣ k x′ dx′ = ½ k x² (J).The force is not constant, so the work is an integral, not F×x; that is exactly why the ½ appears.
3. This store depends only on the shape (configuration) of the spring, not on its height or its speed ⇒ it is elastic potential energy.
4. Eliminate the distractors: (a) needs a height change against gravity — none here; (b) "mechanical energy" is the sum KE + PE, a broader term, not a type of PE; (d) kinetic energy needs v ≠ 0.

Concept Near the Earth's surface the gravitational force mg (N) is essentially constant, so the work needed to raise a body slowly through a height h is simply force × distance = mgh — and that work is stored as gravitational potential energy. The zero level is arbitrary; here "above ground" fixes h = 5 m measured from the ground.
Formula U = m g h — U in J, m in kg, g = 10 m/s² (stated in the question), h in m. kg × m/s² × m = kg·m²/s² = J ✓, so the expression really is an energy.
Solution
1. List the data with units: m = 2 kg, h = 5 m, g = 10 m/s² (the question tells you to use 10, not 9.8).
2. The lifting agent must supply an upward force equal in magnitude to the weight, F = mg = 2 × 10 = 20 N."Raised" means quasi-statically — no leftover kinetic energy — so the applied force just balances the weight.
3. Work done against gravity over the vertical displacement h:
  W = F h = 20 N × 5 m = 100 J.
4. All of that work is stored: U = mgh = 2 × 10 × 5 = 100 J.

3A mass of M kg is suspended by a weightless string. Calculate the horizontal force that is required to displace it until the string makes an angle of 45° with the initial vertical direction. — (Level-3)Constant horizontal force on pendulumMaximum Position of a Pendulum Pushed Horizontally — Energy vs Force🟡 SimilarF = Mg(√2 − 1) ≈ 0.414 Mg
Concept This question has two different right answers depending on how the force is applied, and the answer key tells you which one the book wants. If the force were increased gradually so the bob is in equilibrium at every angle, you would need F = Mg tan45° = Mg. But if a single constant horizontal force F is switched on and the bob swings from rest to rest at 45°, energy bookkeeping gives a smaller force — because the bob overshoots the equilibrium angle. The key value Mg(√2−1) is the constant-force answer, so use the work–energy theorem.
Formula Work–energy theorem between the two rest positions: WF + Wgravity = ΔKE = 0 (J). Geometry of a string of length L (m) swung to angle θ: horizontal displacement x = L sinθ (m), rise h = L(1 − cosθ) (m). So F·L sinθ = M g L(1 − cosθ). Both sides are (N)(m) = J ✓; L cancels, so the answer cannot depend on the string length.
Solution
1. Start: bob hanging at rest, string vertical. End: bob momentarily at rest with the string at θ = 45°. Both KE values are zero, so ΔKE = 0."Displace it until the string makes 45°" = the extreme of the swing, where the bob is instantaneously at rest.
2. Work done by the constant horizontal force over the horizontal displacement:
  WF = F × L sin45° = F L /√2 (J).Only the component of displacement along F counts; the vertical rise is perpendicular to F and contributes nothing.
3. Work done by gravity as the bob rises by h = L(1 − cos45°):
  Wg = − M g L (1 − 1/√2) (J).Negative because gravity is down while the displacement has an upward component.
4. Set the total to zero and cancel L:
  F L/√2 = M g L (1 − 1/√2) ⇒ F/√2 = Mg(1 − 1/√2)
5. Multiply both sides by √2:
  F = Mg(√2 − 1) ≈ Mg(1.414 − 1) = 0.414 Mg newtons.Units: (kg)(m/s²) = N ✓. Note F < Mg, exactly as expected for a force that is allowed to overshoot.

4An elastic string of unstretched length L and force constant k is stretched by a small length x. It is further stretched by another small length y. The work done in the second stretching is (a) ½ky² (b) ½k(x² + y²) (c) ½k(x + y)² (d) ½ky(2x + y) — (Level-4)Work in a second stretchWork To Stretch A Spring Using Hooke's Law (force & spring constant)🟡 Similard½ k y (2x + y)
Concept The whole point of this question is that spring energy is quadratic, so work done in a stretch is not proportional to the extra length — it depends on where you started. Work in any stage = (final stored energy) − (initial stored energy), never ½k(extra)². The natural length L is a pure distractor: it never enters the energy formula.
Formula U(e) = ½ k e² — U in J, k in N/m, e the extension from natural length in m. Work in a stage: W = Ufinal − Uinitial = ½k(e₂² − e₁²) (J). (N/m)(m²) = J ✓. Equivalent integral form W = ∫e₁e₂ k e de gives the same thing.
Solution
1. After the first stretch the extension is e₁ = x, so the stored energy is U₁ = ½ k x² (J).
2. After the second stretch the total extension is e₂ = x + y (measured from natural length, not from the first position), so U₂ = ½ k (x + y)² (J).This is the step everyone gets wrong: extensions add from the natural length, so you must use (x+y), not y alone.
3. Work done in the second stretching:
  W₂ = U₂ − U₁ = ½k(x + y)² − ½k x²
4. Expand (x + y)² = x² + 2xy + y²:
  W₂ = ½k(x² + 2xy + y² − x²) = ½k(2xy + y²)
5. Factor out y:
  W₂ = ½ k y (2x + y) = option (d).Units: (N/m)(m)(m) = N·m = J ✓.
6. Cross-check the limit x → 0 (i.e. the second stretch is actually the first): W₂ → ½k·y·y = ½ky², which is the familiar single-stretch result ✓.

⚡ JEE Trick Use the average-force shortcut: in a linear spring the force during the second stage goes from kx to k(x+y), average = k(x + y/2); multiply by the distance y ⇒ W = ky(x + y/2) = ½ky(2x+y) — one line, no expansion. Trap: option (a) ½ky² is the answer only if the string started unstretched; it ignores the cross term kxy, which is precisely the "you were already stretched" penalty. Option (c) ½k(x+y)² is the total energy after both stretches, not the work of the second one.
Answer: (d) ½ k y (2x + y).
5A force F = 30 N acts parallel to the inclined plane as it accelerates a block of mass m = 2 kg up the 30° incline, with a coefficient of kinetic friction μk = 0.3. Find the speed of the block after travelling 0.2 m up the incline. — (Level-5)Four-work energy balance on inclineEnergy Conservation — Block on a rough incline with a spring🟡 Similarv ≈ 1.49 m s⁻¹
Concept Four different agents do work here — the applied force, gravity, friction and the spring — and only one of them (the spring) has a non-constant force. Trying to use Newton's second law would force you to solve a differential equation, because the net force changes with position. The work–energy theorem sidesteps that entirely: add the four works algebraically and set the total equal to the gain in kinetic energy.
Formula WF + Wgrav + Wfric + Wspring = ½ m v² − 0 (all terms in J). Component-wise, with d the distance along the incline (m): WF = F d; Wgrav = − m g d sinθ; Wfric = − μk m g cosθ · d; Wspring = − ½ K d². Every term is (N)(m) = J ✓. Use g = 9.8 m/s² — the printed key value 1.49 m/s only comes out with 9.8, not with 10.
Solution
1. Data: F = 30 N, m = 2 kg, θ = 30°, μk = 0.3, K = 40 N/m, d = 0.2 m, g = 9.8 m/s², v0 = 0.
2. Applied force (parallel to the incline, along the motion):
  WF = 30 × 0.2 = +6.000 J
3. Gravity: the along-incline component of the weight is mg sin30° = 2 × 9.8 × 0.5 = 9.8 N, pointing down the slope:
  Wgrav = −9.8 × 0.2 = −1.960 J
4. Friction: normal reaction N = mg cos30° = 2 × 9.8 × 0.8660 = 16.97 N, so f = μkN = 0.3 × 16.97 = 5.092 N, opposing the upward motion:
  Wfric = −5.092 × 0.2 = −1.018 JThe spring pulls along the incline, not into it, so it does not change N — this is why the friction force stays constant even though the spring force does not.
5. Spring: it started unstressed and is now stretched by 0.2 m, so it has absorbed
  Wspring = −½ × 40 × (0.2)² = −½ × 40 × 0.04 = −0.800 JNegative: the spring force points back down the slope while the block moves up. The ½ is essential — the force grows from 0 to 8 N, it is not constant.

  ΣW = 6.000 − 1.960 − 1.018 − 0.800 = +2.222 J
7. Work–energy theorem: ½ m v² = 2.222 J ⇒ ½ (2) v² = 2.222 ⇒ v² = 2.222 m²/s²
8. v = √2.222 = 1.49 m/s (up the incline).

Concept The single fact that settles this question is that U = ½kx² contains x squared. Squaring destroys the sign, so a deformation of −5 cm (compression) stores exactly the same energy as +5 cm (extension). Physically: whichever way you push the spring away from its natural length, you do work against the restoring force, so the store can only grow.

4. Eliminate: (a) says "only when stretched" — false, compression also stores energy; (b) and (c) claim U decreases, impossible since U ≥ 0 and U = 0 at the natural length is already the minimum.
