L-1

Moment of Inertia of a Particle and of a System of Particles

Moment of Inertia — what it is

  • Moment of inertia is the property of a body that relates to its mass distribution and measures how strongly it resists rotational motion. It is the rotational analogue of mass.
  • A body at rest with a larger moment of inertia is harder to set into rotation; a body already rotating with a larger moment of inertia is harder to stop.
  • It is never an absolute property of the body: it is always quoted about a specific axis (usually the axis of rotation), and its value depends on how the mass is distributed about THAT axis.
  • For a single particle, I = mr², where m is the mass of the particle and r is the PERPENDICULAR distance of the particle from the axis.
  • Because r is squared, mass placed far from the axis counts far more than mass near it — moving a particle to twice the distance quadruples its contribution.
  • SI unit: kg·m² (the book's examples also use g·cm²); dimensions [M L² T⁰]. It is a scalar for a fixed axis.
I = mr² (single particle, r = perpendicular distance from the axis)
💡 Noter is the perpendicular distance to the AXIS, not the distance to a point on it. From the x-axis r = |y|; from the y-axis r = |x|; from the z-axis r = √(x² + y²).

Moment of Inertia of a System of Particles about a Given Axis

  • For a system of discrete particles, the moments of inertia simply add: sum each particle's mᵢrᵢ² over the whole system.
  • mᵢ is the mass of the i-th particle and rᵢ its perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation; Σ denotes summation over every particle.
  • For three particles of masses m₁, m₂, m₃ at perpendicular distances r₁, r₂, r₃ from the axis, I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² + m₃r₃².
  • Particles may lie on either side of the axis — only the magnitude of the perpendicular distance matters, so there are no sign cancellations and I is always positive.
  • Massless connectors (a 'light rod', a 'massless ring', a 'massless wire frame') contribute nothing; only the point masses enter the sum.
  • The result quantifies the system's resistance to rotation about that axis and depends on both the masses and their distances from the axis.
I = Σ mᵢ rᵢ²
I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² + m₃r₃² (three-particle system)
★ RememberMoment of inertia is additive over particles: build any discrete system by adding one mr² term per mass.
⚠ TrapChange the axis and every rᵢ changes, so I changes. A body has no single moment of inertia — quoting one without naming the axis is meaningless.
💡 NoteFor particles that all lie in one plane (z = 0), the perpendicular-axis relation I_z = Iₓ + I_y falls straight out of r² = x² + y² and makes a free check on any three-axis calculation.

Moment of Inertia of a Continuous Body — Ring, Rod and Disc (with Derivations)

Moment of Inertia of a Continuous Body

  • For a continuous body the mass is not in discrete lumps, so the discrete sum Σmᵢrᵢ² is replaced by an integral over the mass distribution.
  • Divide the body into infinitesimally small mass elements dm. Let r be the PERPENDICULAR distance of that element from the axis of rotation.
  • The contribution of one element is dI = r² dm; summing (integrating) over the whole body gives the total moment of inertia.
  • ∫ denotes integration over the entire mass distribution; r is the perpendicular distance of the element from the axis, dm the mass element.
  • Moment of inertia quantifies a body's resistance to ROTATIONAL motion about a given axis, and depends on both the mass distribution and the distances of the mass elements from the axis.
  • Because r is measured from the axis, the same body has different I about different axes — I is not a property of the body alone but of the body-plus-axis.
dI = r² dm
I = ∫ r² dm
Linear bodies: dm = λ dx with λ = m/l (mass per unit length)
Laminae: dm = σ dA with σ = m/A (mass per unit area)
💡 NoteThe whole technique is three steps: (1) choose an element every point of which is at the SAME distance from the axis, (2) write dm in terms of that distance using the density, (3) integrate r² dm over the correct limits.
⚠ Trapr is the perpendicular distance from the AXIS, not the distance from the origin or from the centre of mass. Choosing an element whose points lie at different distances from the axis makes dI = r² dm invalid.

Moment of Inertia of a Rectangular Plate (Examples 1 and 2)

  • Rectangular plate of mass m with sides a and b; axis 1 is the in-plane central axis parallel to a, axis 2 the in-plane central axis parallel to b, axis 3 the central axis perpendicular to the plate.
  • For axis 1 take a strip of length a and width dy at distance y: dm = (m/ab)(a dy) = (m/b) dy; integrating y from −b/2 to +b/2 gives I₁ = mb²/12.
  • For axis 2 take a strip of height b and width dx at distance x: dm = (m/ab)(b dx) = (m/a) dx; integrating x from −a/2 to +a/2 gives I₂ = ma²/12.
  • The dimension PARALLEL to the axis never appears in the answer — a lamina about an in-plane central axis behaves exactly like a rod of length equal to the perpendicular dimension.
σ = m/(ab) (mass per unit area)
Axis 1 (parallel to side a): I₁ = ∫(−b/2 → b/2) y²(m/b) dy = (m/b)(b³/12) = mb²/12
Axis 2 (parallel to side b): I₂ = ∫(−a/2 → a/2) x²(m/a) dx = (m/a)(a³/12) = ma²/12
Axis 3 (perpendicular, by perpendicular-axis theorem): I₃ = I₁ + I₂ = m(a² + b²)/12
★ RememberRemember I = m(perpendicular dimension)²/12 for a central in-plane axis of a rectangular lamina; it is the rod formula ml²/12 in disguise.

Moment of Inertia of a Ring (central perpendicular axis)

  • Ring of mass M and radius R, axis through the centre and perpendicular to the plane of the ring.
  • Every elemental mass dm on the ring lies at exactly the same distance R from the axis, so R² comes outside the integral.
  • ∫dm over the whole ring is simply the total mass M, giving I = MR² immediately — no real integration is needed.
  • This is the largest possible I for a given M and R, because all the mass sits at the greatest possible distance from the axis.
I = ∫ R² dm = R² ∫ dm = MR²
💡 NoteThe ring result MR² holds for a NON-uniform ring too — the derivation never assumed uniform mass distribution, only that every element is at distance R.

Moment of Inertia of a Rod about its Perpendicular Bisector

  • Rod of mass m and length l; axis AB is perpendicular to the rod and passes through its midpoint O.
  • Take an element of length dx at distance x from the centre O; its mass is dm = (m/l) dx, where m/l is the linear mass density.
  • dI = x² dm = (m/l)x² dx; integrate x from −l/2 to +l/2 to cover the two halves of the rod.
  • The rod is assumed thin, so its transverse dimension contributes nothing.
dm = (m/l) dx, dI = (m/l)x² dx
I = (m/l)∫(−l/2 → l/2) x² dx = (m/l)[x³/3] from −l/2 to l/2 = (m/l)(l³/24 + l³/24) = ml²/12
⚠ Trap⚠ Book wording slip (page 51): the text says the element dx is taken at distance x from ONE END of the rod, but the limits used are −l/2 to +l/2. For this derivation x is measured from the CENTRE O; measuring from an end requires limits 0 to l, which is the next derivation and gives ml²/3.

Moment of Inertia of a Rod about its End

  • Same rod and same element, but x is now measured from one end and the axis passes through that end, perpendicular to the rod.
  • The only change from the previous derivation is the limits: 0 to l instead of −l/2 to +l/2.
  • Result I = ml²/3, four times the value about the perpendicular bisector.
  • Consistent with the parallel-axis theorem: I_end = I_centre + m(l/2)² = ml²/12 + ml²/4 = ml²/3.
I = ∫(0 → l) x²(m/l) dx = (m/l)[x³/3] from 0 to l = (m/l)(l³/3 − 0) = ml²/3
I_end / I_centre = (ml²/3)/(ml²/12) = 4
★ RememberThe 4 : 1 ratio between the end axis and the central axis of a rod is a standard one-line MCQ answer.

Moment of Inertia of a Disc (central perpendicular axis)

  • Treat the disc of mass m and radius R as a collection of infinitesimally thin concentric rings; axis OX is perpendicular to the disc through the centre O.
  • Draw two concentric circles of radii x and x + dx. The elemental ring area is π[(x+dx)² − x²] = 2πx dx (the dx² term is dropped as second order).
  • Mass per unit area of the disc is m/(πR²); multiplying by the ring area gives dm = [m/(πR²)]·2πx dx = (2mx/R²) dx.
  • Each such ring has all its mass at distance x from the axis, so dI = x² dm — the ring result MR² applied to the elemental ring.
  • Integrate x from 0 to R to sweep the whole disc.
Elemental ring area = π[(x+dx)² − x²] = 2πx dx
dm = [m/(πR²)]·2πx dx = (2mx/R²) dx
I = ∫(0 → R) x²(2mx/R²) dx = (2m/R²)∫(0 → R) x³ dx = (2m/R²)[x⁴/4] from 0 to R = mR²/2
💡 NoteBuilding a disc out of rings is the model for every 2-D body: pick the element on which a KNOWN result already applies, then integrate that known result.
★ RememberStandard set to memorise: ring about central ⊥ axis = MR²; disc about central ⊥ axis = MR²/2; rod about perpendicular bisector = ml²/12; rod about end = ml²/3; rectangular lamina about in-plane central axis = m(⊥ dimension)²/12.

Unit conversion caution (worked Examples 3 and 4)

  • Moment of inertia has dimensions [M][L²], so converting CGS to SI uses 1 g·cm² = 10⁻³ kg × 10⁻⁴ m² = 10⁻⁷ kg·m².
  • Examples 3 and 4 in the book carry out this conversion wrongly — see the misprint notes on those examples.
  • Correct values: rod (200 g, 10 cm) gives 1666.67 g·cm² = 1.667×10⁻⁴ kg·m² about the bisector and 6666.67 g·cm² = 6.667×10⁻⁴ kg·m² about an end; disc (100 g, 2 cm) gives 200 g·cm² = 2×10⁻⁵ kg·m².
1 g·cm² = 10⁻⁷ kg·m²
Radius of gyration: k = √(I/m); ring k = R, disc k = R/√2, rod about its bisector k = l/(2√3), rod about its end k = l/√3

Moment of Inertia of Sphere; Perpendicular and Parallel Axis Theorems

Moment of Inertia of a Solid Sphere

  • Moment of inertia measures a body's resistance to a change in its rotational motion — the rotational analogue of mass.
  • For a uniform solid sphere the moment of inertia about any diameter (an axis through the centre of mass) is I = (2/5) M R².
  • M is the mass of the sphere and R its radius; the (2/5) is a pure number fixed by how the mass is distributed, obtained by integration (calculus).
  • Because every diameter of a sphere is equivalent, the same value holds for all axes through the centre.
I(solid sphere, diameter) = (2/5) M R²
⚠ TrapThe book says the (2/5) factor 'represents the fraction of the total mass concentrated at the outer shell'. That wording is wrong — it is a geometric shape factor from integrating r² dm, not a mass fraction. Same slip is repeated for the (2/3) of the hollow sphere.

Moment of Inertia of a Hollow Sphere (thin spherical shell)

  • For a thin hollow sphere about a diameter, I = (2/3) M R².
  • The shape factor is larger than the solid sphere's because all of the shell's mass sits at the maximum distance R from the centre.
  • For equal M and R, I(shell) / I(solid) = (2/3)/(2/5) = 5/3.
I(hollow sphere / thin shell, diameter) = (2/3) M R²
I(shell) : I(solid sphere) = 5 : 3
★ RememberMemorise the family of shape factors k in I = k M R²: ring/hoop & thin cylindrical shell 1, disc & solid cylinder 1/2, solid sphere 2/5, spherical shell 2/3.

Perpendicular Axis Theorem

  • Applies only to a PLANE body (a lamina) lying entirely in one plane.
  • Take the x- and y-axes in the plane of the body and the z-axis perpendicular to the plane, all three meeting at one point.
  • Then the moment of inertia about the perpendicular (z) axis equals the sum of the moments of inertia about the two perpendicular in-plane axes: I_z = I_x + I_y.
  • Proof: for a particle P with foot of perpendiculars Q on the x-axis and R on the y-axis, I_z = Σ m_i (PO)² = Σ m_i [(PQ)² + (OQ)²] = Σ m_i [(PQ)² + (PR)²] = Σ m_i (PQ)² + Σ m_i (PR)² = I_x + I_y.
  • The theorem is a consequence of the geometry of plane bodies and CANNOT be extended to three-dimensional bodies.
  • For a rectangular plate of sides a and b about the central axis perpendicular to its plane, I = (1/12) M (a² + b²).
I_z = I_x + I_y (plane bodies only)
I(rectangular plate, ⊥ to plane through centre) = (1/12) M (a² + b²)
💡 NoteFor a plane body with I_x = I_y (ring, disc, square plate), the theorem gives the in-plane value immediately: I(in-plane axis) = I_z / 2.
⚠ TrapBook wording: 'For three-dimensional bodies, a different principle known as the Parallel Axis Theorem is used.' Misleading — the parallel axis theorem is about shifting to a PARALLEL axis (any body, 2D or 3D); it is not a 3D replacement for the perpendicular axis theorem.

Results from the Perpendicular Axis Theorem

  • Ring of mass M, radius R: I_z = M R² (all mass at distance R) ⇒ I about a diameter = M R² / 2.
  • Disc of mass M, radius R: I_z = M R² / 2 ⇒ I about a diameter = M R² / 4.
  • In both cases I_x = I_y is used, which holds because the body is uniform (symmetric).
I(ring, diameter) = M R² / 2
I(disc, diameter) = M R² / 4

Parallel Axis Theorem

  • Gives the moment of inertia about an axis AB that is parallel to an axis CZ through the centre of mass, at perpendicular distance d from it.
  • I = I₀ + M d², where I₀ is the moment of inertia about the parallel axis through the centre of mass, M the total mass and d the perpendicular separation of the two axes.
  • Proof: take the centre of mass at the origin with CZ as the z-axis and CA as the x-axis, so A = (d, 0, 0). For a particle P(x_i, y_i, z_i), I = Σ m_i (PR)² = Σ m_i [(x_i − d)² + y_i²] = Σ m_i (x_i² + y_i²) − 2d Σ m_i x_i + Σ m_i d².
  • Since the origin is the centre of mass, Σ m_i x_i = 0, and Σ m_i (x_i² + y_i²) = I₀, leaving I = I₀ + M d².
  • It works for bodies of any shape, 2D or 3D; the only requirement is that the two axes be parallel and that I₀ be taken about the axis through the CENTRE OF MASS.
  • Because M d² ≥ 0, the moment of inertia is always least about an axis through the centre of mass.
I = I₀ + M d²
Σ m_i x_i = 0 at the centre of mass
⚠ TrapI₀ must be about the axis through the centre of mass. Shifting between two non-central parallel axes directly with Md² is wrong — go through the CM axis both times.

Radius of Gyration

  • The radius of gyration K is the distance from the axis at which the whole mass could be concentrated to give the same moment of inertia.
  • It is defined by I = M K², so K = √(I / M).
  • Example: solid sphere about a tangent, I = (7/5) M R² ⇒ K = √(7/5) R ≈ 1.18 R.
  • K depends on the axis chosen, not just on the body.
I = M K²
K = √(I / M)
💡 NoteTo get K in an exam, never re-derive: compute I first, then equate it to M K².

Standard parallel-axis results worked in this lecture

  • Solid cylinder about a line on its surface parallel to its own axis: I = M R² / 2 + M R² = (3/2) M R².
  • Thin spherical shell about a tangent: I = (2/3) M R² + M R² = (5/3) M R².
  • For a tangential axis, d = R always.
I(solid sphere, tangent) = (7/5) M R²
I(solid cylinder, surface line ∥ axis) = (3/2) M R²
I(shell, tangent) = (5/3) M R²

Rotational Kinematics, Rotational Kinetic Energy, Torque and Rotational Equilibrium

Rotational kinematics — the idea

  • The rotational kinematic equations are the exact analogues of the linear equations, written for a rigid body rotating about a FIXED axis.
  • They let you find angular velocity ω, angular displacement θ and angular acceleration α for a body rotating with CONSTANT angular acceleration.
  • Validity: every one of the three equations assumes α stays constant over the interval. If α varies with time, go back to the differential definitions (α = dω/dt, ω = dθ/dt) and integrate, or use numerical methods.
  • Linear ↔ angular dictionary: s ↔ θ, v ↔ ω, a ↔ α, m ↔ I, F ↔ τ.
ω = dθ/dt
α = dω/dt = ω(dω/dθ)
v = ωr (linear speed of a particle at distance r from the axis)
⚠ TrapDo not use ω = ω₀ + αt when α is given as a function of time (e.g. α = 4at³ − 3bt²) — integrate instead.

First equation of motion: ω = ω₀ + αt

  • Relates the final angular velocity ω to the initial angular velocity ω₀, the angular acceleration α and the elapsed time t.
  • Derivation: from α = dω/dt we get dω = α dt; integrating ω from ω₀ to ω and t from 0 to t gives (ω) from ω₀ to ω = α(t) from 0 to t, i.e. ω − ω₀ = αt.
dω = α dt
∫(ω₀→ω) dω = ∫(0→t) α dt ⇒ ω − ω₀ = αt
ω = ω₀ + αt

Second equation of motion: θ = ω₀t + (1/2)αt²

  • Relates the angular displacement θ to ω₀, α and t: θ is the product ω₀t plus half the product of α and t².
  • Derivation: from ω = dθ/dt we get dθ = ω dt = (ω₀ + αt)dt; integrating θ from 0 to θ and t from 0 to t gives the result.
∫(0→t)(ω₀ + αt)dt = ∫(0→θ) dθ
[ω₀t + (1/2)αt²] from 0 to t = (θ) from 0 to θ
θ = ω₀t + (1/2)αt²
💡 NoteAngles may be kept in revolutions throughout, provided ω is in rev/s and α in rev/s² — that kills every factor of 2π (see Example 1).

Third equation of motion: ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

  • The time-free equation: relates the final angular velocity to ω₀, α and the angular displacement θ covered.
  • Derivation: write α = dω/dt = (dω/dθ)(dθ/dt) = ω(dω/dθ), so α dθ = ω dω; integrating θ from 0 to θ and ω from ω₀ to ω gives αθ = (ω²/2) − (ω₀²/2).
α = (dω/dθ)(dθ/dt) = ω(dω/dθ)
α dθ = ω dω
∫(0→θ) α dθ = ∫(ω₀→ω) ω dω ⇒ αθ = (ω²/2) − (ω₀²/2)
ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ
★ RememberUse this whenever time is neither given nor asked — it is the one-step route.

Rotational kinetic energy

  • Rotational kinetic energy is the energy associated with the rotational motion of a rigid body.
  • Kinetic energy of ONE particle i of a body rotating about the line PQ with angular speed ω: Kᵢ = (1/2)mvᵢ², and since vᵢ = ωrᵢ, Kᵢ = (1/2)mω²rᵢ², where rᵢ is the radius of the circle that particle describes about the axis.
  • Kinetic energy of the WHOLE body: sum over all N particles. ω is the SAME for every particle of a rigid body, so it comes out of the sum: K = Σ(1/2)mᵢω²rᵢ² = (1/2)ω²Σmᵢrᵢ² = (1/2)Iω².
  • So the total rotational KE is just the sum of the individual particles' kinetic energies, repackaged through the moment of inertia I = Σmᵢrᵢ².
Kᵢ = (1/2)mvᵢ² = (1/2)mω²rᵢ²
v = ω·r
K = Σ (1/2)mᵢω²rᵢ² = (1/2)Iω²
I = Σ mᵢrᵢ² (continuous body: I = ∫r² dm)
💡 Noteω factors out of the sum only because the body is RIGID — every particle shares the same ω. The rᵢ are perpendicular distances from the AXIS, never distances from a point.

Torque

  • Torque measures the turning or twisting effect produced by a force on an object; it is a vector quantity.
  • It depends on both the magnitude of the force and the perpendicular distance from the line of action of the force to the axis of rotation.
  • For a force F acting on a particle P at position vector r with respect to a point O, the torque about O is the cross product τ = r × F.
  • By the right-hand rule for cross products, τ is perpendicular to both r and F.
τ = r × F
|τ| = r F sin φ = (perpendicular distance) × F
τ = determinant of rows (î, ĵ, k̂), (x, y, z), (Fx, Fy, Fz)
⚠ TrapOrder matters: τ = r × F, never F × r — that flips the sign.
💡 NoteCheck a computed τ instantly: it must satisfy τ·r = 0 and τ·F = 0.

Torque about a line (an axis)

  • For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis AB with a force F applied at a point P: take a point O on the axis AB and compute τ = r × F, where r is the position vector from O to P.
  • The component of this τ ALONG the axis AB is what is called the torque of F about AB — only this component can change the rotation about that axis.
  • That axial component equals τ = r⊥F, where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance between the LINE OF ACTION of the force and the axis AB.
τ (about the point O) = r × F
τ (about the line AB) = r⊥ F, r⊥ = perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of F
★ RememberA force whose line of action meets the axis, or runs parallel to it, produces zero torque about that axis.

Couple

  • A couple is a pair of equal and opposite forces acting on an object along DIFFERENT lines of action.
  • The two forces have zero resultant, so a couple produces no translational motion — only a rotational effect; it tends to rotate the object without moving it in a straight line.
  • Taking a point O midway between the two lines, each force contributes F(d/2), so the total torque is τ = Fd, where F is the magnitude of one force and d the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action.
  • Key property: the torque produced by a couple is INDEPENDENT of the point about which it is calculated — a couple is a pure rotational effect with no net translation.
  • Applications: creating torque in engines, motors and other rotating devices; the study of rotational equilibrium and the analysis of rotational motion.
τ = F(d/2) + F(d/2) = Fd
Net force of a couple = F + (−F) = 0
★ RememberBecause a couple's torque is point-independent, take moments about ANY convenient point in a problem containing couples.

τ = Iα and rotational equilibrium

  • Newton's second law for rotational dynamics of a rigid body: the net torque equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration, τ = Iα.
  • The angular acceleration is directly proportional to the net torque and inversely proportional to the moment of inertia about the axis. I represents the body's resistance to rotational motion, just as m represents resistance to linear motion.
  • Rotational equilibrium is the state of zero NET torque: the sum of all torques acting on the body balances out, so the body keeps a constant angular velocity or stays at rest. Internal torques always add up to zero, so only external torques matter.
  • Two possibilities in rotational equilibrium: (i) a body initially at rest remains at rest, since there is no torque to cause angular acceleration; (ii) a body already rotating with constant angular velocity continues at the same angular velocity.
  • Applications: balancing objects, designing stable structures, analysing rotating machinery and understanding the stability of systems.
τ = Iα
Rotational equilibrium: Στ = 0, i.e. τ(net) = 0
(Full static equilibrium also requires ΣF = 0)
⚠ TrapΣτ = 0 alone is NOT equilibrium — a body can have zero net torque while accelerating linearly. Static equilibrium needs ΣF = 0 as well.
💡 NoteWhen a string over a wheel/pulley does not slip, link the two worlds with v = ωr and a = αr; that one constraint turns a rotational problem into a single-unknown linear one.

The weights are vertical, so the moment arm of each is the HORIZONTAL distance from O: r₁ = (l/2)sin α for C₁ and r₂ = l sin β for C₂.
Equilibrium ⇒ net torque about the hinge O is zero, and the two weights turn the body opposite ways: mg·r₁ − 2mg·r₂ = 0.
Substitute: (mg)(l/2)sin α − (2mg)(l sin β) = 0 ⇒ (1/2)sin α = 2 sin β.
Hence sin α / sin β = 4, i.e. sin α = 4 sin β.
(b) Translational equilibrium: the only forces are the two weights (mg + 2mg = 3mg downward) and the hinge reaction N, so N = 3mg vertically upward (no horizontal component, since the weights have none).
(a) sin α / sin β = 4 (sin α = 4 sin β); (b) hinge force N = 3mg, directed vertically upward

Problems on τ = Iα, Work Done in Pure Rotation and Rotational Power

Problems on τ = Iα

  • The rotational analogue of Newton's second law: the net torque about a fixed axis equals the moment of inertia about that axis times the angular acceleration.
  • Standard three-step attack: (1) find I about the stated axis, (2) find the net torque τ about the same axis, (3) use whichever of τ = Iα is the unknown.
  • For a string wrapped over a rim or pulley the pull is tangential, so the lever arm is simply the radius: τ = rF with no component resolution.
  • Moments of inertia used in this lecture: square plate of edge a about one edge, I = ma²/3; wheel/pulley about its centre, I from τ/α.
τ = Iα
τ = rF (force tangential at radius r)
I_square plate about an edge = ma²/3
💡 NoteConvert everything to SI before substituting: g → kg, cm → m. A 120 g, 5 cm plate is 0.12 kg, 0.05 m.
⚠ TrapExample-1's solution line in the book says 'moment of inertia of the rod about its edge' while the body is a square plate — a wording misprint; the formula ma²/3 applied is the correct plate-about-edge result.

Work Done in Pure Rotation of a Rigid Body

  • When a force F acts at the rim of a pivoted wheel and turns it through a small angle dθ in time dt, the work done is F·ds, where ds is the displacement of the point of application.
  • For a wheel of radius R the displacement of the rim point is along the arc: ds = R dθ, with dθ the small angular displacement.
  • Hence the elementary work is dW = FR dθ = τ dθ — the exact rotational analogue of dW = F ds.
  • Total work in turning from θ₁ to θ₂ is the integral of τ dθ; for a constant torque this collapses to W = τΔθ.
  • Only the tangential component of the force does work and produces torque. The component along the radius (the axial/radial component) contributes nothing, because the displacement of the point of application is purely tangential.
  • Therefore the expression W = τΔθ is valid for any direction of the applied force — the formula already accounts for the inactive radial part through τ.
  • The idea quantifies the energy transfer in rotational motion and is the working tool for wheels, gears, turbines and other rotating machinery.
  • Combined with the work–energy theorem it gives the standard solving route: τΔθ = (1/2)I(ω_f² − ωᵢ²).
dW = F·ds, ds = R dθ
dW = FR dθ = τ dθ
W = ∫ from θ₁ to θ₂ of τ dθ = τΔθ (constant τ)
τΔθ = (1/2)I(ω_f² − ωᵢ²) (work–energy theorem for rotation)
Δθ = 2πn for n complete revolutions
★ RememberFor a string unwinding off a pulley, W = FRΔθ = Fd where d = RΔθ is the length of string unwound — the angle never has to be computed.

💡 Note'After the fifth revolution' means Δθ = 5 × 2π = 10π rad, not 5 rad.

Power in Rotational Motion

  • When a torque acts on a rigid body and does work, by the work–energy theorem that work equals the change in the body's kinetic energy.
  • The rate of doing work in a small interval dt is dW/dt = τ (dθ/dt).
  • Power is defined as the rate at which work is done; in rotation it is called rotational power, P = dW/dt.
  • Substituting dW = τ dθ gives P = τ(dθ/dt), and since angular velocity ω = dθ/dt, this becomes P = τω.
  • So rotational power equals the torque on the body multiplied by its angular velocity — the analogue of P = Fv.
  • Used both ways: given P and ω find τ (Example-5), or given τ and ω find P (Example-6).
  • Important in analysing and designing rotating machinery such as engines, turbines and motors, where power rating and efficiency are critical.
dW/dt = τ (dθ/dt)
P = dW/dt = (τ·dθ)/dt
P = τω
ω (rad/s) = n (rev/min) × 2π/60 = n × π/30
⚠ Traprev/min is NOT rad/s. Always multiply rpm by 2π/60 before using P = τω — this is the commonest slip in these problems.
★ RememberP = τω is the rotational twin of P = Fv; remember the pair together.

Angular Momentum

Angular Momentum of a Particle

  • Angular momentum is the rotational equivalent of linear momentum: it is a property of rotating objects, and it is always defined about a chosen point (or axis), never on its own.
  • For a particle of mass m moving with linear momentum p̄ = mv̄, the angular momentum about a point O is the cross product of the position vector r̄ of the particle from O with its linear momentum p̄.
  • r̄ is the radius vector of the particle from O at the given instant t; p̄ is the instantaneous linear momentum.
  • Magnitude: |L̄| = |r̄||p̄| sin θ, where θ is the angle between r̄ and p̄.
  • Equivalently L = m v r⊥, where r⊥ = r sin θ is the perpendicular distance of the line of action of v̄ from the point O. Drop a perpendicular from O onto the line of v̄ and that length is r⊥.
  • Direction (right-hand rule): curl the fingers of the right hand from the direction of r̄ to the direction of p̄; the thumb points along L̄.
  • Sign convention from the four standard diagrams in the book: clockwise rotation about O gives L̄ perpendicular to the plane and INTO the page (inward); counter-clockwise rotation gives L̄ perpendicular to the plane and OUT of the page (outward).
  • SI unit of angular momentum: kg·m²/s (same as J·s).
  • Angular momentum is fundamental in mechanics, celestial mechanics and quantum mechanics; its conservation is the key tool for analysing rotational dynamics and spinning bodies.
L̄ = r̄ × p̄
L̄ = r̄ × mv̄ = m(r̄ × v̄)
|L̄| = |r̄| |p̄| sin θ
L = m v r sin θ = m v r⊥
r⊥ = r sin θ
💡 NotePull the scalar mass out of the cross product first — L̄ = m(r̄ × v̄) — so the determinant is worked with clean numbers and scaled once at the end.
⚠ TrapAngular momentum is meaningless without naming the point/axis. A particle moving in a straight line has L = 0 about any point ON the line, but L = mvr⊥ ≠ 0 about a point off the line.
★ RememberL̄ for a particle in a circle about the centre: r̄ ⊥ v̄ always, so θ = 90° and L = mvr with no sine factor.

Angular Momentum of a Rigid Body in Pure Rotational Motion (L = Iω)

  • Take a particle P of mass m on a rigid body, moving in a circle of radius r about the axis of rotation AB, with linear velocity v at time t; the origin is at the centre of that circle.
  • For this particle the position vector r̄ and the linear momentum p̄ = mv̄ are perpendicular to each other, so its angular momentum about AB is simply mvr — the component of r̄ measured from the axis AB is just the distance r of P from the axis.
  • Sum over every particle of the body: L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ vᵢ, and since each particle shares the same angular velocity, vᵢ = rᵢ ω.
  • Hence L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ² ω = (Σ mᵢ rᵢ²) ω = Iω, where I = Σ mᵢ rᵢ² is the moment of inertia of the rigid body about the axis AB.
  • I measures the body's resistance to rotational motion about that particular axis, exactly as mass measures resistance to linear acceleration — so L = Iω is the rotational twin of p = mv.
  • Direction: by the right-hand rule, curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation and the thumb gives the direction of L̄ (in the book's figure, upward along the axis AB).
  • Because I depends on the axis, the SAME body spinning at the SAME ω has different L about different axes — e.g. a rod about its end (ml²/3) versus about its centre (ml²/12).
L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ vᵢ
vᵢ = rᵢ ω
L = Σ mᵢ rᵢ² ω = Iω
I = Σ mᵢ rᵢ²
Rod about a perpendicular axis through one end: I = m l²/3
Solid sphere about a diameter: I = (2/5) m r²
Point mass at distance R: I = m R²
ω = 2π/T
💡 NoteBoth routes must agree: for a single particle on a circle, L = mvr and L = Iω = (mr²)(v/r) give the same number — use one as a check on the other.
⚠ TrapL = Iω holds for PURE rotation about a fixed axis. For a body that also translates (rolling), the angular momentum about an outside axis needs the spin term plus the m v r term of the centre of mass.
★ RememberRatio problems (earth spin vs orbit, two bodies) cancel masses and units — form L₁/L₂ = (I₁ω₁)/(I₂ω₂) symbolically first, substitute only at the last step, and keep r and R in the same unit so the conversion is unnecessary.

Relationship Between Angular Momentum, Linear Momentum and Torque; Conservation of Angular Momentum

Angular momentum of a particle

  • The angular momentum of a particle about a reference point O is the cross product of its position vector (measured from O) with its linear momentum.
  • r̄ is the position vector of the particle with respect to the reference point O, and p̄ = mv̄ is the particle's linear momentum.
  • L̄ therefore depends on the choice of reference point — the same motion has different angular momenta about different points.
  • For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis the same quantity reduces to L = Iω, the product of moment of inertia and angular velocity.
L̄ = r̄ × p̄
L = Iω (rigid body, fixed axis)
💡 NoteAlways state the reference point before writing L̄ — 'angular momentum' alone is meaningless without it.

Deriving τ̄ = dL̄/dt

  • Differentiate L̄ = r̄ × p̄ with respect to time using the product rule for cross products.
  • Substitute F̄ = dp̄/dt (Newton's second law) and v̄ = dr̄/dt.
  • The term v̄ × p̄ vanishes: v̄ ∥ p̄ (since p̄ = mv̄), so their cross product is the null vector.
  • What survives is r̄ × F̄, which is precisely the torque τ̄ about the same reference point.
  • Result: in an inertial frame, the rate of change of angular momentum equals the net torque acting on the body — the rotational analogue of F̄ = dp̄/dt.
  • The relation holds component-wise along each axis, so each torque component equals the rate of change of the corresponding angular-momentum component.
  • For a system of particles, the net external torque about a reference point equals the rate of change of the total angular momentum of the system about that same point, in an inertial frame.
dL̄/dt = r̄ × dp̄/dt + (dr̄/dt) × p̄
F̄ = dp̄/dt , v̄ = dr̄/dt
dL̄/dt = r̄ × F̄ + v̄ × p̄ , with v̄ × p̄ = 0 since v̄ ∥ p̄
τ̄ = dL̄/dt = r̄ × F̄
τ_x = (dL/dt)_x , τ_y = (dL/dt)_y , τ_z = (dL/dt)_z
★ Rememberτ̄ = dL̄/dt is the master equation of rotational dynamics; τ = Iα is only its special case for constant I.
⚠ TrapThe reference point for τ̄ and for L̄ must be the SAME point, and the frame must be inertial, or the relation fails.
💡 NoteIf L̄(t) is given as a vector function of time, torque is just its term-by-term derivative — any constant component contributes zero torque.

Conservation of angular momentum

  • If the net external torque on a system about a reference point is zero, the total angular momentum of the system about that point stays constant in both magnitude and direction.
  • This follows directly from τ̄ = dL̄/dt: τ̄ = 0 ⇒ dL̄/dt = 0 ⇒ L̄ = constant.
  • For a body spinning about a fixed axis this reads I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂, so shrinking I makes ω rise and vice versa (the spinning-skater effect).
  • Internal torques — friction between parts, a person walking on a platform, a particle sticking to a rim — can never change the total angular momentum; they only redistribute it among the parts.
  • The angular momentum of individual components of the system CAN change; the changes always cancel so the total is unchanged.
  • Kinetic energy is generally NOT conserved in such 'rotationally inelastic' events — the slipping friction dissipates it.
  • Applications: spinning tops, planets in orbit, rotating galaxies, divers and skaters, and analysis of rotating systems in engineering, astrophysics and quantum mechanics.
τ̄ = 0 ⇒ dL̄/dt = 0 ⇒ L̄ = constant
I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂
L = Iω = constant ⇒ ω ∝ 1/I
K_rot = (1/2)Iω² = L²/(2I)
★ RememberStandard JEE template for 'mass lands on / sticks to a rotating body': Iω = (I + mR²)ω′ ⇒ ω′ = Iω/(I + mR²).
⚠ TrapNever equate kinetic energies in a sticking/dropping problem — only angular momentum is conserved. Two identical wheels coupling lose exactly half the kinetic energy.
💡 NoteSince K = L²/2I at constant L, reducing I raises the kinetic energy — the extra energy comes from the work done pulling the mass inwards.

Kinematics of Combined Motion and Rolling Motion

Kinematics of Combined Motion — velocity of any particle

  • A rigid body in general plane motion does two things at once: its centre of mass C translates with velocity v̄ and the whole body spins about C with angular velocity ω̄.
  • To get the velocity of a particle P sitting a distance r from C, split the motion: velocity of P relative to C (pure rotation) plus velocity of C (pure translation).
  • ω̄ × r̄ is the cross product of the position vector r̄ (drawn from C to P) with the angular velocity vector; its magnitude is rω and its direction is perpendicular to CP, turned the way the body spins.
  • So the velocity of point P is the VECTOR sum of v̄ and the rω term — not an algebraic sum; resolve both into î, ĵ before adding.
  • Every particle of the body has the same ω̄ but a different r̄, which is why different points have different speeds.
v̄PC = v̄P − v̄C
v̄P = v̄PC + v̄C
v̄C = v̄ (centre of mass)
v̄PC = ω̄ × r̄ , |v̄PC| = rω
v̄P = v̄ + ω̄ × r̄
💡 NoteDraw the rω vector first: put it at P, perpendicular to CP, pointing the way the spin carries P. Only then resolve into components.
⚠ TrapThe book writes the rotation term as 'r × ω' in the running text. The correct ordering is ω̄ × r̄ — reversing a cross product flips its sign, so use ω̄ × r̄.

Kinematics of Combined Motion — acceleration of any particle

  • The same split works for acceleration: āP = āPC + āC, where āC = ā is the acceleration of the centre of mass.
  • The relative part āPC is circular motion of P about C, so it has TWO components, tangential and radial.
  • Tangential acceleration a_t is due to the change in angular velocity, has magnitude rα, and is perpendicular to CP.
  • Radial (centripetal) acceleration a_r has magnitude rω² and always points from P towards C.
  • Net acceleration of P is therefore the vector sum of THREE terms: a (of the cm), rα (tangential) and rω² (radial).
  • This framework is what lets us analyse wheels rolling without slipping, and bodies translating along curved paths while spinning.
āPC = āP − āC
āP = āPC + āC , āC = ā
a_t = ᾱ × r̄ , |a_t| = rα
a_r = rω² (directed P → C)
āP = ā + āt + ār ⇒ |āPC| = √((rα)² + (rω²)²)
★ RememberThree terms, always: a_cm + rα + rω². Dropping the rω² term is the single most common mistake in combined-motion problems.
⚠ TrapThe book's text calls a_r 'due to the linear acceleration of the centre of mass' — that is loosely worded. a_r = rω² comes from the ROTATION about C, not from a_cm; a_cm is the separate third term.

Rolling Motion — the pure-rolling condition

  • Rolling motion = translation of the centre of mass (v_cm) combined with rotation of the body about the centre (ω).
  • In PURE rolling there is no relative motion (no sliding) between the contact point and the surface, so the contact point is instantaneously at rest.
  • A rim point P on a purely rolling wheel traces a curve called a cycloid.
  • In one complete rotation the centre of mass advances exactly one circumference, s = 2πR, moving along a straight line.
  • With T the period of one revolution, v_cm = s/T = 2πR/T and ω = 2π/T, which together give the rolling constraint v_cm = Rω.
  • If the wheel slips FORWARD (v_cm > Rω) the centre advances more than 2πR per rotation; if it slips BACKWARD (v_cm < Rω) it advances less than 2πR.
s = 2πR (distance of centre per revolution)
v_cm = s/T = 2πR/T , ω = 2π/T
v_cm = Rω (pure-rolling condition)
v_contact = 0 in pure rolling
v_cm > Rω ⇒ forward slipping ; v_cm < Rω ⇒ backward slipping
★ Rememberv_cm = Rω is the whole of pure rolling. Differentiate it once and you also get a_cm = Rα for the accelerated case.
⚠ TrapThe book's slipping sentence says 'the point P will move a greater distance than the distance travelled by the centre of mass in one complete rotation, 2πR'. What is really being compared is the advance of the CENTRE against 2πR; the rim point's own path length is a separate quantity (8R, derived below).

Velocity of a point on a rolling body

  • Each point of a rolling body carries two velocity vectors: v_cm from translation and ωR from rotation, both of magnitude v_cm in pure rolling.
  • The resultant of two equal vectors of magnitude v_cm enclosing an angle θ is 2v_cm cos(θ/2), where θ is the angle the point makes measured from the TOPmost point.
  • Measuring instead from the contact point (angle α = 180° − θ) gives the equivalent form v_P = 2v_cm sin(α/2).
  • Key rim speeds: topmost point 2v_cm (fastest point of the body), contact point 0 (instantaneously at rest), ends of the horizontal diameter √2·v_cm, and the points 120° from the top move at exactly v_cm.
  • The contact point being at rest means the rolling body can be treated at that instant as in PURE rotation about the contact point (the instantaneous axis of rotation).
v̄P = v̄cm + ω̄ × r̄
v_P = √(v_cm² + v_cm² + 2·v_cm·v_cm·cosθ) = 2v_cm cos(θ/2) (θ from the top)
v_P = 2v_cm sin(α/2) (α from the contact point)
v_top = 2v_cm , v_contact = 0 , v_ends-of-horizontal-diameter = √2·v_cm
💡 NoteTreating the contact point as a fixed pivot turns any rolling question into simple circular motion: v_P = ω·(distance of P from the contact point).
★ RememberMemorise the rim-speed map (2v_cm top, √2 v_cm sides, 0 at contact) — it answers most single-mark rolling MCQs in a line.

Path length of a rim point — the cycloid arch

  • A rim point's speed varies through the revolution, so its path length is found by integrating v_P dt, not by multiplying a constant speed by T.
  • Using α measured from the contact point, v_P = 2v_cm sin(α/2) and dt = dα/ω, so dℓ = (2v_cm/ω) sin(α/2) dα.
  • Integrating α from 0 to 2π gives one complete arch of the cycloid: ℓ = 8v_cm/ω = 8R.
  • Compare: in that same revolution the CENTRE travels only 2πR ≈ 6.28R, so a rim point covers about 27% more distance than the centre.
  • The displacement of the rim point over one revolution, however, is just 2πR (horizontal) — path length and displacement are different things here.
dℓ = v_P dt = 2v_cm sin(α/2) dα/ω
ℓ = ∫₀^{2π} 2v_cm sin(α/2) dα/ω = (2v_cm/ω)(4) = 8v_cm/ω
ℓ = 8R (using v_cm = Rω)
distance of centre in one revolution = 2πR
★ Rememberℓ = 8R per revolution for a rim point — a standard JEE one-liner. Pair it with 2πR for the centre.
💡 NoteBecause v_P = 0 at the contact instant, the cycloid has a sharp cusp at the ground — the rim point is momentarily at rest each revolution, which is also why a rolling tyre leaves a crisp print.

Dynamics of Combined Motion (Rotation + Translation) and Rolling on an Incline

Dynamics of combined motion: the two governing equations

  • In combined motion the translation of the centre of mass and the rotation about the centre of mass are handled simultaneously, by two independent equations.
  • Translation: Newton's second law on the centre of mass — net force = mass × acceleration of the centre of mass.
  • Rotation: Newton's second law for rotation about the centre of mass — net torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration, with τ, I and α all taken about the axis of rotation.
  • The two are linked by the rolling (no-slip) constraint a = rα, which is what makes the pair solvable.
F = ma (translation of the centre of mass)
τ = Iα (rotation about the centre of mass)
a = rα ⇒ α = a/r (pure rolling constraint)
💡 NoteWrite the torque equation about the CENTRE of mass; then mg and N (which pass through the centre) drop out and only the tangential forces survive.

Worked case 1: cylinder falling while suspended by two strings (yo-yo)

  • A cylinder of mass m is suspended by two strings wound on it and falls through a height h, unwinding without slipping.
  • The string portions in contact with the cylinder are at rest, so the points of the cylinder where the strings leave it are instantaneously at rest — the strings play the role of the 'ground' and α = a/r.
  • Translation (taking down as positive): mg − 2T = ma.
  • Rotation about the axis: the two tensions give torque 2Tr, and I = (1/2)mr² for the cylinder, so 2Tr = (1/2)mr²α = (1/2)mra ⇒ 2T = (1/2)ma.
  • Substituting: mg − (1/2)ma = ma ⇒ (3/2)ma = mg ⇒ a = 2g/3, and then T = (1/4)ma = mg/6.
mg − 2T = ma
2Tr = ((1/2)mr²)(a/r) ⇒ 2T = (1/2)ma
a = 2g/3
T = mg/6
v = √(2ah) = √(4gh/3) (speed after falling h, from v² = 2ah)
★ RememberThe falling cylinder accelerates at only 2g/3, not g — a third of gravity goes into spinning it up.

Worked case 2: force applied at the top of a rolling body

  • A sphere of mass m on a rough horizontal surface is pushed by a horizontal force F applied at its highest point; it rolls without slipping.
  • Translation: F + f = ma (write f with sign; the algebra returns its true direction).
  • Rotation about the centre: F acts r above the centre and f acts r below, so Fr − fr = Iα with α = a/r.
  • Sphere, I = (2/5)mr²: (F − f)r = ((2/5)mr²)(a/r) ⇒ F − f = (2/5)ma; adding the two gives 2F = (7/5)ma ⇒ F = (7/10)ma ⇒ a = 10F/(7m).
  • The same algebra for a thin spherical shell (I = (2/3)mr²) gives F = (5/6)ma ⇒ a = 6F/(5m) — this is Example-1.
F + f = ma
F − f = (I/mr²)ma
a = 2F/(m(1 + k²/r²)) (force at the topmost point)

Direction of friction on rolling

  • The direction of friction on a rolling body depends on whether the body is in pure rolling or there is sliding at the contact.
  • In pure rolling there is no sliding at the contact point, so friction there is static (and can be zero). The contact point is at rest with the surface.
  • The normal reaction's line of action passes through the centre, so its torque about the centre is zero; the static friction acts at a point with zero velocity, so it does no work — hence mechanical energy is conserved in pure rolling.
  • If the surface deforms and the sphere slides a little, the contact force is distributed over an area, the normal force is shifted forward and exerts a torque opposing rotation — this is rolling friction.
  • To decide the direction, compute the velocity of the bottom-most point: v_contact = v − rω. If v > rω it is positive, the body slips forward; if v < rω it is negative, the body slips backward. Friction opposes this slipping.
Pure rolling: v = rω ⇒ v_contact = v − rω = 0
v − rω > 0 ⇒ slips forward ⇒ friction backward
v − rω < 0 ⇒ slips backward ⇒ friction forward
Accelerated pure rolling: differentiating v = rω gives a = rα
⚠ TrapNever assume friction opposes the motion of the centre of mass. On a rolling body it opposes the slipping of the CONTACT POINT, so it can point forward.
★ Remembera > Rα ⇒ slips forward, friction backward; a < Rα ⇒ slips backward, friction forward (Example-3).

Rolling on an inclined plane

  • A body of mass M, radius R and moment of inertia I is released on a rough incline of inclination θ; no external force is applied, Mg sinθ already acts at the centre, and friction acts backward (up the incline).
  • Translation along the incline: a = (Mg sinθ − f)/M.
  • Rotation about the centre: α = fR/I, and pure rolling demands a = Rα ⇒ a = fR²/I.
  • Eliminating a: Mg sinθ − f = MfR²/I ⇒ f(1 + MR²/I) = Mg sinθ ⇒ f = Mg sinθ/(1 + MR²/I).
  • Putting f back: a = g sinθ/(1 + I/MR²).
  • Because I_solid < I_hollow, a_solid > a_hollow and t_solid < t_hollow — the solid sphere reaches the bottom first; a is independent of M and R, depending only on the shape factor k²/R² = I/MR².
  • Pure rolling survives only while the required friction does not exceed μMg cosθ, which fixes μ_min; if μ > μ_min the actual friction is the f computed above.
a = g sinθ/(1 + I/MR²)
f = Mg sinθ/(1 + MR²/I)
Mg sinθ/(1 + MR²/I) ≤ μ Mg cosθ ⇒ μ ≥ tanθ/(1 + MR²/I)
μ_min = tanθ/(1 + MR²/I) = tanθ·(k²/R²)/(1 + k²/R²)
v at the bottom (energy): v = √(2gh/(1 + I/MR²))
★ RememberShape-factor table (k²/R²): ring/hollow cylinder 1, disc/solid cylinder 1/2, thin spherical shell 2/3, solid sphere 2/5. Everything on an incline follows from it: a = g sinθ/(1 + k²/R²), μ_min = tanθ·(k²/R²)/(1 + k²/R²).

a = (5/7)g sinθ, f = (2/7)mg sinθ, μ_min = (2/7)tanθ

Energy and Angular Momentum in Combined Motion

Energy in Combined Motion — the setup

  • A body in combined motion has both translation (motion of its centre of mass) and rotation about its centre of mass.
  • In the centre-of-mass frame the body is doing nothing but pure rotation with angular velocity ω — this is what makes the energy split clean.
  • For a particle of mass mᵢ: its lab velocity is the CM-frame velocity plus the CM's velocity, v̄ᵢ = v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀.
v̄ᵢ = v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀
vᵢ' = rᵢ'ω (in the CM frame the body only spins)
💡 NoteEvery combined-motion formula in this lecture comes from one move: write v̄ᵢ = v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀ and expand the square (for KE) or the cross product (for L).

Deriving K = (1/2)I_cm ω² + (1/2)Mv₀²

  • Particle KE in the lab frame: (1/2)mᵢvᵢ² = (1/2)mᵢ(v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀)·(v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀) = (1/2)mᵢvᵢ,cm² + (1/2)mᵢv₀² + mᵢ(v̄ᵢ,cm·v̄₀).
  • Sum over all particles. The first sum is the CM-frame kinetic energy, KE−cm = (1/2)Σmᵢ(vᵢ')², which equals (1/2)I_cm ω² because the body is purely rotating in that frame.
  • The second sum gives (1/2)Mv₀², the translational KE of the whole mass moving with the centre of mass.
  • The cross term Σmᵢ(v̄ᵢ,cm·v̄₀) = 0, because Σmᵢv̄ᵢ,cm/M is the velocity of the centre of mass measured in the centre-of-mass frame, which is zero by definition.
  • So the total kinetic energy splits exactly into 'spin about the CM' plus 'translation of the CM' — no interference term.
(1/2)mᵢvᵢ² = (1/2)mᵢvᵢ,cm² + (1/2)mᵢv₀² + mᵢ(v̄ᵢ,cm·v̄₀)
Σ(1/2)mᵢvᵢ,cm² = (1/2)I_cm ω²
Σmᵢ(v̄ᵢ,cm·v̄₀) = 0
K = (1/2)I_cm ω² + (1/2)Mv₀²
★ RememberK = (1/2)I_cm ω² + (1/2)Mv₀² — König's theorem for a rigid body. Valid for ANY combined motion, rolling or not.

Pure rolling: the one-term form K = (1/2)Iω²

  • For pure rolling the contact point is instantaneously at rest, which locks ω to v: ω = V/R, i.e. v₀ = Rω.
  • Substituting: K = (1/2)I_cm ω² + (1/2)M(Rω)² = (1/2)(I_cm + MR²)ω².
  • By the parallel-axis theorem I_cm + MR² = I, the moment of inertia about the line through the contact point parallel to the axis.
  • Hence a rolling body can be treated as being in PURE rotation about its contact point: K = (1/2)Iω².
  • Standard rolling kinetic energies: sphere (7/10)Mv², disc/solid cylinder (3/4)Mv², ring/hollow cylinder Mv².
ω = V/R (pure rolling)
K = (1/2)(I_cm + MR²)ω²
I_cm + MR² = I (parallel-axis theorem)
K = (1/2)Iω² (I about the contact line)
K = (1/2)(1 + k²/R²)Mv² (k = radius of gyration)
💡 NoteRolling down a height h from rest: v = √(2gh/(1 + k²/R²)). Sphere √(10gh/7) > cylinder √(4gh/3) > ring √(gh) — smaller k²/R² wins, independent of mass and radius.
⚠ TrapThe contact-point shortcut K = (1/2)Iω² works ONLY in pure rolling. If the body slips, go back to K = (1/2)I_cmω² + (1/2)Mv₀² with independent v and ω.

Problems based on conservation of energy during pure rolling

  • In pure rolling the friction force acts at a point that is instantaneously at rest, so static friction does NO work — mechanical energy is conserved.
  • Standard equation: mgh = (1/2)mv² + (1/2)I_cm ω², then eliminate ω using ω = v/r.
  • A body rolling inside a bowl/hemisphere: the CENTRE moves on a circle of radius (R − r), so the drop is (R − r) and the centripetal term is mv²/(R − r).
  • At the lowest point the normal force follows from N − mg = mv²/(R − r), which always exceeds mg.
mgh = (1/2)mv² + (1/2)I_cmω², ω = v/r
Rolling in a bowl: K_f = mg(R − r)
N − mg = mv²/(R − r)
⚠ TrapEnergy is conserved only for ROLLING friction contact (no slipping). The moment slipping starts, friction is kinetic and dissipates energy — use work-energy with f·s_rel instead.

Angular Momentum in Combined Motion

  • Same decomposition as for energy: v̄ᵢ = v̄ᵢ,cm + v̄₀, with r̄ᵢ = r̄ᵢ,cm + r̄₀, where r̄₀ locates the centre of mass relative to the fixed point O.
  • Expanding L̄ = Σmᵢ r̄ᵢ × v̄ᵢ gives four terms; the two mixed terms die because Σmᵢr̄ᵢ,cm = MR̄_cm,cm = 0 and Σmᵢv̄ᵢ,cm = MV̄_cm,cm = 0 (position and velocity of the CM measured in the CM frame are both zero).
  • What survives: the spin angular momentum about the centre of mass plus the 'orbital' angular momentum of the whole mass placed at the centre of mass.
  • For a rolling body about the contact point: L = I_cmω + MvR = (I_cm + MR²)ω = I_contact ω. A rolling solid sphere gives L = (7/5)MvR.
L̄ = Σmᵢ r̄ᵢ × v̄ᵢ
Σmᵢr̄ᵢ,cm = MR̄_cm,cm = 0 ; Σmᵢv̄ᵢ,cm = MV̄_cm,cm = 0
L̄ = L̄_cm + M r̄₀ × v̄₀
L̄_cm = I_cm ω̄
Rolling sphere about contact point: L = (2/5)MvR + MvR = (7/5)MvR
★ RememberL̄ = L̄_cm + M r̄₀ × v̄₀ — spin + orbital. The exact angular-momentum twin of K = (1/2)I_cmω² + (1/2)Mv₀².
💡 NoteIf r̄₀ is parallel to v̄₀ (the CM moves straight along the line to the reference point) the orbital term vanishes and L = I_cmω — the shortcut used in the rod-collision example.

Angular Impulse, Conservation of Angular Momentum in Combined Motion and Toppling

Angular Impulse

  • Angular impulse describes the change in angular momentum of a body over a given time interval — the rotational analogue of linear impulse (which is the change in linear momentum).
  • It is defined as the time integral of the torque acting on the body between the instants t₁ and t₂.
  • If the net torque is constant over the interval, the integral collapses to the simple product (net torque × time interval).
  • Starting from the rotational form of Newton's second law τ = dL/dt, we get dL = τ dt, so integrating gives J = ΔL directly.
  • Conclusion: the angular impulse equals the change in angular momentum of the body, J = L₂ − L₁.
  • Units: N·m·s = kg·m²/s — the same units as angular momentum itself.
J = ∫ τ dt (from t₁ to t₂; J and τ are vectors)
τ = dL/dt ⇒ dL = τ dt
J = ∫ τ dt = ∫ (dL/dt) dt = ΔL
J = L₂ − L₁
Constant torque: J = τ·Δt = IΔω
💡 NoteWhen the torque varies with time (τ = kt, τ = 2t, …) use the AREA under the τ–t graph, i.e. ∫τ dt. Using τ = Iα with an 'average' α is wrong.
★ RememberChoose the axis cleverly: taking moments about the contact point of a sphere kills the torques of the normal, of friction and of the weight in one stroke.

Angular Impulse = Change in Angular Momentum

  • Angular impulse is a concept of rotational dynamics stating that the angular impulse exerted on a body equals the change in its angular momentum.
  • Angular momentum measures the rotational motion of an object and is the product of its moment of inertia and its angular velocity, L = Iω.
  • Torque is the rotational equivalent of force and is responsible for changes in angular momentum.
  • Integrating τ = dL/dt over the time of action gives ∫dL = ∫τ dt, i.e. ΔL = ∫τ dt.
  • The right side is the angular impulse J, the left side is the change in angular momentum ΔL — hence J = ΔL.
τ = dL/dt
∫dL = ∫τ dt ⇒ ΔL = ∫τ dt = J
L = Iω ⇒ for a fixed axis, J = I(ω₂ − ω₁)
💡 NoteFor an impulsive blow of linear impulse A applied at perpendicular distance h from the axis, the angular impulse is simply J = hA (h stays constant during the brief blow).
⚠ TrapJ = ΔL holds about a chosen point; the SAME point must be used for the torque and for both angular momenta.

Conservation of Angular Momentum in Combined Motion

  • If the net torque acting on a system about a given point is zero, the total angular momentum of the system about that point stays constant.
  • In combined motion (translation + rotation together) the total angular momentum is the sum of the spin angular momentum about the centre of mass and the term due to the centre of mass moving past the reference point.
  • L_cm = I_cm ω is the spin term, r₀ is the position vector from the reference point O to the centre of mass and v₀ is the velocity of the centre of mass.
  • If the net torque about O is zero, the total angular momentum about O remains constant in time — this is conservation of angular momentum.
  • Standard use: a body slipping on a rough floor. Friction acts at the contact point, so its torque about that point (and about any point on the contact line) is zero — L about the contact point is conserved all the way until pure rolling sets in.
  • Same idea in collisions: during an instantaneous collision the impulsive forces are internal and the external impulses have negligible moment, so L about a suitably chosen point is conserved.
L = L_cm + M(r₀ × v₀) (total = spin about cm + cm term about O)
L = I_cm ω + M r v_cm (scalar form; r = perpendicular distance of the cm velocity line from O)
Net torque about O = 0 ⇒ L about O = constant
Solid sphere slipping → rolling: (2/5)Mr²ω₀ + Mrv₀ = (7/5)Mrv
Pure rolling condition: v = Rω
★ RememberFor a solid sphere slipping with v₀ and no spin, conservation about the contact point gives v = (5/7)v₀; with an initial forward spin ω₀ = v₀/2r it gives v = (6/7)v₀.
⚠ TrapDo not conserve angular momentum about the CENTRE of a slipping sphere — friction does have a torque about the centre. Use the contact point.

Toppling on a Horizontal Surface

  • A horizontal force F applied to a block topples it when the torque it produces overcomes the restoring torque provided by the weight of the block and the friction at the base.
  • A tall narrow block (A) topples more easily than a short wide block (B) under the same force, because the height b of the application point above the base is larger and the half-width is smaller.
  • Translational equilibrium alone (F balanced by friction f, and N = mg) does NOT guarantee rotational equilibrium: an unbalanced torque still tends to rotate the block about the far bottom edge E.
  • To restore rotational equilibrium the normal reaction shifts forward by a distance a from the centre so that the anticlockwise and clockwise torques balance: F·b = (mg)·a, hence a = Fb/(mg).
  • As F or b increases, a increases. But a cannot exceed the distance from the centre to the edge; once N is forced to act at the edge E, any further increase makes the block topple.
  • So a broader base = larger margin for N to shift = lower chance of toppling; a narrower base topples sooner.
  • For a round body the normal reaction has ZERO margin to shift (contact is a single point), so the body is in translational equilibrium but an unbalanced torque is always present — this is why even a very small force sets a round body moving (rolling is 'easy').
  • Note: for a body already rolling without slipping there is no toppling involved, though an unbalanced torque can still be present and affect the motion.
  • Working rule for a block of width w with the force applied at height b: toppling needs F·b ≥ mg·(w/2); sliding needs F ≥ μmg. Whichever threshold is crossed first decides what happens.
Rotational equilibrium with N shifted by a: F·b = (mg)·a ⇒ a = Fb/(mg)
Toppling threshold (width w, force at height b): F·b = mg·(w/2)
Cube of side a with the force at height 3a/4: F_min = 2mg/3
Equilateral triangular block of side a, force at the apex: F_topple = mg/√3, and μ_min = 1/√3 to topple before sliding
Topple before slide ⇔ F_topple ≤ μmg; slide before topple ⇔ F_topple > μmg
💡 NoteAlways take moments about the FAR bottom edge — N and f both pass through it, so only F and mg appear in the equation.
★ RememberCompare the two thresholds: the body topples before sliding when μ ≥ (half-width)/(height of application), e.g. μ ≥ (a/2)/(3a/4) = 2/3 for the cube of Example-7.

Torque cross-check: τ = FR = 20(0.1) = 2 N·m, α = τ/I = 20 rad/s², θ = s/R = 10 rad, ω = √(2αθ) = √(400) = 20 rad/s ✓
(a) ω = 20 rad/s
⚡ JEE trick: 'spokes of negligible mass' ⇒ I = MR² (ring), not ½MR². Treating it as a disc gives 28.3 rad/s, which is not even offered.
Strong Box · PYQ 31 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 24th January Morning Shift
A uniform solid cylinder of mass m and radius r rolls along an inclined rough plane of inclination 45°. If it starts to roll from rest from the top of the plane then the linear acceleration of the cylinder's axis will be: (a) √2 g (b) (1/√2) g (c) (1/(3√2)) g (d) √2 g/3
Rolling without slipping down an incline: mg sinθ − f = ma and f r = Iα with a = αr, which combine to a = g sinθ/(1 + I/mr²).
Solid cylinder about its own axis: I = ½ m r² ⇒ I/mr² = 1/2.
Hence a = g sinθ/(1 + 1/2) = (2/3) g sinθ.
With θ = 45°, sin45° = 1/√2 ⇒ a = (2/3)(g/√2) = 2g/(3√2).
Rationalising: 2g/(3√2) = 2√2 g/6 = √2 g/3 ⇒ option (d), about 0.471 g.
Consistency check: the friction required is f = ma/2 ≈ 0.236 mg, below μ mg cos45° for a reasonably rough plane, so pure rolling is indeed possible.
(d) a = √2 g/3 ≈ 0.471 g
⚡ JEE trick: memorise a = g sinθ/(1 + k²/R²) — ring 1, disc/cylinder 1/2, solid sphere 2/5, shell 2/3 — then it is one substitution.
Strong Box · PYQ 32 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 8th April Evening Shift
A rod of linear mass density 'λ' and length 'L' is bent to form a ring of radius 'R'. Moment of inertia of the ring about any of its diameters is: (a) λL³/(8π²) (b) λL³/(16π²) (c) λL³/(4π²) (d) λL³/12
Mass is unchanged by bending: M = λL.
The rod's length becomes the circumference: 2πR = L ⇒ R = L/(2π).
Ring about its central axis perpendicular to its plane: I_z = MR².
Perpendicular-axis theorem on the planar ring: I_x + I_y = I_z with I_x = I_y = I_diameter ⇒ I_diameter = ½ MR².
Substitute: I = ½ (λL)(L/2π)² = ½ λL · L²/(4π²) = λL³/(8π²) ⇒ option (a).
Trap: option (d) λL³/12 is ML²/12, the moment of inertia of the STRAIGHT rod about its centre — true before bending, not after.
(a) I = λL³/(8π²)
⚡ JEE trick: 'bent into a ring' always means 2πR = L; write R = L/2π first and the rest is substitution.
Strong Box · PYQ 33 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 28th January Evening Shift
A uniform rod of mass 250 g having length 100 cm is balanced on a sharp edge at the 40 cm mark. A mass of 400 g is suspended at the 10 cm mark. To maintain the balance of the rod, the mass to be suspended at the 90 cm mark is: (a) 290 g (b) 200 g (c) 190 g (d) 300 g
The rod is uniform, so its own weight (250 g-wt) acts at its centre, the 50 cm mark; the pivot is the sharp edge at the 40 cm mark.
Rod's own torque about the pivot: arm = 50 − 40 = 10 cm to the right ⇒ clockwise τ = 250 × 10 = 2500 g-wt·cm.
400 g at the 10 cm mark: arm = 40 − 10 = 30 cm to the left ⇒ anticlockwise τ = 400 × 30 = 12000 g-wt·cm.
Unknown mass m at the 90 cm mark: arm = 90 − 40 = 50 cm to the right ⇒ clockwise τ = 50 m g-wt·cm.
Balance about the edge: 12000 = 2500 + 50 m.
50 m = 12000 − 2500 = 9500 ⇒ m = 190 g ⇒ option (c).
(c) 190 g
⚡ JEE trick: g cancels, so work in g-wt·cm and never convert units. Forgetting the rod's own weight at the 50 cm mark gives 240 g — that omission is exactly what the distractors test.
Strong Box · PYQ 34 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 24th January Evening Shift
A solid sphere and a hollow sphere of the same mass and of same radius are rolled on an inclined plane. Let the time taken to reach the bottom by the solid sphere and the hollow sphere be t₁ and t₂, respectively, then: (a) t₁ < t₂ (b) t₁ = 2t₂ (c) t₁ > t₂ (d) t₁ = t₂
Rolling down an incline: a = g sinθ/(1 + k²/R²); mass and radius cancel completely, only the shape factor k²/R² survives.
Solid sphere: k²/R² = 2/5 ⇒ a₁ = g sinθ/(7/5) = (5/7) g sinθ ≈ 0.714 g sinθ.
Hollow sphere (thin shell): k²/R² = 2/3 ⇒ a₂ = g sinθ/(5/3) = (3/5) g sinθ = 0.600 g sinθ.
So a₁ > a₂: the solid sphere accelerates faster down the same slope.
From rest over the same slope length L: L = ½ a t² ⇒ t = √(2L/a), so larger a means smaller t ⇒ t₁ < t₂ ⇒ option (a).
Quantitatively t₁/t₂ = √(a₂/a₁) = √((3/5)/(5/7)) = √(21/25) = 0.917, which also rules out option (b) t₁ = 2t₂.
(a) t₁ < t₂
⚡ JEE trick: in a rolling race only k²/R² matters — the body with its mass nearer the axis always wins, whatever M, R and θ are.
Strong Box · PYQ 35 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 2nd April Evening Shift
The moment of inertia of a circular ring of mass M and diameter r about a tangential axis lying in the plane of the ring is: (a) (3/8)Mr² (b) 2Mr² (c) (1/2)Mr² (d) (3/2)Mr²
Read the data carefully: r is the DIAMETER, so the radius is R = r/2.
Ring about a diameter lying in its plane through the centre: I_d = ½ MR².
A tangent in the plane of the ring is parallel to that diameter and a distance R from it, so the parallel-axis theorem gives I = I_d + MR² = ½MR² + MR² = (3/2)MR².
Substitute R = r/2: I = (3/2) M (r/2)² = (3/2) M r²/4.
I = (3/8) M r² ⇒ option (a).
Option (d) (3/2)Mr² is the same formula left in terms of the RADIUS — the designed trap for anyone who misses 'diameter r'.

Strong Box · PYQ 36 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 24th January Evening Shift
A solid sphere is rolling without slipping on a horizontal plane. The ratio of the linear kinetic energy of the centre of mass of the sphere and the rotational kinetic energy is: (a) 3/4 (b) 4/3 (c) 5/2 (d) 2/5
Translational kinetic energy of the centre of mass: K_t = ½ M v².
Solid sphere about its centre: I = (2/5) M R², and rolling without slipping gives ω = v/R.
Rotational kinetic energy: K_r = ½ I ω² = ½ (2/5 M R²)(v/R)² = (1/5) M v².
Ratio K_t : K_r = (½ M v²)/((1/5) M v²) = (1/2)(5/1) = 5/2 ⇒ option (c).
Equivalently K_t/K_r = 1/(k²/R²) = 1/(2/5) = 5/2; the total is K = (7/10)Mv².
The reciprocal 2/5 is offered as option (d) — the question asks linear-to-rotational, in that order.
(c) 5/2
⚡ JEE trick: for any rolling body K_trans : K_rot = 1 : (k²/R²) — ring 1:1, disc 2:1, solid sphere 5:2, shell 3:2.
Strong Box · PYQ 37 · JEE Main 2025 Online, 22nd January Evening Shift

Torque about the origin: τ = r × F with r = î + ĵ + k̂ and F = 2î + ĵ + 2k̂.
Write the determinant with rows (î, ĵ, k̂), (1, 1, 1), (2, 1, 2).
î-component: (1)(2) − (1)(1) = 2 − 1 = 1.
ĵ-component: −[(1)(2) − (1)(2)] = −(2 − 2) = 0.

Strong Box · PYQ 38 · JEE Main 2024 Online, 1st February Evening Shift
A disc of radius R and mass M is rolling horizontally without slipping with speed v. It then moves up an inclined smooth surface as shown in figure. The maximum height that the disc can go up the incline is: (a) (3/4)(v²/g) (b) v²/g (c) (2/3)(v²/g) (d) (1/2)(v²/g)
On the horizontal floor the disc rolls, so its total kinetic energy is ½Mv² + ½Iω² = ½Mv² + ¼Mv² = (3/4)Mv², using I = ½MR² and ω = v/R.
The incline is SMOOTH: the normal reaction and gravity both act along lines through the centre of mass, and there is no friction, so the net torque about the centre of mass is zero.
Zero torque about the centre ⇒ α = 0 ⇒ ω stays locked at v/R all the way up, so the rotational energy ¼Mv² cannot be spent on climbing.
Only the translational kinetic energy is available: ½Mv² = Mgh.
h = v²/(2g) = (1/2)(v²/g) ⇒ option (d).
Trap check: option (a) (3/4)(v²/g) comes from converting the TOTAL rolling energy, which would need a ROUGH incline where friction can bleed off the spin.
(d) h = (1/2) v²/g
⚡ JEE trick: 'smooth incline' + rolling body ⇒ the spin is frozen, so equate only ½Mv² to Mgh. The single word 'smooth' is the whole question.

At the highest point the vertical component of velocity is zero, so the velocity is purely horizontal: v = u cos30° = (√3/2) u.
Maximum height: h = u² sin²30°/(2g) = u²(1/2)²/(2g) = u²/(8g).
Angular momentum about the launch point: L = m v d, where d is the perpendicular distance from that point to the horizontal line of velocity, i.e. d = h.

General result worth remembering: L at maximum height = m u³ sin²θ cosθ/(2g).
(d) L = (√3/16)(m u³/g)
⚡ JEE trick: at the top of a projectile's path v is horizontal, so the lever arm is simply h — no vector algebra is needed at all.

Strong Box · PYQ 41 · JEE Main 2024 Online, 8th April Evening Shift
A thin circular disc of mass M and radius R is rotating in a horizontal plane about an axis passing through its centre and perpendicular to its plane with angular velocity ω. If another disc of same dimensions but of mass M/2 is placed gently on the first disc co-axially, then the new angular velocity of the system is: (a) (4/5)ω (b) (5/4)ω (c) (3/2)ω (d) (2/3)ω
The second disc is placed GENTLY and co-axially, so no external torque acts about the common vertical axis ⇒ angular momentum is conserved: I₁ω = (I₁ + I₂)ω′.
First disc: I₁ = ½ M R².
Second disc, same dimensions but mass M/2: I₂ = ½ (M/2) R² = ¼ M R².
Combined: I₁ + I₂ = ½MR² + ¼MR² = (3/4) M R².
ω′ = I₁ω/(I₁ + I₂) = (½MR²)ω ÷ ((3/4)MR²) = (2/3)ω ⇒ option (d).
Energy check: K_i = ½(½MR²)ω² = (1/4)MR²ω², K_f = ½((3/4)MR²)(2ω/3)² = (1/6)MR²ω² < K_i — the deficit goes to friction while the discs reach a common ω, so energy must NOT be conserved here.
(d) ω′ = (2/3)ω
⚡ JEE trick: 'placed gently' ⇒ conserve angular momentum, never kinetic energy. For identical geometry I₂/I₁ = m₂/m₁, so ω′ = ω/(1 + m₂/m₁) in one step.
