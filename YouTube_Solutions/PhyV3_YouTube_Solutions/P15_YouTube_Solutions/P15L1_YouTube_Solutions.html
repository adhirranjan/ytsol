1
For a given volume, the minimum surface is of (a) Cube (b) Sphere (c) Cylinder (d) Cone (Level-1)
P15L1VSuggQ1Minimum surface for a given volume
🟡 SimilarWhy are liquid drops…
bSphere
Concept Surface tension makes a free liquid body shrink its exposed area to the smallest possible value for its fixed volume, because surface energy = T × area is minimised. Among all shapes enclosing a given volume, the sphere has the least surface area — which is exactly why free drops and bubbles are spherical.
Formula Surface energy E = T × A; equilibrium shape minimises A at fixed V. For fixed volume, Asphere < Acylinder < Acube < Acone.
Solution
1. A liquid left to itself minimises surface energy T·A, hence minimises surface area A.
2. Mathematically, of all closed shapes of a given volume the sphere has the smallest surface area (isoperimetric property).
3. A cube, cylinder or cone of the same volume all expose more area than the sphere.
4. So the minimum-surface shape is the sphere — option (b).
5. This is why small raindrops and soap bubbles are spherical.
⚡ JEE Trick Any “minimum surface / free drop shape” question is answered by the sphere — least area per unit volume, least surface energy.
Answer: (b) Sphere.
2
A straight piece of wire 5 cm long is placed horizontally on the surface of water and gently pulled up. It is found that a force of 7×10−3 N in addition to the weight of wire is required for that purpose. What is the surface tension of water? (Level-2)
P15L1VSuggQ2Surface tension from wire pull (F = 2TL)
🟡 SimilarThe maximum force, i…
7×10−2 N/m (= 0.07 N/m)
Concept A straight wire lying on the water surface is gripped along its length by the liquid surface on both sides (front and back edge), so the extra pull needed to lift it is the surface-tension force over twice its length. Dividing that force by 2L gives the surface tension.
Formula Extra force F = 2TL ⇒ T = F/(2L). The factor 2 is the two liquid surfaces pulling along the two sides of the wire.
Solution
1. Length L = 5 cm = 0.05 m; extra force F = 7×10−3 N.
2. The water surface contacts the wire along both sides, so F = 2TL.
3. Rearrange: T = F/(2L) = 7×10−3 / (2 × 0.05).
4. Denominator 2 × 0.05 = 0.10 m.
5. T = 7×10−3 / 0.10 = 7×10−2 N/m.
⚡ JEE Trick For a straight wire on a liquid surface use F = 2TL (two sides). Forgetting the 2 doubles the answer.
Answer: T = 7×10−2 N/m (= 0.07 N/m).
3
A wire ring of 0.03 m radius is rested on the surface of a liquid and then raised. The pull required is 0.003 kg wt more before the film breaks than it is after. Find the surface tension of liquid. (Level-3)
P15L1VSuggQ3Surface tension from ring pull
🟢 ExactA thin wire ring of…
0.079 N/m (≈ 0.078 N/m)
Concept When a ring is raised from a liquid, a cylindrical film clings to it with two circular edges — the inner and outer circumference, each of length 2πr for a thin ring. The extra pull before the film snaps equals the surface-tension force over both edges, i.e. over 4πr.
Formula Extra pull F = T(2πr + 2πr) = 4πrT ⇒ T = F/(4πr), with F = mg.
Solution
Given wire ring of radius 0.03 m raised from the liquid; extra pull of 0.003 kg wt needed before the film breaks
Find the surface tension of the liquid
1. Convert the pull: F = mg = 0.003 × 9.81 = 0.0294 N.
2. Thin ring ⇒ inner and outer radii both ≈ r = 0.03 m, total contact length = 4πr.
3. T = F/(4πr) = 0.0294 / (4π × 0.03).
4. Denominator 4π × 0.03 = 0.3770 m.
5. T = 0.0294 / 0.3770 = 0.078 N/m ≈ 0.079 N/m (key).
⚡ JEE Trick A ring (not a disc) has TWO edges ⇒ use 4πr, not 2πr. The “before minus after” pull is exactly the film force.
Answer: T ≈ 0.079 N/m.

A soap film is formed on a rectangular frame of length 0.03 m dipping in soap solution. The frame hangs from the arm of a balance. An extra mass of 2.20×10−4 kg must be placed in the other pan to balance the pull of the film. Calculate the surface tension of the soap solution. (Level-4)
P15L1VSuggQ4Soap film on a frame (balance)
🟡 SimilarA soap film is forme…
0.036 N/m
Concept A soap film has two surfaces (front and back), so it pulls down on the frame’s wetted edge with a force 2TL. The extra mass added to the other pan balances this pull, so mg = 2TL and the surface tension follows.
Formula Film pull F = 2TL = mg ⇒ T = mg/(2L). Two surfaces of the soap film give the factor 2.
Solution
Given soap film on a rectangular frame of length 0.03 m; extra mass 2.20 × 10⁻⁴ kg balances the pull
Find the surface tension of the soap solution
1. L = 0.03 m, m = 2.20×10−4 kg, g = 9.8 m/s2.
2. Film force balancing the pan: mg = 2TL.
3. T = mg/(2L) = (2.20×10−4 × 9.8)/(2 × 0.03).
4. Numerator = 2.156×10−3 N; denominator = 0.06 m.
5. T = 2.156×10−3 / 0.06 = 0.036 N/m.
⚡ JEE Trick Soap film = 2 surfaces ⇒ F = 2TL. A plain liquid lamina on a single wire would be TL; a film has both faces.
Answer: T = 0.036 N/m.
5
A container of width 3a is filled with a liquid. A thin wire of mass per unit length λ is gently placed over the liquid surface in the middle of the surface. As a result, the liquid surface is depressed by a distance y (y << a). Determine the surface tension of the liquid. (Level-5)
P15L1VSuggQ5Wire depressing a liquid surface
🟡 SimilarDerivation of surfac…
T = 3λga/4y ⚠
Concept The wire sits at the centre, so the depressed surface on each side runs from the wire up to the wall a horizontal distance half the width = 3a/2 away. The weight of the wire per unit length is held up by the vertical components of the surface-tension pull T on the two sloping surfaces. Small depression y makes the slope angle tiny, so sinθ ≈ tanθ = y/(3a/2).
Formula Balance per unit length: 2T sinθ = λg, with sinθ ≈ y/(3a/2) = 2y/(3a) ⇒ T = λg/(2 sinθ) = 3λga/(4y). Half-width = 3a/2 because the full width is 3a and the wire is central.
Solution
Given container of width 3a filled with liquid; thin wire of mass per unit length λ placed at the middle; surface depressed by y (y ≪ a)
Find the surface tension of the liquid
1. Weight per unit length of wire = λg, pulling down.
2. Surface tension T acts along each of the two surfaces; vertical component per side = T sinθ.
3. Equilibrium: 2T sinθ = λg.
4. Geometry (y << a): sinθ ≈ tanθ = y / (3a/2) = 2y/(3a).
5. So T = λg/(2 × 2y/3a) = 3λga/(4y).
6. ⚠ The key quotes λga/(2y) — that is the result for Example-2 where the width is 2a (half-width a). It was not re-scaled for width 3a.
⚡ JEE Trick Read the container width carefully: half-width (not full width) sets the slope. Width 3a ⇒ use 3a/2, giving 3λga/4y — the book’s key copied the 2a answer.
Answer: T = 3λga/(4y). (Key prints λga/2y — the un-scaled width-2a value.)

1
Surface tension arises due to: (a) Friction (b) Gravity (c) Cohesive forces between surface molecules (d) Pressure
P15L1V1Q1Origin of surface tension
🟡 SimilarSurface Tension for…
cCohesive forces between surface molecules
Concept Surface tension is a consequence of cohesive forces — the mutual attraction between molecules of the same liquid. A surface molecule has liquid neighbours only below and to the sides, so it feels a net inward pull; the surface behaves like a stretched membrane. Friction, gravity and pressure play no role in creating it.
Formula Origin: unbalanced cohesive (intermolecular) attraction on surface molecules.
Solution
1. Inside the liquid a molecule is pulled equally in all directions — net force zero.
2. At the surface there are no molecules above, so the cohesive pull is net inward.
3. This inward pull makes the surface act like a stretched skin — surface tension.
4. Friction (a), gravity (b) and pressure (d) do not produce this effect.
5. Hence the cause is cohesive forces between surface molecules — (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Surface tension = cohesion; viscosity/drag = friction. Keep the two origins separate.
Answer: (c) Cohesive forces between surface molecules.
2
Surface tension is: (a) an atomic phenomenon (b) a molecular phenomenon (c) a gravitational effect (d) a proton effect
P15L1V1Q2Nature of surface tension
🟡 SimilarDerivation of surfac…
ba molecular phenomenon
Concept Surface tension originates from forces between whole molecules (intermolecular cohesion), not from forces inside an atom or between subatomic particles, and not from gravity. It is therefore a molecular phenomenon.
Formula Cause = intermolecular (molecule–molecule) attraction.
Solution
1. The relevant forces act between neighbouring molecules of the liquid.
2. They are not intra-atomic (a) nor proton-level (d) effects.
3. Gravity (c) does not create the inward surface pull.
4. Since molecule–molecule cohesion is responsible, it is a molecular phenomenon.
5. Hence option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Watch the wording: “molecular” (between molecules) ≠ “atomic” (within an atom).
Answer: (b) a molecular phenomenon.
3
Surface tension mainly arises due to: (a) gravitational forces (b) electrostatic forces (c) cohesive molecular forces (d) adhesive molecular forces
P15L1V1Q3Origin of surface tension
🟡 SimilarSurface Tension for…
ccohesive molecular forces
Concept Surface tension comes from cohesion — attraction between like molecules of the same liquid. Adhesion (attraction to a different substance) governs wetting and capillarity, not the surface tension itself; gravity and bulk electrostatics are not the main cause.
Formula Main cause = cohesive (like–like) molecular forces.
Solution
1. Surface tension is a property of the liquid’s own molecules sticking together — cohesion.
2. Adhesion (d) is attraction between unlike substances (e.g. water–glass) — that drives capillary rise, not T itself.
3. Gravity (a) and generic electrostatics (b) are not the principal cause.
4. So the main origin is cohesive molecular forces.
5. Option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Cohesion makes the surface; adhesion decides whether the liquid climbs or beads on a solid.
Answer: (c) cohesive molecular forces.

When there is no external force, the shape of a small liquid drop is determined by: (a) density of liquid (b) viscosity of air (c) surface tension (d) elasticity of liquid
P15L1V1Q4Shape of a liquid drop
🟡 SimilarSmall droplets of a…
csurface tension
Concept With no external force (no gravity to flatten it), a small drop takes the shape that minimises its surface energy T·A — the sphere, which has the least area per unit volume. So the governing property is surface tension; density, air viscosity and “elasticity” do not set the free shape.
Formula Free shape set by minimising surface energy = T × A ⇒ sphere.
Solution
1. Without external forces the drop is free to minimise its surface energy.
2. Surface energy = T × area, so it minimises area ⇒ becomes a sphere.
3. Density (a) and air viscosity (b) do not fix the equilibrium shape.
4. Liquids have no shear elasticity (d) to set a shape.
5. Hence surface tension determines the drop’s shape — (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Small drops are more spherical because for them surface tension dominates gravity (which would flatten a large drop).
Answer: (c) surface tension.
5
The dimension ML−1T−2 can correspond to: (a) moment of a force (b) surface tension (c) modulus of elasticity (d) None of these
P15L1V1Q5Dimensions
🟢 ExactThe dimension ML^-1…
cmodulus of elasticity
Concept ML−1T−2 is the dimension of pressure / stress. Modulus of elasticity = stress/strain, and strain is dimensionless, so a modulus carries the dimension of stress — exactly ML−1T−2. Moment of force is ML2T−2 and surface tension is MT−2, so neither fits.
Formula Stress = F/A = MLT−2/L2 = ML−1T−2; modulus = stress/strain (strain dimensionless).
Solution
1. Moment of force = force × distance = MLT−2 × L = ML2T−2 — not it.
2. Surface tension = force/length = MLT−2/L = MT−2 — not it.
3. Modulus of elasticity = stress/strain; strain is dimensionless.
4. Stress = force/area = MLT−2/L2 = ML−1T−2.
5. So ML−1T−2 is the modulus of elasticity — (c).
⚡ JEE Trick ML−1T−2 = pressure = stress = any elastic modulus. Surface tension is one power of L higher: MT−2.
Answer: (c) modulus of elasticity.

What is the surface tension of water if an extra 3.97 gm weight is required to pull it away from water? (g = 980 cm/sec2) (a) 72.13 dyne/cm (b) 72.13 N/m (c) 7.213 dyne/m (d) None of these

🟡 SimilarThe force of surface…
a72.13 dyne/cm (≈ 72.0)
Concept A ring cut from a tube wets water along two circles — the inner circumference and the outer circumference. The extra pull equals T times the sum of both perimeters, π(dinner + douter). Work in CGS.
