Concept A free-body diagram (FBD) isolates one body and draws only the external forces acting on that body — never the forces it exerts on others, and never internal forces. Every force must have an identifiable physical agent: gravity (the Earth), a normal force (each surface actually touching the body), tension (each attached string), or an applied push/pull. Counting forces therefore reduces to a mechanical checklist: one weight, plus one normal per contacting surface, plus any applied force.
Formula Force count = 1 (weight W = mg, in N) + (number of touching surfaces → one normal N each, in N) + (applied/tension forces, in N). Weight always acts vertically down through the centre of mass; a normal always acts perpendicular to and away from the contact surface. "Smooth" in the figure means frictionless, so no surface-parallel force is added.
Solution
1. (a) Block m on a smooth horizontal surface. Agents: Earth → weight; ground → one normal. Contact surfaces = 1.Smooth ⇒ no friction force, so nothing acts parallel to the ground.
  • Forces = W = mg (down) + N (up) = 2 forces
2. (b) Block m (A) resting on block m₂ (B), B on the ground. Treat each body separately.
  • On A: weight m·g (down) + normal N₁ from B (up) = 2 forces
  • On B: weight m₂·g (down) + N₁′ from A pressing down + N₂ from ground (up) = 3 forcesN₁′ is the Newton's-third-law partner of N₁ — equal in size, opposite in direction, but it acts on B, so it belongs in B's FBD.
3. (c) Block m on a smooth incline of angle θ. Contact surfaces = 1 (the incline face).
  • Forces = W = mg (vertically down) + N (⊥ to the incline face) = 2 forcesSmooth ⇒ no friction up the slope; the block is therefore not in equilibrium and slides down with a = g sin θ.
4. (d) Sphere A resting in a smooth V-groove (60°–60°–60°). The sphere touches both groove walls, so two contact surfaces.
  • Forces = W = mg (down) + N₁ (⊥ left wall) + N₂ (⊥ right wall) = 3 forcesEach normal points from the contact point toward the sphere's centre, because a normal to a sphere's surface is along its radius.
5. (e) Block M (A) pushed by horizontal force F against a vertical wall. Contact surfaces = 1 (the wall face).
  • Forces = F (applied, horizontal) + W = Mg (down) + N from wall (horizontal, opposing F) = 3 forcesThe wall's normal is horizontal because a normal is ⊥ to the contact surface, and the wall is vertical. The figure marks no friction, so we take the wall as smooth — the block is then not in vertical equilibrium and slides down.

Concept For a stack of blocks at rest, the normal force from the ground on the bottom block must support everything resting above it. Formula Equilibrium of the system: N_ground = (m_A + m_B + m_C) · g, with masses in kg, g = 10 m s⁻², N in newton (N). kg × m s⁻² = N, so the units are consistent. Assumption: the stack is in equilibrium (a = 0), as nothing is pushing it.

Concept The thrust (force on the pulley's hinge/support) comes from the string pressing on the pulley. A string wrapped over a pulley pulls downward on both sides, so the support must hold up 2T, not the blocks' weights directly. The catch is that T equals mg only when the system is not accelerating. With m₁ = m₂ the Atwood machine is balanced, a = 0, and each tension is exactly mg — so the thrust works out to the full (m₁+m₂)g.
Formula Atwood acceleration a = (m₂ − m₁)g / (m₁ + m₂) (m s⁻²); tension T = 2m₁m₂g / (m₁ + m₂) (N); thrust on a frictionless, massless pulley F = 2T (N). Both string segments hang vertically and pull down on the pulley, so their pulls add: F = T + T = 2T. Assumption: pulley frictionless and massless, string massless and inextensible (as stated).
Solution
1. Put m₁ = m₂ = m (kg), the condition given.
2. Find the acceleration: a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂) = (m − m)(10 m s⁻²)/(m + m) = (0)(10)/(2m) = 0 m s⁻².Equal masses ⇒ equal and opposite pulls ⇒ the system is balanced and stays at rest (or moves at constant speed).
3. With a = 0, apply ΣF = ma to one block, say m₁ (forces: T up, m₁g down): T − m₁g = m₁a = (m)(0 m s⁻²) = 0 N.
4. Solve: T = m₁g = mg (N). The same equation for m₂ gives T = m₂g = mg — consistent.This is the key step: only because a = 0 does the tension equal the full weight. If m₁ ≠ m₂ the tension would be less than the heavier weight.
5. Now take the pulley as the body. The string presses down on it on both sides with T each, and the hinge holds it up with F:
  • F = T + T = 2T = 2(mg) = 2mg (N)
6. Express in the form of the options: since m₁ = m₂ = m, we have m₁ + m₂ = 2m, so 2mg = (m₁ + m₂)g.
7. Match: thrust = (m₁ + m₂)g exactly — equal to (m₁+m₂)g, which is option (c).
8. Dimensional check: (kg)(m s⁻²) = N ✓ — a force, as a thrust must be.

1What is the SI unit of force? (a) Kilogram (b) Newton (c) Pascal (d) Joule (Ans: b)SI units — forceWhat is the SI Unit of Force || SI Unit of Force || Unit of Force || physics · What Is the SI Unit of Force?🟢 ExactbNewton
Concept The SI unit of force is the newton (N). It is a derived unit, fixed entirely by Newton's second law F = ma: force is mass times acceleration, so its unit must be (unit of mass)×(unit of acceleration). The three distractors are units of completely different quantities — mass, pressure and energy — placed here because they crowd the same corner of mechanics.
Formula F = m·a ⇒ [F] = [m][a] = kg·m·s⁻² ≡ 1 N. One newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s². This is the definition itself, not a table lookup.
Solution
1. Start from Newton's second law: F = m·a.
2. Insert SI base units for mass (kg) and acceleration (m/s²): [F] = kg × m/s² = kg·m·s⁻².This composite unit is given the special name newton (N), after Isaac Newton.
3. Dimension-check every option instead of guessing:
  • (a) kilogram → unit of mass, not force.
  • (b) newton → kg·m·s⁻² → force ✓
  • (c) pascal → N/m² = kg·m⁻¹·s⁻² → pressure.
  • (d) joule → N·m = kg·m²·s⁻² → energy / work.
4. Only the newton carries the dimensions of force, so option (b) is the unique match.

2What does a free-body diagram (FBD) show? (a) All internal forces inside an object (b) Only the weight of the object (c) All forces acting on an isolated object (d) The motion of the object (Ans: c)Free body diagram — definitionWhat is Free Body Diagram? | Steps to write FBD | #engineeringmechanics | #class11physics🟢 ExactcAll forces acting on an isolated object
Concept A free-body diagram (FBD) mentally cuts one body out of its surroundings and draws every external force acting on that body — and nothing else. It deliberately excludes internal forces (they cancel in pairs inside the body), forces the body exerts on other bodies, and the body's motion or velocity. Its sole purpose is to give you a clean left-hand side for ΣF = ma.
Formula ΣF = m·a — the FBD is the bookkeeping tool that lists all forces in the sum ΣF. Every force drawn must have a real external agent: gravity, a normal from a touching surface, tension from a string, or an applied push/pull. No agent ⇒ not a force ⇒ not on the FBD.
Solution
1. State the definition precisely: an FBD shows all external forces on a single, isolated body.
2. Test option (a) "all internal forces": internal forces occur in action–reaction pairs inside the body and cancel; an FBD never draws them. ✗
3. Test option (b) "only the weight": weight is just one of possibly many forces (normals, tension, applied). Showing only weight is incomplete. ✗
4. Test option (d) "the motion of the object": motion (velocity, path) belongs to a kinematics/trajectory sketch, not a force diagram. ✗The FBD is about causes (forces); motion is the effect, found afterwards from ΣF = ma.
5. Test option (c) "all forces acting on an isolated object": this is exactly the definition. ✓

  ----------------                    ---------------
  external forces on the chosen body   YES  <-- option (c)
  internal (action-reaction) forces    no
  forces the body exerts on others     no
  velocity / path / motion             no
⚡ JEE Trick Build an FBD as a checklist — one weight, one normal per touching surface, one tension per string, plus any applied force — and draw them on the isolated body only. Trap: option (b) tempts you when a block "just sits there," but the table's normal force is still acting; and option (a) baits students who confuse internal molecular forces with the external forces an FBD needs.
Answer: (c) all forces acting on an isolated object.

4In the universal law of gravitation, what is the role of G? (a) Mass of Earth (b) Acceleration due to gravity (c) Gravitational constant (d) Radius of Earth (Ans: c)Gravitational constant G — roleRelationship Between Universal Gravitational Constant (G) and Acceleration due to Gravity (g)🟡 SimilarcGravitational constant
Concept Newton's universal law of gravitation says every pair of masses attracts with a force proportional to the product of the masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation. Writing that proportionality as an equation requires a constant of proportionality — that constant is G, the universal gravitational constant. It is "universal" because it has the same value everywhere in the cosmos, for any pair of masses; it is not a property of the Earth or of any particular body.
Formula F = G·m₁·m₂ / r², with F in newton (N), m₁, m₂ in kg, r in metre (m), and G = 6.674×10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². Rearranging, G = F·r²/(m₁m₂), so its unit must be (N)(m²)/(kg·kg) = N m² kg⁻² — that unit is the fingerprint that identifies G among the options.
Solution
1. Start from the proportionality Newton deduced: F ∝ m₁m₂ and F ∝ 1/r², so F ∝ m₁m₂/r².
2. Convert a proportionality into an equation by inserting a constant: F = G·m₁m₂/r². That constant G is the gravitational constant — option (c).Its job is purely to fix the scale (and the units) of the gravitational force; it carries no information about which bodies are involved.
3. Confirm by a dimensional check. Solve for G: G = F·r² / (m₁·m₂) → unit = (N × m²) ÷ (kg × kg) = N m² kg⁻².This is neither a mass (kg), an acceleration (m s⁻²), nor a length (m) — so it cannot be any of the other three options.
4. Eliminate the distractors on units alone:
  • (a) Mass of Earth M = 5.97×10²⁴ kg — a mass, and specific to Earth ✗
  • (b) Acceleration due to gravity g = 9.8 m s⁻² — an acceleration, and it varies with location ✗
  • (d) Radius of Earth R = 6.37×10⁶ m — a length, again Earth-specific ✗
5. Only (c) has the unit N m² kg⁻² and is truly universal.
