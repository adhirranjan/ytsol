1A perfectly elastic body will: (a) Partially return to original shape (b) Never deform (c) Regain original shape completely and instantly (d) Absorb all deforming force (Level-1)Perfectly elastic bodyLecture 2 - Elasticity (Perfectly Elastic, Plastic, Perfectly Rigid Body, Elastic Limit, Stress) 🟡 SimilarcRegain original shape completely and instantly
Concept A perfectly elastic body is an idealisation: once the deforming force is removed it recovers its original size and shape completely (no residual/permanent set) and instantaneously (no time lag or hysteresis). Real materials always fall short — a little energy is lost as heat and some deformation lingers — so the perfectly elastic body is the limiting benchmark against which real elasticity is measured. The key words to test every option against are “completely” and “instantly”.
Formula On removing the stress, residual strain εres = 0 for a perfectly elastic body, and the recovery time τ → 0. These two conditions — zero permanent strain and zero recovery lag — are exactly what “completely and instantly” encodes.
Solution
1. Option (a) “Partially return” describes a partially elastic (real) body, which keeps some permanent deformation — this is the opposite of perfect elasticity.
2. Option (b) “Never deform” describes a perfectly rigid body, not an elastic one; an elastic body must deform under load, then recover.Rigidity and elasticity are different idealisations — don't confuse “recovers fully” with “does not deform at all”.
3. Option (c) “Regain original shape completely and instantly” matches both defining conditions εres = 0 and τ → 0 — this is the definition.
4. Option (d) “Absorb all deforming force” describes a perfectly plastic/inelastic body, which retains the energy as permanent deformation and does not spring back.
5. Only (c) survives both tests, so (c) is correct.
6. Cross-check: a perfectly elastic body has zero hysteresis loop area on its stress–strain cycle — loading and unloading retrace the same line, confirming complete, instant recovery.
⚡ JEE Trick Map each option to a named idealised body: rigid (no deformation), plastic (keeps deformation), real (partial recovery), perfectly elastic (full, instant recovery). The phrase “completely and instantly” is the fingerprint of perfect elasticity — any option missing either word is a trap.
Answer: (c) Regain original shape completely and instantly.
2Two rods A and B each of equal length but different materials are suspended from a common support as shown. The rods A and B can support a maximum load of W1 = 600 N and W2 = 6000 N respectively, with cross-sectional areas A1 = 10 mm2 and A2 = 1000 mm2. Identify the stronger material. (Level-2)Breaking stress comparisonUnderstanding Material Strength, Ductility and Toughness 🟡 Similarσ1 = 60×10^6 N/m^2, σ2 = 6×10^6 N/m^2 → rod (1) is the stronger material
Concept “Stronger material” is decided by breaking stress (the maximum force per unit area a material withstands), not by the raw breaking load. Rod B holds a far bigger load (6000 N vs 600 N), but it is also 100 times thicker; what matters is how much each square metre of material can bear. So we must divide each maximum load by that rod's own cross-sectional area before comparing.
Formula σbreak = Wmax / A. Breaking stress is an intrinsic property of the material; breaking load is not, because it also depends on the specimen's thickness.
Solution
1. Convert areas to SI: A1 = 10 mm2 = 10 × 10−6 = 1×10−5 m2; A2 = 1000 mm2 = 1×10−3 m2.1 mm2 = 10−6 m2 — forgetting this factor is the usual slip.
2. Rod 1 breaking stress: σ1 = W1/A1 = 600 / (1×10−5) = 6×107 N/m2 = 60×106 N/m2.
3. Rod 2 breaking stress: σ2 = W2/A2 = 6000 / (1×10−3) = 6×106 N/m2.
4. Compare: σ1/σ2 = 60×106 / 6×106 = 10. Material of rod 1 bears ten times the stress per unit area.
5. Therefore the material of rod 1 is the stronger, even though rod 2 supports the larger absolute load.
6. Sanity: rod 2 carries 10× the load but has 100× the area, so its stress is 10/100 = 1/10 of rod 1's — consistent with σ2 being the smaller.
⚡ JEE Trick Never rank strength by breaking load alone — always reduce to stress = load/area. The fat rod wins on load but loses on stress. A 10× load with a 100× area is a 10× weaker material.
Answer: σ1 = 60×106 N/m2, σ2 = 6×106 N/m2 — the material of rod 1 is stronger.
3A string passes over a frictionless pulley and carries masses 1 kg and 2 kg at its two ends (as shown). The breaking stress of the metal of the string is 2 × 109 N/m2. What is the minimum radius of the string if it is not to break? (g = 10 ms−2) (Level-3)Breaking stress, Atwood tensionMinimum Radius of Wire to Prevent Breaking | Breaking Stress & Strength | Class 11 Physics 🟡 Similar0.46×10^-4 m (≈ 4.6×10^-5 m)
Concept The string runs over a pulley carrying unequal masses (an Atwood machine), so it is not simply holding a hanging weight — the system accelerates and the string tension is less than either weight. We must first find that tension, then insist the stress it produces stays at or below the breaking stress. The minimum radius is the one that makes the stress exactly equal to the breaking stress.
Formula Atwood tension T = 2m1m2g / (m1+m2); breaking condition σbreak = T / (πr2) ⇒ r = √(T / πσbreak). Thinner wire → smaller area → higher stress, so the smallest safe radius is set by σ = σbreak.
Solution
1. Tension in the string of an ideal Atwood machine with m1 = 1 kg, m2 = 2 kg: T = 2m1m2g/(m1+m2).The same string passes over the pulley, so its tension is single-valued; it is not 2g or 1g but the Atwood value.
2. Substitute: T = (2 × 1 × 2 × 10)/(1+2) = 40/3 = 13.33 N.
3. Set stress to the breaking value: σbreak = T/(πr2) ⇒ r2 = T/(πσbreak).
4. Insert numbers: r2 = 13.33 / (π × 2×109) = 13.33 / (6.283×109) = 2.122×10−9 m2.
5. Take the square root: r = √(2.122×10−9) = 4.61×10−5 m = 0.46×10−4 m.
6. Any smaller radius makes σ > σbreak and the string snaps, so this is the minimum allowed radius.
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is using mg (10 N or 20 N) for the tension. Over a pulley with unequal masses the tension is the Atwood value 2m1m2g/(m1+m2), which lies between the two weights. Compute T first, then r = √(T/πσ).
Answer: minimum radius r = 0.46×10−4 m (≈ 4.6×10−5 m).
4Find the longitudinal (normal) stress and the tangential (shearing) stress on the top face of a fixed block when a force of 100 N is applied at 30° to that face. The face measures 5 m × 2 m. (Level-4)Normal & tangential stress on a faceNormal Stress and Tangential (Shear) Stress, Resultant Stress, Angle of Obliquity | Solved Problems 🟡 SimilarNormal (longitudinal) 5 N/m^2 & tangential (shearing) 5√3 N/m^2 (≈ 8.66 N/m^2)
Concept A single force applied at an angle to a surface splits into two parts: a component perpendicular to the face, which produces normal (longitudinal) stress, and a component along the face, which produces tangential (shearing) stress. Both act over the same area — the area of the face the force is applied to — so once that area is fixed, the two stresses differ only through the two force components.
Formula σnormal = F sinθ / A, σtangential = F cosθ / A, where θ is the angle the force makes with the face. Measured from the face, the perpendicular component carries sinθ and the along-face component carries cosθ.
Solution
1. Area of the loaded face: A = 5 m × 2 m = 10 m2.The force acts on this top face, so this is the common area for both stresses.
2. Resolve F = 100 N at 30° to the face: perpendicular part F sin30° = 100 × 0.5 = 50 N; along-face part F cos30° = 100 × (√3/2) = 50√3 ≈ 86.6 N.
3. Normal (longitudinal) stress: σn = F sin30° / A = 50 / 10 = 5 N/m2.
4. Tangential (shearing) stress: σt = F cos30° / A = 50√3 / 10 = 5√3 ≈ 8.66 N/m2.
5. Check the resultant: √(52 + (5√3)2) = √(25 + 75) = √100 = 10 N/m2 = F/A = 100/10 ✓ — the two components recombine to the total stress F/A.
6. So the normal stress is 5 N/m2 and the tangential stress is 5√3 N/m2.
⚡ JEE Trick Watch whether the angle is measured from the face or from the normal — it swaps sin and cos. Here 30° is to the face, so the perpendicular (normal) part uses sin30°. A fast check: the two stresses must satisfy σn2 + σt2 = (F/A)2.
Answer: normal stress 5 N/m2; tangential stress 5√3 N/m2 (≈ 8.66 N/m2).
5The breaking stress for a metal is 7.8 × 109 N/m2. Calculate the maximum length of a wire made of this material which may be suspended (vertically) without breaking under its own weight. Density of the metal = 7.8 × 103 kg/m3, g = 10 N/kg. (Level-5)Self-weight breaking lengthThe breaking stress for a metal is 7.8 x 10^9 Nm-2. Calculate the maximum length of the wire made of this material 🟢 Exact10^5 m (= 100 km)
Concept A wire hanging under its own weight is most heavily stressed at the top, where it must support the entire weight below it. As the wire gets longer, that top stress grows until it reaches the breaking stress — that length is the maximum the wire can hang without snapping. Crucially the cross-sectional area cancels out: weight and supporting area both scale with it, so the answer depends only on breaking stress, density and g.
Formula Top stress of a hanging wire = (mass × g)/A = (ρ A L g)/A = ρ g L. Setting this to σbreak gives Lmax = σbreak / (ρ g). The area A divides out, so a thick wire is no stronger per unit length than a thin one against self-weight.
Solution
1. Weight of the whole wire = ρ × (A L) × g, acting on the top cross-section of area A.
2. Stress at the top: σ = ρ A L g / A = ρ g L — independent of A.This is why breaking length, not breaking load, is the fair measure of a material against its own weight.
3. Breaking condition: ρ g Lmax = σbreak ⇒ Lmax = σbreak/(ρ g).
4. Substitute σbreak = 7.8×109 N/m2, ρ = 7.8×103 kg/m3, g = 10 N/kg: Lmax = 7.8×109 / (7.8×103 × 10).
5. Simplify the denominator: 7.8×103 × 10 = 7.8×104. Then Lmax = 7.8×109 / 7.8×104 = 105 m.
6. So the greatest length is 105 m = 100 km — the numeric 7.8 factors cancel cleanly.
⚡ JEE Trick The cross-sectional area always cancels in a self-weight breaking-length problem — so never go looking for the radius; it isn't needed. Just compute σ/(ρg). The matching 7.8 values here are a hint the arithmetic collapses to a clean power of ten.
Answer: maximum length Lmax = 105 m (= 100 km).

The cross-section cancels (weight and area both scale with it), so the answer needs only breaking stress, density and g — all given here in CGS units, which we keep consistent throughout.

5. Divide: Lmax = 7.5×108 / 2646 = 2.834×105 cm.

⚡ JEE Trick When data is handed to you in CGS (dyne, g, cm), stay in CGS — converting mid-way is where errors creep in. The length pops out in cm; only convert to km at the very end. And remember area cancels, so the radius is never needed.

4An iron rod of length 1 m is used to hang a mass of 100 kg using a horizontal support. If the elongation of the rod is 2 cm, find the strain produced in the rod.Longitudinal strainRod Elongation Trick: Young's Modulus & Stress-Strain Problem | NEET/JEE Physics 🟡 Similar0.02 (= 2%)
Concept Longitudinal strain is the fractional change in length — how much the rod stretches relative to its own length. It is a pure ratio with no units, and it does not depend on the hanging mass, the cross-section or the material; all of those act only through the elongation, which is already given. So the mass of 100 kg is a distractor here — the elongation and original length are all we need.
Formula strain ε = ΔL / L. Strain is defined as change in length divided by original length; it is dimensionless, so both lengths must be in the same unit before dividing.
Solution
1. Identify the given quantities: original length L = 1 m, elongation ΔL = 2 cm.
2. Put both lengths in the same unit: ΔL = 2 cm = 0.02 m, L = 1 m.Mixing cm and m is the one real trap — convert first, divide second.
3. Apply the definition: ε = ΔL/L = 0.02 / 1.
4. Evaluate: ε = 0.02.
5. As a percentage this is 0.02 × 100 = 2% — the rod has stretched by 2% of its length.
6. Note the 100 kg load plays no part: it would be needed for stress or Young's modulus, but strain follows directly from the two lengths.Spotting which data a formula actually uses saves time and avoids inventing extra steps.
⚡ JEE Trick Strain is a dimensionless ratio — if your answer carries a unit you have made an error. Convert the elongation to the same unit as the length before dividing, and ignore the mass: it is bait for students who reach for stress or Young's modulus when only strain is asked.

1What is plasticity? Concept Plasticity is the exact opposite of elasticity: a plastic body, stressed beyond its elastic limit, keeps a permanent change of shape even after the deforming force is removed. Clay, soft metals and dough behave this way. The test for each option is simple — does the body retain its new shape (plastic) or recover the old one (elastic)?
Formula After unloading, residual strain εres ≠ 0 for a plastic body (contrast εres = 0 for a perfectly elastic one). A non-zero leftover strain is the mathematical signature of plastic, permanent deformation.
Solution
1. Option (a) “Ability to return to original shape” is elasticity, not plasticity — the reverse property.
2. Option (b) “Property of resisting all deformation” describes rigidity; a plastic body deforms readily.Resisting deformation entirely is rigidity; plasticity is about keeping the deformation, not preventing it.
3. Option (c) “Permanent deformation after force removal” is precisely the definition of plasticity — the body does not spring back.
4. Option (d) “Ability to absorb water” is an unrelated material property with nothing to do with deformation.
5. Only (c) captures “permanent change that survives unloading”, so (c) is correct.
6. Cross-check: on a stress–strain graph, a plastic material does not return along the loading line — it ends at a non-zero strain when the stress is back to zero.
⚡ JEE Trick Elasticity and plasticity are a yes/no pair: does the shape come back? “Permanent” is the keyword for plasticity. Options that mention returning (elastic) or resisting (rigid) are deliberate decoys — and “absorb water” is pure noise.
Answer: (c) Permanent deformation after force removal.
2Which of the following influences elasticity the most? (a) Surface area (b) Length (c) Interatomic forces (d) Colour of materialCause of elasticityNEET Physics | Factors Affecting Elasticity | Theory | In English 🟡 SimilarcInteratomic forces
Concept Elasticity is a restoring effect: when atoms are pushed off their lattice positions, the interatomic (intermolecular) forces pull them back, returning the body to its original shape. These bonds act like tiny springs, so the strength and nature of the interatomic forces is what fundamentally sets how elastic a material is. External geometry such as length or surface area changes the stiffness of a specimen but not the elasticity of the material.
Formula Restoring force per displacement, the bond “spring constant” kbond, fixes Young's modulus and hence the elasticity. Stronger interatomic bonds → stiffer effective springs → higher modulus → more elastic.
Solution
1. Option (a) Surface area and (b) Length are geometric features of the sample; they alter how much a given rod stretches but not the material's intrinsic elasticity.Young's modulus is a material constant — independent of the specimen's size and shape.
2. Option (d) Colour is an optical property, wholly unrelated to mechanical restoring forces.
3. Option (c) Interatomic forces are the actual source of the restoring action, so they govern elasticity most directly.
4. The spring-and-ball model makes this concrete: the balls are atoms, the springs are the interatomic bonds, and it is the springs that restore the shape.
5. Hence the factor that influences elasticity the most is (c) interatomic forces.
6. Confirm by elimination: every other option is either geometric (a, b) or irrelevant (d), leaving (c) as the only physical cause.
⚡ JEE Trick Separate material properties from specimen properties: elasticity (via Young's modulus) is intrinsic and set by interatomic bonds, while stretch for a given load also depends on length and area. The cause is the bonds; geometry only scales the effect.
Answer: (c) Interatomic forces.
3The ratio of change in length to original length is: (a) Stress (b) Shear modulus (c) Strain (d) Volume stressDefinition of strainStrain | Properties of bulk matter | Class 11 Physics 🟡 SimilarcStrain
Concept The ratio of change in length to original length is the definition of longitudinal strain — a dimensionless measure of how much a body has deformed relative to its own size. 4Which of the following is not a type of stress? (a) Tensile (b) Compressive (c) Lateral (d) ShearingTypes of stressTypes of Stresses: Tensile, Compressive, Shear, Torsional, Bending Stress 🟡 SimilarcLateral

2. Option (b) Compressive stress — a normal stress that shortens the body; a valid type.
