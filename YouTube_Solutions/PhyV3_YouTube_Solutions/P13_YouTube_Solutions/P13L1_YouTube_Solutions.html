1Which of the following statements about gravitational force is correct? (a) Gravitational force can be repulsive in some cases (b) Gravitational force requires a medium to act (b) Gravitational force always acts along the line joining two masses (d) Gravitational force acts only when objects are in contact — (Level-1)Nature of gravitational forceGravitational force is a conservation and medium independent force. Its nature is attractive.🟡 SimilarcAlways acts along the line joining two masses
Concept Newton's gravitational force is a central, always-attractive, action-at-a-distance force. It acts along the straight line joining the centres of the two masses, needs no medium (it works across empty space, which is how the Sun holds the Earth), and never needs contact. The trap is to carry over ideas from electrostatics (repulsion) or from contact forces (friction, normal reaction). Note: the book prints the third option label as "(b)" again; it is really option (c).
Formula F̄₁₂ = −(G m₁m₂/r²) r̂₁₂ The force on mass 1 points from 1 towards 2, i.e. along the line of centres, and the minus sign makes it attractive for every pair of (always positive) masses.
Solution
1. Option (a), "can be repulsive": mass has only one sign, so G m₁m₂/r² is always positive and the force is always attractive ⇒ false.Charges can be + or −, masses cannot; that is why only electric force repels.
2. Option (b), "requires a medium": the Sun–Earth force acts across about 1.5 × 10¹¹ m of near-vacuum ⇒ no medium needed ⇒ false.
3. Option (c) [printed as a second "(b)"], "acts along the line joining two masses": F̄ is along r̂ joining the centres, so gravity is a central force ⇒ true.
4. Option (d), "acts only in contact": gravity is a field force; the Moon is attracted by the Earth from 3.84 × 10⁵ km away ⇒ false.
5. Extra check: the force also obeys Newton's third law (equal and opposite pair along the same line), which is consistent only with a force directed along the line of centres.
6. Exactly one statement survives ⇒ option (c).
 (a) repulsive?        masses > 0 => always attractive  X
 (b) needs medium?     acts through vacuum              X
 (c) along line?       F = -(G m1 m2/r^2) r^   central   OK
 (d) only on contact?  field force, acts at distance     X

⚡ JEE Trick Remember the four "always" words for gravity: always attractive, always central, always mutual (third law), always present (no medium, no shielding). Trap: option (a) tempts students who have just done Coulomb's law, but there is no negative mass, so gravity never repels.
Answer: (c) Gravitational force always acts along the line joining two masses.
2Calculate the force of attraction between two bodies, each of mass 100 kg and 1 m apart on the surface of the Earth. Will the force of attraction be different if the same bodies are taken on the Moon, their separation remaining constant? — (Level-2)Newton's law of gravitation numericalCalculate the force of gravitation between the bodies , each of mass 100 kg and🟢 Exact6.67 × 10−7 N; No
Concept Newton's law gives the force between two masses using only G, the two masses and their separation. G is a universal constant: the same on the Earth, on the Moon or anywhere. So moving the pair to the Moon changes their weights (the pull of the Moon on each body), but not the mutual attraction between the two bodies. The trap is to mix up "weight" (depends on g) with "mutual gravitational force" (does not).
Formula F = G m₁m₂/r² with G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻². The bodies are treated as point masses 1 m apart; the law holds for any pair of masses regardless of location.
Solution
1. Data: m₁ = m₂ = 100 kg, r = 1 m, G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
2. Product of masses: m₁m₂ = 100 × 100 = 10⁴ kg²; r² = 1 m², so dividing by r² leaves the number unchanged.With r = 1 m the force is numerically just G × m₁m₂.
3. F = G m₁m₂/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 10⁴/1² = 6.67 × 10⁻⁷ N.
4. Compare with weight: each body weighs 100 × 9.8 = 980 N, so the mutual pull is about 10⁹ times smaller.This is why we never notice two people attracting each other.
5. On the Moon, m₁, m₂ and r are unchanged, and G is universal ⇒ F = 6.67 × 10⁻⁷ N again.
6. Only the weights change (g_moon ≈ g/6 ⇒ about 163 N each); the force between the two bodies does not change.
 F = G m1 m2 / r^2
   = 6.67e-11 x 100 x 100 / 1^2
   = 6.67e-11 x 1e4
   = 6.67e-7 N
 On Moon: G, m1, m2, r same  => F same
 -----------------------------------------
 F = 6.67 x 10^-7 N ; No change   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Ask: "which quantities in G m₁m₂/r² changed?" If none did, the force is unchanged, whatever planet you stand on. Trap: scaling the answer by 1/6 because "gravity on the Moon is one-sixth" - that factor applies to the Moon's pull on the bodies (their weight), not to the bodies' pull on each other.
Answer: F = 6.67 × 10⁻⁷ N; No, the force is the same on the Moon.

Concept The mass of the body is the same everywhere; its weight W = mg changes because g at the surface depends on the planet's mass and radius. "4.5 kgwt" means the body weighs what 4.5 kg weighs on Earth, i.e. its mass is 4.5 kg. The trap is to scale only by the mass ratio (1/9) and forget that a smaller radius increases g as 1/R².

5. Wp = We × (4/9) = 4.5 × 4/9 = 2 kgwt.

4Three masses, each equal to M, are placed at the three corners of a square of side a. Calculate of the force of attraction on unit mass at the fourth corner. — (Level-4)Superposition, masses on square cornersThree masses, each equal to M, are placed at the three corners of a square of side a. the force of🟢 Exact(GM/a2)(√2 + 1/2)
Concept Use the principle of superposition: the force on the unit mass is the vector sum of the three separate pulls. The two masses on adjacent corners pull along the two sides (at 90° to each other); the mass on the opposite corner pulls along the diagonal from distance a√2. By symmetry all three resultants point along the diagonal, so they simply add. The trap is to add all three magnitudes directly, or to forget that the diagonal distance is a√2.
Formula F = GMm/r² with m = 1, and R = √(F₁² + F₂²) for two perpendicular forces. Each mass acts independently; perpendicular equal forces add to √2 times one of them, along the bisector (the diagonal).
Solution
1. Adjacent corner along the top side: F₁ = GM·1/a² = GM/a².
2. Adjacent corner along the right side: F₂ = GM/a², perpendicular to F₁.
3. Resultant of F₁ and F₂: R₁₂ = √(F₁² + F₂²) = √2·GM/a², directed along the diagonal (45° to each side).Equal perpendicular vectors give a resultant along their bisector.
4. Opposite corner, distance d = a√2: F₃ = GM/d² = GM/(a√2)² = GM/(2a²), also along the diagonal.
5. R₁₂ and F₃ are collinear and in the same sense ⇒ F = √2·GM/a² + GM/(2a²).
6. F = (GM/a²)(√2 + 1/2) ≈ 1.91 GM/a², directed along the diagonal towards the opposite corner.
 F1 = F2 = GM/a^2   (sides, at 90 deg)
 F12 = sqrt(2) GM/a^2   (along diagonal)
 F3 = GM/(a sqrt2)^2 = GM/(2a^2)  (diagonal)
 F  = F12 + F3 = (GM/a^2)(sqrt2 + 1/2)
 -----------------------------------------
 F = (GM/a^2)(sqrt2 + 1/2) = 1.91 GM/a^2  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For any square-corner problem: two sides give √2·F₀ along the diagonal, the far corner gives F₀/2, total F₀(√2 + 1/2) ≈ 1.91 F₀. Trap: adding 1 + 1 + 1/2 = 2.5 F₀ (ignores directions) or using a instead of a√2 for the far corner (giving √2 + 1).
Answer: F = (GM/a²)(√2 + 1/2), along the diagonal towards the opposite corner.
5Three equal masses of 'm' kg each are fixed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle ABC. (i) What is the force acting on a mass 2m placed at the centroid G of the triangle ? (ii) What is the force if the mass at the vertex A is doubled? Take AG = BG = CG = 1m. — (Level-5)Force at centroid of triangleThree equal masses of m kg each are fixed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle ABC.(a) What is🟢 Exact(i) 0 (ii) 2Gm2 ĵ
Concept At the centroid of an equilateral triangle the three vertices are equidistant and 120° apart, so three equal pulls cancel by symmetry. When mass A is doubled, think of it as the original symmetric set plus an extra mass m at A: the symmetric set still gives zero, so only the extra m acts. The trap is to recompute all three forces and resolve components when superposition does it in one line.
Formula F = G m₁m₂/r² and F̄net = Σ F̄i Superposition: each vertex mass pulls the 2m at G independently, along the line from G to that vertex.
Solution
1. Each vertex mass m pulls 2m at distance 1 m: F = G·m·2m/1² = 2Gm², along GA, GB, GC.
2. Take A on the +y axis above G. GB and GC make 120° with GA ⇒ y-components: 2Gm²[1 + 2cos120°] = 2Gm²[1 − 1] = 0.cos120° = −1/2.
3. x-components of the pulls towards B and C are equal and opposite ⇒ 0. Hence (i) F̄ = 0.
4. (ii) Mass at A becomes 2m = m + m. The three m's still give zero (part i).
5. Extra m at A: F = G·m·2m/1² = 2Gm², directed from G towards A (+ĵ).
6. Check directly: pull of A = G(2m)(2m)/1² = 4Gm² up; B and C give 2·2Gm²·cos60° = 2Gm² down; net = 4Gm² − 2Gm² = 2Gm² ĵ.The downward components of GB, GC are along −y at 60° to the vertical.
 (i)  F_A = F_B = F_C = G m (2m)/1^2 = 2Gm^2
      at 120 deg  => vector sum = 0
 (ii) A -> 2m : 4Gm^2 (up)
      B + C   : 2 x 2Gm^2 x cos60 = 2Gm^2 (down)
      net     : 4Gm^2 - 2Gm^2 = 2Gm^2 (towards A)
 -----------------------------------------
 (i) 0   (ii) 2Gm^2 j^ (towards A)    <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick "Add and subtract" symmetry: any extra mass placed on an already-balanced arrangement gives the net force by itself. Trap: answering 4Gm² in (ii) by taking only the doubled vertex and forgetting B and C, or writing Gm² by forgetting the test mass is 2m, not m.
Answer: (i) F̄ = 0; (ii) F̄ = 2Gm² ĵ, i.e. 2Gm² N directed from G towards A.

1The gravitational force between two bodies is 1 N. If distance between them is doubled, what will be the gravitational force between them? — (Level: Board)Inverse square lawThe gravitational force between two bodies in 1 N if the distance between them is doubled, what ...🟢 Exact0.25 N (one-fourth)
Concept Gravitational force follows the inverse square law: with the masses fixed, F ∝ 1/r². Doubling r multiplies r² by 4, so the force drops to one-fourth. The trap is to think "inversely proportional" means simply halving the force when distance doubles. Notice the question gives no masses and no distance: none are needed, because the ratio method cancels everything except r.
Formula F = G m₁m₂/r² ⇒ F₂/F₁ = (r₁/r₂)² G, m₁ and m₂ are unchanged, so they cancel in the ratio and only the distance matters.
Solution
1. Initially F₁ = G m₁m₂/r² = 1 N.
2. New distance r₂ = 2r; masses unchanged.
3. Ratio: F₂/F₁ = (r₁/r₂)² = (r/2r)² = (1/2)² = 1/4.Square of the distance ratio, inverted.
4. F₂ = F₁ × (1/4) = 1 × 1/4 = 0.25 N.
5. Direct check: F₂ = G m₁m₂/(2r)² = G m₁m₂/(4r²) = (1/4)·(1 N) = 0.25 N.
6. General rule: r → kr gives F → F/k²; here k = 2 ⇒ F/4. Conversely, halving r would make the force 4 N.Useful for quick ratio questions in the MCQ round.
7. Physical picture: the gravitational "lines of influence" spread over a sphere of area 4πr²; doubling r spreads them over 4 times the area, so each body feels one-fourth the pull.
 F  proportional to 1/r^2   (m1, m2 fixed)
 F2/F1 = (r1/r2)^2 = (r/2r)^2 = 1/4
 F2    = 1 N x 1/4
       = 0.25 N
 check : G m1 m2/(2r)^2 = (1/4) G m1 m2/r^2
 -----------------------------------------
 F_new = 0.25 N (one-fourth)      <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For any inverse-square force (gravity, Coulomb, intensity of a point source), the factor is always the square of the distance factor. Distance ×2 ⇒ force ÷4; distance ×3 ⇒ force ÷9. Trap: answering 0.5 N treats the law as 1/r instead of 1/r². Another trap: answering 4 N by multiplying instead of dividing; increasing the distance must weaken gravity, so the answer has to be less than 1 N.
Answer: The force becomes one-fourth, i.e. 0.25 N.

The text says "four" masses but names only A, B, C (and the key uses three), so we take masses at A, B, C. Trap: adding three magnitudes to get 4Gm²/a².
Formula F₀ = Gm²/((√3/2)a)² = 4Gm²/(3a²), F = F₀|ûA + ûB + ûC| Equal magnitudes, so only the unit vectors need adding.
Solution
1. Axes: x right, y up, z towards the viewer (front face z = a). From the figure A = (0, a, 0), B = (a, a, a), C = (0, 0, a); D = (a/2, a/2, a/2).
2. Distance of each corner from D: (√3/2)a ⇒ F₀ = Gm²/(3a²/4) = 4Gm²/(3a²).
3. Unit vectors D → corner (each ×1/√3): ûA = (−1, 1, −1), ûB = (1, 1, 1), ûC = (−1, −1, 1).
4. Sum: (−1 + 1 − 1, 1 + 1 − 1, −1 + 1 + 1)/√3 = (−1, 1, 1)/√3. No two of A, B, C are diagonally opposite, so nothing cancels completely.
5. Magnitude of the sum: √(1 + 1 + 1)/√3 = 1 ⇒ F = 1·F₀ = 4Gm²/(3a²).
6. Direction: along (−1, 1, 1), i.e. from D towards the vertex (0, a, a), the top-left corner of the front face (unmarked in the figure).
 D = (a/2, a/2, a/2);  |DA| = |DB| = |DC| = (sqrt3/2) a
 F0 = G m^2 /(3a^2/4)              = 4Gm^2/(3a^2)
 uA + uB + uC = [(-1,1,-1)+(1,1,1)+(-1,-1,1)]/sqrt3
              = (-1, 1, 1)/sqrt3,   |sum| = 1
 -----------------------------------------
 F = 4Gm^2/(3a^2), towards (0, a, a)   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick At the cube centre, a mass at a vertex is cancelled by an equal mass at the body-opposite vertex. The sum of the three unit vectors (each a vector of ±1 entries) came out as a single vertex direction, so the net equals one pull F₀. Trap: 3F₀ = 4Gm²/a² (scalar sum) or Gm²/a² (using side a as the distance).
Answer: F = 4Gm²/(3a²), directed from D towards the vertex (0, a, a), the unmarked top-left vertex of the front face.

Find the gravitational force on a fourth particle P of same mass, placed at a distance d from the particle B on the perpendicular bisector of the line AC as shown in figure. — (Level-6)Superposition, collinear massesThree particles A B and C each of mass in are placed in a line with AB = BC = d. Find the🟢 Exact(Gm2/d2)(1 + 1/√2) along PB
Concept Superposition again: P lies on the perpendicular bisector of AC, so the pulls of A and C are mirror images. Their components parallel to AC cancel and their components along PB add to the direct pull of B. Trap: taking PA = d (it is √2 d) or adding the full pulls of A and C without the cos 45° factor.
Formula F = FB + 2FAcos45° with FB = Gm²/d², FA = Gm²/(√2d)². PA makes 45° with PB because PB = AB = d.
Solution
1. Geometry: PB = d, AB = BC = d ⇒ PA = PC = √(d² + d²) = √2 d, and ∠APB = ∠CPB = 45°.
2. Pull of B: FB = Gm·m/d² = Gm²/d², along PB.
3. Pull of A (same for C): FA = Gm²/(√2d)² = Gm²/(2d²).
4. Horizontal components FAsin45° (towards A) and FCsin45° (towards C) cancel. Equal magnitudes, opposite directions.
5. Components along PB: 2FAcos45° = 2·(Gm²/2d²)·(1/√2) = Gm²/(√2 d²).
6. Only the PB direction survives, so the vector sum reduces to an ordinary scalar sum of the PB components.
7. Net: F = Gm²/d² + Gm²/(√2 d²) = (Gm²/d²)(1 + 1/√2) ≈ 1.707 Gm²/d², directed from P towards B.
 PB = d,  PA = PC = sqrt2 d,  angle APB = 45 deg
 F_B          = G m^2/d^2
 F_A = F_C    = G m^2/(2 d^2)
 along PB: 2 F_A cos45 = G m^2/(sqrt2 d^2)
 sideways: F_A sin45 - F_C sin45 = 0
 -----------------------------------------
 F = (G m^2/d^2)(1 + 1/sqrt2) along PB   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Bracket the answer: it must exceed FB = Gm²/d² and be less than FB + 2FA = 2Gm²/d²; 1.707 fits. This is the general form of Level-5 Q10 (there m = 3 kg, AB = 2 m, PB = 3 m, so the angle is not 45°). Trap: 2Gm²/d², which forgets the cosine and adds the full pulls of A and C, or (Gm²/d²)(1 + √2), which uses PA = d.
Answer: F = (Gm²/d²)(1 + 1/√2) ≈ 1.71 Gm²/d², directed along PB (from P towards B).
9Two balls of mass m each are hung side by side by two long threads of equal length l. If the distance between upper ends is r, show that the distance r' between the centres of the ball is given by g r'²(r − r') = 2l Gm — (Level-6)Hanging balls attraction equilibriumTwo balls of mass m each are hung side by side by side by two long threads of equal🟢 Exact(r − r')/l = Gm2/(mgr'2) ⚠
Concept Each ball is in equilibrium under three forces: weight mg down, tension T along the thread, and the tiny gravitational pull F of the other ball, horizontal. The pull tilts each thread inward by a small angle θ. By symmetry each ball moves in by half the total shortening, (r − r')/2. Trap: using the whole r − r' as one ball's shift, which loses a factor 2.
Formula tanθ = F/(mg), F = Gm²/r'², sinθ ≈ tanθ = [(r − r')/2]/l. Long threads and a feeble pull ⇒ θ is very small.
Solution
1. Horizontal balance: T sinθ = F = Gm²/r'². Vertical balance: T cosθ = mg.
2. Divide: tanθ = F/(mg) = Gm²/(r'²·mg) = Gm/(g r'²).
3. Geometry: suspension points are r apart, centres r' apart ⇒ each ball shifts x = (r − r')/2 towards the other.
4. Small angle: sinθ = x/l ≈ tanθ ⇒ tanθ = (r − r')/(2l). Valid because θ is minute for gravitational forces.
5. Equate: (r − r')/(2l) = Gm/(g r'²).
6. Cross-multiply: g r'²(r − r') = 2lGm, exactly the result asked for. ✔
7. The key's line (r − r')/l = Gm²/(mgr'²) would give g r'²(r − r') = lGm, which misses this factor 2 (it treats r − r' as one ball's shift).
 x (each ball) = (r - r')/2
 T sin(t) = Gm^2/r'^2 ;  T cos(t) = mg
 tan(t) = Gm/(g r'^2) ~ sin(t) = x/l = (r - r')/(2l)
 (r - r')/(2l) = Gm/(g r'^2)
 -----------------------------------------
 g r'^2 (r - r') = 2 l G m          <-- shown
⚡ JEE Trick In any "two hanging bodies repel/attract" problem (charged pith balls too), write tanθ = F/(mg) and remember each ball takes half the change in separation. A quick dimension check: g r'²(r − r') has units m⁴ s⁻², and lGm has m·m³ s⁻² = m⁴ s⁻², consistent. Dimensions cannot catch a missing 2, so check the symmetry step.
Answer: (r − r')/l = Gm²/(mgr'²) per the key — my working gives (r − r')/(2l) = Gm²/(mgr'²), i.e. g r'²(r − r') = 2lGm as the question states ⚠
10Four identical point masses m each are kept at the vertices A, B, and C of a cube having side length 'a' (see figure). Another identical mass is placed at the center point D of the cube. Where will you place a fifth identical mass so that the net gravitational force acting on mass at D becomes zero? — (Level-6)Null force at cube centreFour identical point masses m each are kept at the vertices A, B, and C of a cube having side length🟡 SimilarAt the corner of the floor (bottom face) diagonally opposite C
Concept From Q7, the three corner masses A, B, C pull D with a net force F₀ = 4Gm²/(3a²) pointing at one particular vertex of the cube. Any vertex is at the same distance (√3/2)a from D, so one more mass m at the vertex body-opposite to that direction pulls with the same F₀ the other way and cancels it. The task is only to identify that vertex. Trap: putting the fifth mass opposite to one of A, B, C, which cancels just one pull.
Formula Fnet = F₀(ûA + ûB + ûC + û₅) = 0 ⇒ û₅ = −(ûA + ûB + ûC) All five pulls have the same magnitude F₀.
Solution
1. Axes as in Q7: x right, y up, z towards the viewer; A = (0, a, 0), B = (a, a, a), C = (0, 0, a), D = (a/2, a/2, a/2).
2. Unit vectors from D (×1/√3): ûA = (−1, 1, −1), ûB = (1, 1, 1), ûC = (−1, −1, 1).
3. Their sum: (−1, 1, 1)/√3, magnitude 1 ⇒ net F₀ towards the vertex (0, a, a).
4. Needed: û₅ = (1, −1, −1)/√3 ⇒ the vertex D + (a/2)(1, −1, −1) = (a, 0, 0). It is a real vertex, so a mass m there pulls with exactly F₀.
5. Identify (a, 0, 0): y = 0 means the bottom face (floor). The floor's corners are (0,0,0), (a,0,0), (0,0,a), (a,0,a); C = (0, 0, a) and (a, 0, 0) are the ends of a face diagonal.
6. So the fifth mass goes at the corner of the floor diagonally opposite C (the bottom-right corner of the back face); then Fnet on D = 0.
 uA + uB + uC = (-1, 1, 1)/sqrt3     |sum| = 1
 u5 must be   = ( 1,-1,-1)/sqrt3
 vertex = D + (a/2)(1,-1,-1)          = (a, 0, 0)
 floor y = 0 : C = (0,0,a)  <-diag->  (a,0,0)
 -----------------------------------------
 5th mass at (a, 0, 0): floor corner opposite C  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Shortcut: the net pull of A, B, C points at vertex V = (0, a, a), the one corner joined to each of A, B and C by a single edge; the cancelling mass goes at the body-opposite of V. Trap: placing it at the vertex body-opposite to C, (a, a, 0), which cancels C's pull alone and leaves a net force.
Answer: Place the fifth mass m at the corner of the floor (bottom face) diagonally opposite C, i.e. vertex (a, 0, 0); the net force on D then becomes zero.
