1Charles's Law: at constant P, volume ∝ which quantity? (Level-1)Charles' law conceptA-CHARLES🟡 SimilarcTemperature (in K)
Concept Charles' Law describes how a fixed amount of gas expands when heated at constant pressure. The key subtlety this question tests is which temperature: gas volume is directly proportional to absolute (Kelvin) temperature, never to Celsius. On the Kelvin scale a doubling of temperature genuinely doubles the volume, because 0 K is the true zero of molecular motion; the Celsius scale has an arbitrary zero (the freezing point of water), so a "doubling" of °C is physically meaningless.
Formula V ∝ T at constant P and n, i.e. V / T = constant, where V = volume and T = absolute temperature in kelvin (T/K = t/°C + 273.15). Proportionality to an absolute scale is what makes V/T a fixed ratio; if you used °C the ratio would change as the zero point shifts.
Solution
1. Hold pressure and amount of gas fixed — the two conditions Charles' Law requires.Only then is volume controlled by temperature alone.
2. Heating expands the gas: molecules move faster, strike the walls harder, and push the piston out until pressure re-balances, so V rises with T.
3. The proportionality holds against the Kelvin temperature: V = k·T with T in K.Only an absolute scale gives V = 0 at T = 0, matching the physical picture of no molecular motion.
4. Therefore volume is proportional to temperature in kelvin — option (c).
 quantity varied   volume behaviour     valid proportionality?
 T in Kelvin        V doubles as T×2     YES  (V ∝ T)   ✓
 T in Celsius       arbitrary zero       NO   (V not ∝ t°C)
 pressure P         V shrinks (Boyle)    that is Boyle's law
⚡ JEE Trick In every Charles'-Law statement or numerical, the instant you see "temperature" think Kelvin — convert °C to K first, always. Trap: options offering "temperature in °C" are designed to catch students who forget the +273; V ∝ T is only true on the absolute scale.
Answer: (c) Temperature (in K).
2Gas 1 atm, 4 L — pressure when volume decreased to 2 L (Level-2)Boyle's law numericalA-BOYLENUM🟡 Similarc2 atm
Concept At constant temperature a gas obeys Boyle's Law: squeeze it into a smaller volume and its pressure rises in exact inverse proportion. The product P×V is a conserved quantity, so the two states of the gas are linked by P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. Halving the volume must therefore exactly double the pressure — no other data is needed.
Formula P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (constant T, n), giving P₂ = P₁V₁ / V₂. Units: P in atm, V in litres; only the ratio V₁/V₂ matters, so units cancel and any consistent pair works. Because PV is constant, whatever factor V shrinks by, P must grow by the same factor.
Solution
1. List the states: P₁ = 1 atm, V₁ = 4 L; V₂ = 2 L, P₂ = ?Temperature is unchanged, so Boyle's Law applies directly.
2. Write Boyle's Law and solve for P₂: P₂ = P₁V₁ / V₂.
3. Substitute the numbers: P₂ = (1 × 4) / 2.1 × 4 = 4 atm·L is the conserved product P₁V₁.
4. Divide: P₂ = 4 / 2 = 2 atm.Volume halved (4→2), so pressure doubled (1→2) — exactly the inverse relationship.
 state   P (atm)   V (L)   P·V (atm·L)
 1        1         4       4
 2        ?         2       must equal 4  ⇒ P₂ = 4/2 = 2
⚡ JEE Trick For Boyle's-law MCQs don't plug into the formula — just read off the volume ratio: V goes 4→2 (÷2), so P goes ×2. Halve V ⇒ double P, on sight. Trap: adding or subtracting pressures; Boyle's law is purely multiplicative in the volume ratio.
Answer: (c) 2 atm.

Concept This is Charles' Law run "backwards": instead of finding the new volume we are told the new volume and must find the temperature that produces it, at constant pressure. Because V/T is constant (with T in kelvin), a bigger volume demands a proportionally bigger absolute temperature. The one trap is scale-handling: we must convert the given 27 °C to kelvin, work entirely in kelvin, then convert the answer back to °C.
Formula V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ ⇒ T₂ = T₁ · V₂/V₁, T in K, V in litres. Conversion: T/K = t/°C + 273. Only the volume ratio V₂/V₁ scales the temperature, so units of V cancel.
Solution
1. Convert the initial temperature to kelvin: T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K.Charles' Law is valid only on the absolute scale.
2. Write the law solved for T₂: T₂ = T₁ · V₂/V₁.
3. Substitute: T₂ = 300 × (4 / 3).Volume grows from 3 L to 4 L, a factor of 4/3, so T must grow by the same factor.
4. Evaluate: 4/3 = 1.333…, so T₂ = 300 × 1.333… = 400 K.
5. Convert back to Celsius: t₂ = 400 − 273 = 127 °C.Always report in the unit the question asks for.
 V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
 3/300 = 4/T₂
 T₂ = 4 × 300 / 3 = 400 K
 t₂ = 400 − 273 = 127 °C
⚡ JEE Trick Convert to kelvin FIRST, scale by the volume ratio, convert back LAST. Trap: students who scale 27 °C directly by 4/3 get 36 °C — badly wrong, because °C is not an absolute scale. The +273 must be applied before any multiplication.
Answer: (b) 127 °C.
4Two isotherms T1 & T2 (T2>T1) on same P–V graph — which statement (Level-4)Isotherms / P–V graphA-GRAPH🟡 SimilaraThe curve for T2 lies above T1 because pressure is higher at the same V.
Concept An isotherm is the P–V curve for a gas held at one fixed temperature; by Boyle's Law each is a rectangular hyperbola with PV = constant. Raising the temperature raises that constant (PV = nRT), so a hotter gas sits on a hyperbola with a larger PV product. On a P vs V plot this pushes the whole T₂ curve further from the origin — i.e. above the cooler T₁ curve when compared at the same volume.
Formula PV = nRT, so along an isotherm PV = constant, and at fixed V the pressure scales as P ∝ T. Because PV rises with T, the constant defining the hyperbola is bigger for T₂ than for T₁.
Solution
1. Each temperature gives its own hyperbola: PV = nRT₁ for the cooler gas, PV = nRT₂ for the hotter (T₂ > T₁).Same n and R, so the only difference is the temperature, and hence the value of the constant.
2. Compare the two at one fixed volume V: P = nRT/V, so P₂/P₁ = T₂/T₁ > 1.At equal V a higher T must give a higher P — the pressures are compared vertically on the graph.
3. Therefore, at any given V, the T₂ curve carries the higher pressure and lies above the T₁ curve — statement (a).
4. Reject the alternatives: the curves never cross (a higher isotherm stays higher everywhere), and the hotter curve is not the lower one.

   at fixed V:  P(T₂) > P(T₁)
⚡ JEE Trick "Higher temperature ⇒ isotherm farther from the origin." On a P–V plot the top-right-most hyperbola is always the hottest. Trap: don't confuse "above" — the ranking holds when you compare curves at the same volume (read pressures vertically); isotherms of different T never intersect.

52 L, 1 atm, 300 K — compress isothermally to 2 atm, then heat at const P to 2 L; final T (Level-5)Combined gas lawA-COMBINED🟡 Similard600 K
Concept This is a two-step gas process that the combined gas law handles in one line, because that law already contains Boyle's and Charles' laws as special cases. The trick is to recognise that only the initial and final states matter — the intermediate state after the isothermal squeeze is not needed if we jump straight from start to finish with PV/T = constant.
Formula P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂, so T₂ = T₁ · (P₂V₂)/(P₁V₁). Units: P atm, V litres, T kelvin. PV/T is fixed for a fixed amount of gas, letting us equate the raw start and end states and skip the middle step.
Solution
1. Initial state: P₁ = 1 atm, V₁ = 2 L, T₁ = 300 K.
2. Final state after both steps: pressure is now 2 atm (from the compression) and the gas was heated back to V = 2 L, so P₂ = 2 atm, V₂ = 2 L, T₂ = ?The heating happens at the compressed pressure of 2 atm, which is why the final pressure is 2 atm, not 1.
3. Apply the combined law: T₂ = T₁ · (P₂V₂)/(P₁V₁).
4. Substitute: T₂ = 300 × (2 × 2) / (1 × 2).Numerator P₂V₂ = 4 atm·L; denominator P₁V₁ = 2 atm·L.
5. Simplify: T₂ = 300 × 4/2 = 300 × 2 = 600 K.
 step               P(atm)  V(L)   T(K)
 start               1       2      300
 after isothermal→   2       1      300   (Boyle: 1×2 = 2×1)
 after heating→      2       2      600   (Charles: 1/300 = 2/T)
 check: P₁V₁/T₁ = 2/300 = P₂V₂/T₂ = 4/600 ✓
⚡ JEE Trick For any multi-step process asking only for the final state, ignore the intermediate values and equate start vs end with P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂. Here PV doubled (2→4 atm·L) while nothing else changed, so T doubled: 300 → 600 K. Trap: forgetting the pressure stayed at 2 atm after the squeeze.
Answer: (d) 600 K.

1State and explain Boyle's lawBoyle's law conceptA-BOYLE🟡 Similar—
Concept Boyle's Law is the first of the gas laws and describes the pressure–volume behaviour of a fixed mass of gas kept at constant temperature. Physically, squeezing a gas into a smaller space forces its molecules to strike the walls more often per second, so the pressure rises; the relationship is not just "pressure goes up" but the precise statement that pressure and volume are inversely proportional.
Statement At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure. V ∝ 1/P ⇒ PV = constant ⇒ P₁V₁ = P₂V₂. The constant depends only on temperature and amount of gas; as long as those are fixed, PV cannot change.
Explanation
1. Take a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature.These two conditions are essential — Boyle's Law fails if either changes.
2. Reduce the volume: molecules travel shorter distances between wall collisions, so they hit the walls more frequently and pressure rises.
3. The rise is exactly proportional: halving V doubles P, so the product PV stays constant.
4. Graphically, a P vs V plot at constant T is a rectangular hyperbola; a P vs 1/V plot is a straight line through the origin.
 P (atm)   V (L)   P·V
 1          6       6
 2          3       6   ← constant
 3          2       6
 6          1       6
⚡ JEE Trick Anchor Boyle's Law to its graph: "P vs V = hyperbola, P vs 1/V = straight line through origin." Examiners love the 1/V version because it turns a curve into a testable straight line. Trap: the law needs constant temperature — quoting it without that condition loses marks.
Key point: at constant temperature, P ∝ 1/V for a fixed mass of gas, so PV = constant (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂); the P–V graph is a rectangular hyperbola.
2Explain the variation of pressure of a gas with volume at constant temperatureBoyle's law (P–V)A-BOYLE🟡 Similar—
Concept This question asks for the same physics as Boyle's Law but phrased as a trend: how does pressure respond as we change the volume, temperature held fixed? The answer is an inverse variation — pressure and volume move in opposite directions in exact proportion — and a full explanation should give the kinetic reason plus the P–V and P–(1/V) graph shapes.
Key relation P ∝ 1/V at constant T (fixed mass), i.e. PV = constant. Compress the gas and pressure rises; expand it and pressure falls, so their product never changes.
Explanation
1. Kinetic picture: pressure is caused by molecules colliding with the container walls.More frequent or harder collisions per unit area = higher pressure.
2. Shrink the volume at constant T: molecular speeds are unchanged (T fixed), but the molecules are packed into a smaller space, so wall collisions become more frequent → pressure increases.
3. Expand the volume: collisions spread out over more wall area and occur less often → pressure decreases.
4. The change is exactly reciprocal: doubling V halves P, tripling V cuts P to one-third — hence PV is constant and P vs V traces a rectangular hyperbola.
 volume change     pressure response    P·V
 V → V/2            P → 2P               constant
 V → 2V             P → P/2              constant
 P vs V  : hyperbola
 P vs 1/V: straight line through origin
⚡ JEE Trick "Constant T + changing V ⇒ inverse variation of P." State the kinetic reason (collision frequency) AND draw both graphs for full marks. Trap: saying pressure "increases with volume" — it is the opposite; P and V vary inversely.
Key point: at constant temperature the pressure of a fixed mass of gas varies inversely with its volume (P ∝ 1/V, PV = constant); compressing the gas raises the wall-collision frequency and hence the pressure.

4State and explain Charles' lawCharles' law conceptA-CHARLES🟡 Similar—
Concept Charles' Law is the temperature–volume gas law, the partner of Boyle's Law. It states how a fixed mass of gas expands when heated at constant pressure. The essential feature — and the point examiners test — is that volume is proportional to the absolute (Kelvin) temperature, which is why extrapolating the V–T line backwards reaches zero volume at −273.15 °C (absolute zero).
Statement At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. V ∝ T ⇒ V/T = constant ⇒ V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (T in K). Direct proportion to an absolute scale is what forces the graph through the true zero of volume at 0 K.
Explanation
1. Hold pressure and amount of gas constant.Both conditions are required; changing either breaks the simple V ∝ T relation.
2. Heat the gas: molecules move faster and push the piston outward until the internal pressure again matches the fixed external pressure, so the volume grows.
3. The growth is proportional to absolute temperature: doubling T (in kelvin) doubles V.
4. A V vs T(K) graph is a straight line through the origin; extended back to V = 0 it meets the temperature axis at −273.15 °C = 0 K, absolute zero.
 T (K)   V (L)   V/T
 200      4       0.02
 300      6       0.02   ← constant
 400      8       0.02
 (line through origin; V→0 at T→0 K = −273.15 °C)
⚡ JEE Trick Tie Charles' Law to absolute zero: the V–T line hitting V = 0 at −273.15 °C is the classic derivation examiners want. Always convert °C→K before using V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. Trap: stating V ∝ T in Celsius — false, because °C has an arbitrary (non-absolute) zero.
Key point: at constant pressure the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (V ∝ T, V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ with T in K); the V–T graph is a straight line meeting V = 0 at absolute zero, −273.15 °C.

5At high altitudes, what causes altitude sickness?Boyle's law applicationA-BOYLE🟡 SimilarbLow atmospheric pressure
Concept This is a real-world application of gas behaviour. As you climb, there is less air column above you, so the atmospheric pressure falls. 4. So the underlying cause is low atmospheric pressure — option (b); it is not "less oxygen fraction" (air is still 21% O₂) nor cold or humidity.

1Find the Kelvin equivalent of 27 °CTemperature conversionwatch?v=WQ5j0nwBmcM🟢 Exactb300 K
Concept The Kelvin scale is the absolute temperature scale: its zero sits at absolute zero (−273.15 °C), the point where molecular kinetic energy is minimum and below which no temperature exists.
