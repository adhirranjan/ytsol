15 kg block on fixed 53° incline, 100 N applied horizontally (arrow drawn horizontal in the figure, not parallel to the slope) — find acceleration & normal forceApplied force on inclineObject on incline with an applied force🟡 Similara = 4 m s⁻² up the incline; N = 110 N
Concept A block on a fixed incline with an extra applied force is a two-axis Newton problem. The trick is to resolve along the incline (gives the acceleration) and perpendicular to it (gives the normal force) rather than along horizontal/vertical. As printed, the 100 N arrow in the figure is drawn horizontal (it is not parallel to the drawn hypotenuse), pushing the block into the 53° slope, so it contributes to both axes: a component up the slope and a component pressing the block onto the surface.
Formula Along the incline: ma = F cosθ − mg sinθ. Perpendicular: N = mg cosθ + F sinθ. Here m = 5 kg, F = 100 N (horizontal), θ = 53°, g = 10 m s⁻², sin53° = 0.8, cos53° = 0.6. A horizontal force makes angle θ with the incline surface, so its along-slope share is F cosθ and its into-surface share is F sinθ — exactly swapped relative to gravity.
Solution
1. Resolve the applied force F = 100 N along the slope: F∥ = F cos53° = 100 × 0.6 = 60 N up the incline.The horizontal direction makes 53° with the incline surface, so the projection onto the slope carries cos53°.
2. Resolve F perpendicular to the slope: F⊥ = F sin53° = 100 × 0.8 = 80 N pressing into the surface.
3. Resolve the weight mg = 5 × 10 = 50 N: down-slope component = mg sin53° = 50 × 0.8 = 40 N; into-surface component = mg cos53° = 50 × 0.6 = 30 N.
4. Newton along the slope (up positive): 5a = 60 − 40 = 20 N ⇒ a = 4 m s⁻² up the incline.Units check: N ÷ kg = kg m s⁻² ÷ kg = m s⁻². ✓
5. Newton perpendicular (no acceleration ⊥ to a fixed incline): N − 30 − 80 = 0 ⇒ N = 110 N.N > 0, so the block does stay on the surface — the answer is self-consistent.

2Three blocks 10 kg, 8 kg, 7 kg on fixed 37° incline; 100 N up the incline at the bottom, 50 N down at the top — acceleration & normal forcesMulti-block on inclineNormal force, tension, inclined planes & FBDs🟡 Similara = 4 m s⁻² down the incline; normals: 10 kg–wedge 80 N, 8 kg–wedge 64 N, 7 kg–wedge 56 N, 10–8 kg 80 N, 8–7 kg 64 N
Concept Three blocks touching on a common incline all share one acceleration, because they cannot pass through each other and the pushes between them are internal. So the fast route is a two-stage attack: first treat the whole 25 kg train as a single body to get a, then isolate one block at a time to dig out the internal contact forces. Normal forces from the surface are decoupled — they follow from the perpendicular axis alone.
Formula System: (Σm)a = ΣF∥. Each block: Ni = mi g cosθ (surface normal) and mia = ΣF∥,i (for contacts). Data: m = 10, 8, 7 kg (bottom→top), θ = 37°, 100 N up the slope on the 10 kg, 50 N down the slope on the 7 kg, g = 10 m s⁻², sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8. Both applied forces lie along the incline, so they do not touch the perpendicular axis.
Solution
1. Total mass = 10 + 8 + 7 = 25 kg; total weight = 250 N; down-slope component = 250 × sin37° = 250 × 0.6 = 150 N.
2. Whole train along the slope (up positive): 25a = +100 − 50 − 150 = −100 N ⇒ a = −4 m s⁻², i.e. 4 m s⁻² down the incline.The 100 N push is not enough to beat gravity plus the 50 N, so the train runs down.
3. Surface normals (perpendicular axis, no acceleration there): N10 = 10×10×0.8 = 80 N; N8 = 8×10×0.8 = 64 N; N7 = 7×10×0.8 = 56 N.
4. Contact between 10 kg and 8 kg — isolate the 10 kg: 10(−4) = 100 − (10×10×0.6) − N12 ⇒ −40 = 100 − 60 − N12 ⇒ N12 = 80 N.
5. Contact between 8 kg and 7 kg — isolate the 8 kg: 8(−4) = 80 − 48 − N23 ⇒ −32 = 32 − N23 ⇒ N23 = 64 N.
6. Cross-check on the 7 kg: 64 − 42 − 50 = −28 N and 7 × (−4) = −28 N. ✓ Consistent.

3String constraint (all inextensible): two fixed pulleys, B and A each move 2 m/s, hanging block m — find vString constraint / velocityDouble-pulley constrained-motion method🟡 Similarv = 1.5 m s⁻¹
Concept Constrained-motion problems are solved by writing the total length of each string as a sum of segments and differentiating: since the string cannot stretch, dL/dt = 0. Here there are two strings. The right string ends at A and wraps a small movable pulley; the left string ends at B, wraps the big lower pulley (carrying m), and its far end is tied to that small movable pulley’s axle. So we must solve the right string first (it fixes the small pulley’s speed) and feed that into the left string.
Formula dL/dt = 0 for each inextensible string, all speeds in m s⁻²… in m s⁻¹. Rate of change of a segment between two points = (v⃗2 − v⃗1)·û1→2. Given: vA = 2 m s⁻¹ (right, away from its fixed pulley), vB = 2 m s⁻¹ (left, away from its fixed pulley). Both fixed pulleys are anchored to the “Fixed” brackets, so only the movable pulleys and the blocks change segment lengths.
Solution
1. Right string (A → fixed pulley → under the small movable pulley → back up to a fixed point). A moves right at 2 m s⁻¹, i.e. away from its pulley, so that segment lengthens at 2 m s⁻¹.
2. Two segments support the small movable pulley, so they must together shorten at 2 m s⁻¹: each shortens at 1 m s⁻¹ ⇒ the small pulley rises at vs = 1 m s⁻¹ upward.Classic 2:1 movable-pulley rule — the pulley moves at half the rate of the free end.
3. Left string: B → left fixed pulley → down → under the big pulley (carrying m) → up → tied to the small pulley’s axle. Let yp = depth of the big pulley and ys = depth of the small pulley below the fixed line. Then L = ℓB + yp + (yp − ys) = constant.
4. Differentiate: ḏB + 2ẏp − ẏs = 0. B moves left, away from its pulley, so ḏB = +2 m s⁻¹. The small pulley rises at 1 m s⁻¹, so ẏs = −1 m s⁻¹.
5. Substitute: 2 + 2ẏp − (−1) = 0 ⇒ 2ẏp = −3 ⇒ ẏp = −1.5 m s⁻¹.Negative ẏ means the depth is decreasing — the pulley (and m hanging from it) rises.
6. Block m hangs rigidly from the big pulley’s axle, so v = 1.5 m s⁻¹ upward.

                |                        |
                |                     (small movable P)  v_s = 1 m/s ↑
                |                        |
             (big movable P) -----------´
                   |
                  [m]  ↑ v = 1.5 m/s

  right string:  2 = 2·v_s          ⇒ v_s = 1 m/s up
  left  string:  2 + 2·ẏ_p - ẏ_s = 0 ⇒ ẏ_p = -1.5 m/s (up)
⚡ JEE Trick With nested pulleys, always solve outward-in: find the movable pulley that is driven by a single known free end first, then treat it as a known moving anchor for the next string. Trap: writing one global equation across two different strings — each string has its own conserved length and (in dynamics) its own tension. Signs: define one downward-positive depth per movable pulley and stick to it.
Answer: v = 1.5 m s⁻¹ upward.
4Massless strings, smooth pulleys, massless movable pulleys: 2m & m on a table, 3m hanging — acceleration of all blocksMovable-pulley constraintPulleys smooth & strings inextensible — find acceleration🟡 Similara(m) = 12g/17, a(2m) = 6g/17, a(3m) = 9g/17
Concept One string, one tension. The two table blocks 2m and m are the two free ends of a single string that dips through the table and wraps under a movable pulley carrying 3m. Because the pulley is massless, the same tension T acts on both of its segments, so it feels 2T upward. The kinematic link is the movable-pulley rule in reverse: the pulley’s acceleration is the average of the two end accelerations.
Formula Constraint: a1 + a2 = 2ap (m s⁻²). Blocks on the smooth table: T = 2m·a1 and T = m·a2 (N). Pulley + hanging block: 3mg − 2T = 3m·ap (N). The massless pulley transmits 2T; the string never stores force.
Solution
1. Write the length: L = s1 + yp + yp + s2, where s1, s2 are the table-top runs of the 2m and m blocks and yp is the pulley depth. Differentiating twice: a1 + a2 = 2ap.
2. Block 2m (smooth table, only T acts horizontally): T = 2m·a1 ⇒ a1 = T/(2m).
3. Block m: T = m·a2 ⇒ a2 = T/m.Same T, half the mass ⇒ the lighter block accelerates twice as fast.
4. Constraint gives ap = (a1+a2)/2 = ½(T/2m + T/m) = 3T/(4m).
5. Hanging block 3m with the pulley: 3mg − 2T = 3m × 3T/(4m) = 9T/4 ⇒ 3mg = 2T + 9T/4 = 17T/4 ⇒ T = 12mg/17.
6. Back-substitute: a1 = T/(2m) = 6g/17; a2 = T/m = 12g/17; ap = 3T/(4m) = 9g/17.Check the constraint: (6g/17 + 12g/17)/2 = 9g/17 ✓. All three are < g, as they must be.

Trap: writing 3mg − T (one tension) for the hanging block; a massless pulley doubles the tension to 2T. Sanity check: every acceleration must be smaller than g, and 6g/17 : 12g/17 = 1 : 2 mirrors the mass ratio 2m : m.

5Two cubes m₁, m₂ on frictionless slopes of block A on a table, joined by a string over a pulley; find horizontal a so m₁, m₂ don't movePseudo-force / non-slidingTwo cubes on frictionless slopes of a block — find a🟢 Exacta = [(m1 sinα + m2 sinβ) / (m1 cosα + m2 cosβ)] g
Concept Sit in the frame of the wedge: each cube then feels a backward pseudo-force ma in addition to gravity, the normal, and the string tension T. A cube on a frictionless face stays put only if the forces along that face cancel. The catch is that the two faces slope in opposite directions, so the same horizontal pseudo-force pushes one cube up its slope but the other down its slope — that is what makes T the same on both sides but the two along-slope balances different.
Formula Along each face (wedge frame, string pulls each cube up toward the apex pulley): left cube T = m1(g sinα + a cosα), right cube T = m2(g sinβ − a cosβ). On the left face the pseudo-force adds to gravity down-slope; on the right face it acts up-slope and subtracts. One string ⇒ one T, so the two right-hand sides are equal.
Solution
1. Left cube m1 (face angle α): gravity down-slope m1g sinα, pseudo-force down-slope m1a cosα, tension up-slope. Balance ⇒ T = m1(g sinα + a cosα).
2. Right cube m2 (face angle β): gravity down-slope m2g sinβ, but pseudo-force now points up-slope (m2a cosβ). Balance ⇒ T = m2(g sinβ − a cosβ).
3. Equate the tensions: m1g sinα + m1a cosα = m2g sinβ − m2a cosβ.
4. Collect a: a(m1cosα + m2cosβ) = g(m2sinβ − m1sinα) ⇒ a = g(m1sinα − m2sinβ)/(m1cosα + m2cosβ), directed toward the face whose block has the larger m·sinθ (here written for m1sinα > m2sinβ).The numerator is a difference, not a sum — the string couples the two cubes so their gravity pulls partly cancel.
5. Sanity check with a symmetric wedge (m1=m2, α=β): the formula gives a = 0, which is correct — by symmetry the string holds both cubes with T = mg sinα and no push is needed. A “sum” numerator would wrongly give g·tanα here.

   m1/  \m2             right face β: g sinβ DOWN , a cosβ  UP
    /____\
  T = m1(g sinα + a cosα) = m2(g sinβ - a cosβ)
  ⇒ a = g(m1 sinα - m2 sinβ)/(m1 cosα + m2 cosβ)   <-- answer

⚡ JEE Trick Always test a wedge-balance answer in the symmetric limit: equal masses and equal angles must need zero acceleration, which instantly rules out a “sum” numerator. Trap: writing both pseudo-force terms with the same sign — the faces slope opposite ways, so one is +a cosθ and the other −a cosθ.
Answer: a = g(m1sinα − m2sinβ)/(m1cosα + m2cosβ), toward the steeper-pull face. ⚠ Printed key says a = g(m1sinα + m2sinβ)/(m1cosα + m2cosβ) (a sum) — that fails the symmetric check; the correct numerator is a difference, see working above.

10Two smooth cylindrical bars 20√3 N each lying next to each other in contact, a similar third bar placed over them — minimum horizontal force on each lower bar to keep contactEquilibrium of stacked cylindersTwo smooth cylindrical bars weighing 20√3 N each lie next to each other in contact, a similar third bar🟢 ExactFmin = 10 N on each lower bar
Concept Three identical cylinders in a pile form an equilateral triangle of centres (each side = 2r), so every line of contact is at a known 60°. With friction neglected, every contact force lies along the line joining the two centres. The top bar wedges the lower two apart; the external F on each lower bar must supply exactly that spreading push. “Minimum F to keep them in contact” is the limiting case where the two lower bars just touch with zero mutual force.
Formula Top bar: 2N sin60° = W (N). Lower bar (horizontal): F = N cos60° + N₀ (N), where N₀ = the push from the neighbouring lower bar. Given W = 20√3 N per bar, sin60° = √3/2, cos60° = ½. Equal radii ⇒ centres 2r apart ⇒ the centre-to-centre lines from the top bar make 60° with the horizontal.
Solution
1. Geometry: all three radii are equal, so the three centres are 2r apart — an equilateral triangle. Each contact line from the top centre to a lower centre is at 60° to the horizontal.
2. Top bar, vertical equilibrium: the two normals N each have an upward component N sin60°, and they must carry its weight W = 20√3 N.

2. Differentiate twice in time: 2a1 = a2 + a3.
3. Regroup: a2 − a1 = a1 − a3.Move a1 to both sides: a2 + a3 − 2a1 = 0 ⇒ (a2−a1) = (a1−a3).
4. Interpretation: a1 is the arithmetic mean of a2 and a3 — the spring block's acceleration always sits exactly midway.
5. This is purely geometric, so it stays true for all spring extensions, including at the maximum extension x0 where all speeds momentarily vanish.
  string over movable pulley:  2 x1 = x2 + x3
  d^2/dt^2  ->  2 a1 = a2 + a3
  =>  a2 - a1 = a1 - a3   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick A movable pulley always carries the average of the two ends it links: 2amiddle = aleft + aright. Recognise it and the relation drops out in one line, no forces needed. Trap: chasing spring forces and masses to prove a relation that is pure kinematics — and note the equal spacing a2−a1 = a1−a3 is just 2a1=a2+a3 in disguise.
Answer: (c) a2 − a1 = a1 − a3.

11Person in a lift holds a water jar with a small hole; match the lift's state of motion with the jet distance (JEE Advanced 2014 Paper 2)Projectile in an accelerating frame (match)Person in a lift holding a water jar with a hole🟢 ExactcP-1, Q-1, R-1, S-4
Concept A jar of water with a small side hole is held in a lift. The water squirts out horizontally and lands some distance away. The exit speed follows Torricelli's law with the effective gravity of the lift, and the fall time also uses that same effective gravity. The beautiful cancellation: the horizontal range comes out independent of geff — so uniform motion or any non-zero acceleration gives the same landing distance; only free fall (geff = 0) breaks it.
Formula Efflux speed v = √(2 geff h); fall time from hole height y: t = √(2y/geff); range R = v t = 2√(h y). geff cancels between v and t, so R depends only on the geometry h, y. Free fall ⇒ geff = 0 ⇒ no efflux, R collapses.
Solution
1. In the lift frame the water feels geff = g ± a (up/down acceleration) or g (uniform velocity).
2. Speed out of the hole: v = √(2 geff h). Time to fall the drop height: t = √(2y/geff).
3. Range: R = v×t = √(2geffh)×√(2y/geff) = 2√(hy) — geff cancels.Whether the lift rises, descends, or coasts, R is the same.
4. (P) uniform velocity, (Q) accelerating up, (R) accelerating down (a < g): all have geff > 0 ⇒ same R ⇒ P→1, Q→1, R→1.
5. (S) free fall: geff = 0 ⇒ no pressure head, water does not shoot out ⇒ different outcome ⇒ S→4.
  R = sqrt(2 g_eff h) x sqrt(2y/g_eff) = 2 sqrt(h y)  (g_eff cancels)
  P up/uniform, Q accel up, R accel down: g_eff>0 -> R same -> 1
  S free fall: g_eff=0 -> no jet -> 4
  P-1, Q-1, R-1, S-4   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Horizontal range of a side-hole jet is independent of g — the same 2√(hy) on Earth, the Moon, or an accelerating lift. Trap: assuming a faster/upward lift throws the water farther; it does not, because higher geff boosts speed but shortens flight time in exact compensation. Free fall is the sole special case: zero effective gravity kills the efflux entirely.
Answer: (c) P-1, Q-1, R-1, S-4.

13Block 0.1 kg on a fixed incline PQ (angle θ) with a horizontal 1 N force through its CM — condition to remain stationary (g=10) (JEE Advanced 2012 Paper 1)Equilibrium on an incline with horizontal forceBlock 0.1 kg on incline PQ, horizontal 1 N — stationary🟢 Exacta, c(a) θ = 45° · (c) θ > 45° and friction acts on the block towards Q
Concept A block on a rough incline PQ (Q up-slope) is pushed by a horizontal 1 N force, while its weight is also mg = 1 N. Whether the block tends to slide up or down — and hence which way static friction points — is decided purely by the along-incline balance of the horizontal push against gravity. At one special angle the two exactly cancel and friction is not even needed. This is a sign-analysis question, not a numeric one.
Formula Along the incline (up-slope positive): net driving force F∥ = F cosθ − mg sinθ, with F = mg = 1 N. A horizontal force has up-slope component F cosθ; gravity has down-slope component mg sinθ. Friction opposes the tendency: it points down-slope (toward P) if F∥ > 0, up-slope (toward Q) if F∥ < 0.
Solution
1. Since F = mg, F∥ = mg(cosθ − sinθ). Its sign is set by cosθ vs sinθ.
2. At θ = 45°: cos45° = sin45° ⇒ F∥ = 0. The block needs no friction along the incline ⇒ it stays stationary. Option (a) is correct.Perpendicular balance just sets N; nothing to resist along-slope.
3. For θ > 45°: sinθ > cosθ ⇒ F∥ < 0, gravity wins, block tends to slide down toward P ⇒ friction acts up-slope, i.e. toward Q. Option (c) is correct.
4. For θ < 45°: cosθ > sinθ ⇒ F∥ > 0, block tends to slide up, friction acts down toward P — so any option claiming “toward Q for θ < 45°” is false.
5. Hence the correct statements are (a) and (c); the others reverse the friction direction.
  F|| = mg(cos t - sin t),  F = mg = 1 N
  t = 45 : F|| = 0        -> no friction, stationary   (a)
  t > 45 : F|| < 0 (down) -> friction up, toward Q     (c)
  t < 45 : F|| > 0 (up)   -> friction down, toward P
⚡ JEE Trick When the horizontal push equals the weight, the whole problem collapses to comparing cosθ with sinθ — θ = 45° is the neutral point where friction vanishes. Trap: assuming the block always slides down so friction is always up toward Q; below 45° the horizontal force actually drives it up-slope and flips friction toward P.
Answer: (a) θ = 45° (stationary, zero friction) and (c) θ > 45° with friction toward Q.
