1Thin uniform tube, trapped air columns 46 cm and 44.5 cm above and below a 5 cm mercury pellet, held at 60° to the vertical; laid horizontal the columns become equal. T = 27°C. Find the pressure of the air columns when horizontal (cm Hg)Boyle's law + mercury pelletA thin tube of uniform cross section has trapped air columns of length...🟢 Exactd75.4 cm
Concept A mercury pellet trapped between two gas columns is a pressure-difference device: the pellet is in equilibrium, so the two gas pressures differ by exactly the weight-component of the pellet along the tube. Tilt the tube and that component changes as cosθ of the angle from the vertical; lay it horizontal and it vanishes, so both columns end at the same pressure. Each column is separately isothermal, so Boyle's law links its tilted state to its horizontal state. Two Boyle equations + one force balance = three equations, three unknowns.
Formula Pellet balance along the tube: P₂ = P₁ + Lpellet cosθ (cm Hg, with θ measured from the vertical). Boyle for each column: P₁ℓ₁ = Pℓ and P₂ℓ₂ = Pℓ (cm Hg × cm). Data: ℓ₁ = 46 cm (upper), ℓ₂ = 44.5 cm (lower), Lpellet = 5 cm, θ = 60°, T constant at 27°C. Working in cm Hg lets the pellet length double as a pressure — no need for ρg at all. Temperature never enters: it is the same throughout, so Boyle (not the full gas law) is enough.
Solution
1. Total trapped gas length is fixed: 46 + 44.5 = 90.5 cm. Laid horizontal the columns are equal, so each becomes ℓ = 90.5/2 = 45.25 cm. The pellet has fixed length, so the two gas columns can only trade length with each other.
2. Weight-component of the pellet along the tube at 60° from the vertical: 5 × cos60° = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 cm Hg. So the lower column is at the higher pressure: P₂ = P₁ + 2.5.
3. Boyle on the upper (46 cm) column: P₁ × 46 = P × 45.25 ⇒ P₁ = 45.25P/46.
4. Boyle on the lower (44.5 cm) column: P₂ × 44.5 = P × 45.25 ⇒ P₂ = 45.25P/44.5.
5. Substitute into the pellet balance: 45.25P/44.5 − 45.25P/46 = 2.5.
6. Factor: 45.25P × (1/44.5 − 1/46) = 2.5. Now 1/44.5 = 0.0224719 and 1/46 = 0.0217391, so the bracket = 0.0007328.
7. Hence 45.25P × 0.0007328 = 2.5 ⇒ 0.033160·P = 2.5 ⇒ P = 75.39 ≈ 75.4 cm Hg. Check: P₁ = 45.25×75.39/46 = 74.16 and P₂ = 45.25×75.39/44.5 = 76.66; difference = 2.50 ✓ exactly the pellet term.
   TILTED 60° from vertical            HORIZONTAL
     upper 46 cm  @ P1 = 74.16          45.25 cm @ P
       [pellet 5 cm]                    [pellet 5 cm]
     lower 44.5cm @ P2 = 76.66          45.25 cm @ P

   pellet: P2 - P1 = 5cos60 = 2.5 cm Hg      (tilted)
           P2 - P1 = 0                       (horizontal)

   Boyle:  74.16 x 46   = 3411 = 75.39 x 45.25  ✓
           76.66 x 44.5 = 3411 = 75.39 x 45.25  ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Measure the tilt angle from the vertical and the pellet term is L cosθ; measure it from the horizontal and it is L sinθ. Getting this backwards here would give 5×cos30° = 4.33 and P ≈ 130 cm — not an option, which is your warning bell. Second trap: the two columns are not 45.25 cm because of symmetry — they are equal only because the problem says so, which is what pins the total length.
Answer: (d) 75.4 cm.

Concept Water leaves the body only as the extra vapour the lungs add: air comes in carrying 5 mm Hg of water vapour and goes out saturated at 45 mm Hg. The net water exported per breath therefore corresponds to a partial-pressure rise of Δp = 40 mm Hg. Treat that vapour as an ideal gas occupying the whole 10,000 L of air processed per day, convert to moles, and multiply by 18.
Formula n = Δp·V / (R T) with Δp in atm, V in L, R = 0.0821 L atm K−1 mol−1, T in K; then m = n × M (g), M(H₂O) = 18 g mol−1. Data: Δp = 45 − 5 = 40 mm Hg, V = 10,000 L day−1, T = 310 K. Only the difference matters — the 5 mm Hg that came in also goes back out, so it carries no net water.
Solution
1. Net water-vapour partial pressure added: Δp = 45 − 5 = 40 mm Hg.
2. Convert to atm: 40/760 = 0.052632 atm.
3. Numerator Δp·V = 0.052632 × 10,000 = 526.32 L atm.
4. Denominator RT = 0.0821 × 310 = 25.451 L atm mol−1.
5. Moles of water exhaled per day: n = 526.32/25.451 = 20.68 mol. Note 20.68 is itself printed as option (a) — the classic trap of stopping at moles instead of grams.
6. Mass = 20.68 × 18 = 372.2 g ≈ 372.23 g per day. Sanity: about 0.37 kg of water a day lost purely through breathing — physiologically about right.
   in : 10,000 L air carrying  5 mm Hg H2O
   out: 10,000 L air carrying 45 mm Hg H2O (saturated @310 K)
   --------------------------------------------------

   n = (0.05263 x 10000) / (0.0821 x 310) = 526.3/25.45 = 20.68 mol
   m = 20.68 x 18 = 372.2 g/day
⚡ JEE Trick The distractors here are all “one step off”: (a) 20.68 g is the mole count mislabelled as grams; (c) 418.76 g uses the full 45 mm Hg instead of the 40 mm Hg difference (45/40 × 372.2 = 418.8); (d) 46.53 g uses only the 5 mm Hg. Always ask which pressure does net work — here it is the difference.

31 g of ideal gas A in an evacuated vessel at 300 K gives 1 atm. Adding 2 g of ideal gas B raises the total to 1.5 atm. Find the ratio of the average speeds of A and B at the same temperatureMolecular speeds + Dalton's lawWhen 1 g of an ideal gas A is introduced into an evacuated vessel at 300 K, the pressure was found to be...🟢 Exacta2 : 1
Concept Two ideas bolt together. (1) At fixed V and T, pressure is a direct proxy for moles, so the partial pressures hand you nA : nB for free. (2) Knowing the masses too, the mole ratio converts into a molar-mass ratio. Finally every molecular speed — average, rms, most probable — scales as 1/√M, so the speed ratio is the inverse square root of the molar-mass ratio. Which speed is asked never matters for a ratio: the numerical prefactors cancel.
Formula Dalton at fixed V, T: Pi ∝ ni. Moles: n = w/M. Average speed: uav = √(8RT/πM) ⇒ uA/uB = √(MB/MA) at the same T. Data: wA = 1 g, wB = 2 g, PA = 1 atm, Ptotal = 1.5 atm.
Solution
1. B's partial pressure is what the total gained: PB = 1.5 − 1 = 0.5 atm. A is unaffected by adding B — ideal gases ignore each other, which is exactly Dalton's law.
2. Same V and T, so nA/nB = PA/PB = 1/0.5 = 2.
3. Write the moles from the masses: nA = 1/MA and nB = 2/MB.
4. Impose the ratio: (1/MA) ÷ (2/MB) = 2 ⇒ MB/(2MA) = 2 ⇒ MB = 4 MA.
5. Speeds: uA/uB = √(MB/MA) = √4 = 2, i.e. 2 : 1. B is 4× heavier per molecule, so it moves √4 = 2× slower. Heavier ⇒ slower, always.
   A: 1 g -> 1.0 atm          n_A / n_B = 1.0/0.5 = 2
   B: 2 g -> 0.5 atm          (same V, same T)

   n_A = 1/M_A ,  n_B = 2/M_B
   (1/M_A)/(2/M_B) = 2   =>  M_B = 4 M_A

   u_A/u_B = sqrt(M_B/M_A) = sqrt(4) = 2      =>  2 : 1
⚡ JEE Trick Do not compute MA and MB separately — the data cannot give them, only their ratio, and the question only wants a ratio. Trap: reading “1.5 atm” as B's own pressure (that would give MB = 3MA and the non-option √3 : 1). Second trap: inverting to 1 : 2 — option (b) is sitting there waiting. Lighter gas = faster.
Answer: (a) 2 : 1.
4For the equation of state P(V − nb) = nRT, with Vm = 10b, find the compressibility factor Z of the gasCompressibility factorIf Z is the compressibility factor, van der Waals' equation at low pressure…🟡 Similara10/9
Concept This is the van der Waals equation with the attraction term switched off — only the excluded-volume correction b survives. Physically that means the molecules take up room but do not attract, so the gas is harder to compress than ideal and Z must come out greater than 1. Knowing that sign in advance kills two of the four options before any algebra.
Formula Definition: Z = PVm/(RT) (dimensionless). Given (per mole, n = 1): P(Vm − b) = RT. Condition: Vm = 10b. Always reduce to a per-mole equation first: Z is defined with molar volume, so carrying n around only invites slips.
Solution
1. Take n = 1 mol, so the state equation is P(Vm − b) = RT.
2. Solve for P: P = RT/(Vm − b).
3. Substitute Vm = 10b: Vm − b = 10b − b = 9b, so P = RT/(9b).
4. Feed into the definition: Z = PVm/(RT) = [RT/(9b)] × 10b / (RT).
5. RT cancels top and bottom, and b cancels: Z = 10b/(9b) = 10/9. Z = 1.11 > 1 ✓ — exactly the repulsion-dominated behaviour we predicted, so the answer is self-consistent.
   P(Vm - b) = RT          with Vm = 10b

   P = RT/(10b - b) = RT/(9b)

   Z = P.Vm/RT = [RT/(9b)] . (10b) / RT
     = 10b / 9b
     = 10/9  = 1.11  ( > 1  ✓ repulsion only )
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the general result for this “b-only” gas: Z = Vm/(Vm − b), which is always > 1. So if Vm = 10b, Z = 10/9 instantly; if Vm = 5b, Z = 5/4; and so on. The distractors 9/11 and 10/11 are both < 1 — physically impossible here — and 11/10 is the answer you get if you sloppily write Vm + b instead of Vm − b.
Answer: (a) 10/9.

The distractors are crude multiples: (c) 2.46 atm is exactly 2× (using Vm = 10 by forgetting M = 44), and (d) 0.64 atm is about half. 6Which of the following statement(s) is/are true? (a) rise in Z with increasing pressure is due to b; (b) average velocity of ideal-gas molecules moving in only one dimension is zero; (c) the speed-distribution fraction for molar mass M at T equals that for molar mass M/2 at T/2; (d) near critical conditions gases behave ideallyReal gases + Maxwell distributionApplicability of the van der Waals equation & compressibility factor (JEE/NEET)🟡 Similara, b, c(a) rise in Z is due to b · (b) average velocity in one dimension is zero · (c) M at T behaves as M/2 at T/2
Concept A four-way true/false sweep across the whole chapter. The single most useful lever is that the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution depends on M and T only through the ratio M/T — so any pair (M, T) with the same quotient gives an identical speed distribution. The other three test sign conventions and definitions.
Formula Maxwell–Boltzmann: f(u) = 4π(M/2πRT)3/2 u2 e−Mu2/2RT — every occurrence of M is divided by T. Compressibility: Z = 1 + Pb/RT − a/(RTVm). Average velocity (a vector) vs average speed (a magnitude): ⟨v⃗⟩ = 0 but ⟨|v|⟩ > 0.
Solution
1. (a) TRUE. Write Z = 1 + Pb/RT − a/(RTVm). As P climbs, the +Pb/RT term grows without bound while the attraction term dies away, so Z rises above 1 and keeps rising. That rise is owned entirely by the excluded-volume constant b. This is why all gases have Z > 1 at sufficiently high pressure — molecules cannot be squeezed into zero volume.
2. (b) TRUE. “Average velocity” is the average of a vector. In one dimension, at equilibrium exactly as many molecules head +x as −x with the same speed distribution, so the components cancel: ⟨vx⟩ = 0. (The average speed ⟨|vx|⟩ is not zero — different quantity.) If ⟨v⃗⟩ were non-zero the gas as a whole would be drifting, i.e. wind, not equilibrium.
3. (c) TRUE. In f(u), M and T appear only as M/T. For the first gas the ratio is M/T. For the second it is (M/2)/(T/2) = M/T — identical. Same M/T ⇒ same distribution ⇒ the same fraction of molecules in every window u to u+du.
4. (d) FALSE. The critical point is precisely where a gas is on the verge of condensing — intermolecular attraction is at its most influential and Zc = 3/8 = 0.375, nowhere near 1. Gases behave ideally at low pressure and high temperature, the opposite corner. Zc = 3/8 for every van der Waals gas — a 62% deviation from ideality. “Near critical” is the worst place to assume ideal behaviour.
  (a) Z = 1 + Pb/RT - a/(RT.Vm)
          \_______/   high P: this term dominates -> Z rises   TRUE

  (b) <v_x> = 0   (vector, cancels)      TRUE
      <|v_x|> != 0  (speed -- different quantity)

  (c) f(u) depends on M/T only:
        gas 1:  M   / T   = M/T
        gas 2: (M/2)/(T/2)= M/T   -> identical distribution  TRUE

  (d) critical point: Zc = 3/8 = 0.375, NOT 1              FALSE
⚡ JEE Trick The M/T invariance in (c) is a gift: halve both M and T and nothing changes; double M and double T and nothing changes. Examiners love dressing this up. And keep “velocity” (vector, averages to zero) firmly apart from “speed” (magnitude, averages to √(8RT/πM)) — statement (b) is pure vocabulary.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).

Formula Step 1: P = Z R T / Vm (atm). Step 2: (P + a/Vm2)(Vm − b) = RT, solved for a (atm L2 mol−2). Data: Z = 0.8, Vm = 0.5 L mol−1, T = 300 K, b = 0.04 L mol−1, R = 0.08. The problem hands you R = 0.08 (not 0.0821) — use exactly what is given, or the arithmetic will drift off the printed options.
Solution
1. RT = 0.08 × 300 = 24 L atm mol−1.
2. Actual pressure from Z: P = Z·RT/Vm = 0.8 × 24/0.5 = 19.2/0.5 = 38.4 atm. Z = 0.8 < 1 means attraction dominates, so the real pressure sits below the ideal 48 atm ✓.
3. Volume correction: Vm − b = 0.5 − 0.04 = 0.46 L mol−1.
4. Pressure correction: a/Vm2 = a/(0.5)2 = a/0.25 = 4a.
5. Plug into van der Waals: (38.4 + 4a)(0.46) = 24.
6. Divide by 0.46: 38.4 + 4a = 24/0.46 = 52.174.
7. Isolate: 4a = 52.174 − 38.4 = 13.774 ⇒ a = 3.443 ≈ 3.44 atm L2 mol−2.

Formula The three critical constants: Vc = 3b, Tc = 8a/(27Rb), Pc = a/(27b2). Substitute these into each candidate and simplify; the expression is correct iff the result is exactly a. Useful corollary to keep handy: Zc = PcVc/RTc = 3/8 for every van der Waals gas.

Concept The van der Waals equation carries two competing corrections, and almost every conceptual question about it is really asking which correction survives in a given limit. 2. (b) FALSE. The 1/r3 law belongs to two stationary dipoles held in fixed orientation. The word “rotating” is doing all the work. Formula For any distribution: ump = the peak; uav = ⟨u⟩ = the mean; urms = √⟨u2⟩. For a symmetric distribution, peak = mean ⇒ ump = uav. Always: urms ≥ uav by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, with equality only if every molecule had identical speed.

So the first two entries of the ratio are equal ⇒ the ratio starts 1 : 1. 28Consider the following compounds in the liquid form: O2, HF, H2O, NH3, H2O2, CCl4, CHCl3, C6H6, C6H5Cl. When a charged comb is brought near their flowing stream, how many of them show deflection as per the figure? (JEE Adv. 2020 (Paper-2), Level-2)Polarity → deflection by a charged combConsider the following compounds in the liquid form: O2, HF, H2O, NH3, H2O2, CCl4, CHCl3, C6H6, C6H5Cl...🟢 Exact6
Concept A charged comb deflects a liquid stream only if the molecules carry a permanent dipole moment — polar molecules align with the field and are pulled towards the comb. So the question reduces to a straight polarity audit of nine molecules. The decisions hinge on molecular geometry: symmetry can cancel individually polar bonds, leaving a nonpolar molecule overall.
Formula Deflects ⇔ μ ≠ 0. Test each molecule: are the bonds polar, and does the geometry let the bond dipoles cancel?
Solution
1. O2 — NONPOLAR. A homonuclear diatomic; identical atoms mean zero electronegativity difference and no bond dipole at all. No deflection.
2. HF — POLAR. Diatomic with a huge electronegativity gap (F = 4.0, H = 2.1). Nothing to cancel it. μ = 1.86 D ⇒ deflects ✓
3. H2O — POLAR. Bent geometry (104.5°), so the two O–H dipoles add rather than cancel. μ = 1.85 D ⇒ deflects ✓
4. NH3 — POLAR. Trigonal pyramidal with a lone pair on top; the three N–H dipoles plus the lone pair give a net moment. μ = 1.47 D ⇒ deflects ✓
5. H2O2 — POLAR. Its open-book (non-planar, skew) shape prevents the two O–H dipoles from cancelling. μ = 2.26 D ⇒ deflects ✓ If H2O2 were planar and anti, the dipoles would cancel exactly. Its ~111° dihedral is what saves it — a classic examiner favourite.
6. CCl4 — NONPOLAR. Four polar C–Cl bonds arranged in a perfectly symmetric tetrahedron: the four bond dipoles cancel vectorially to zero. μ = 0 ⇒ no deflection.
7. CHCl3 — POLAR. Replace one Cl of CCl4 with H and the symmetry is broken; the three C–Cl dipoles no longer cancel against the lone C–H. μ = 1.04 D ⇒ deflects ✓ The CCl4/CHCl3 pair is the sharpest test in the list: identical bond types, opposite answers, purely because of symmetry.
8. C6H6 — NONPOLAR. Benzene is a regular planar hexagon; every C–H dipole is cancelled by the one directly opposite. μ = 0 ⇒ no deflection.
9. C6H5Cl — POLAR. Chlorobenzene has one Cl breaking the hexagonal symmetry, so there is nothing opposite to cancel the C–Cl dipole. μ = 1.69 D ⇒ deflects ✓
10. Count the deflectors: HF, H2O, NH3, H2O2, CHCl3, C6H5Cl = 6.

⚡ JEE Trick Polar bonds ≠ polar molecule. CCl4 and C6H6 are stuffed with polar bonds yet have μ = 0 because their geometry is perfectly symmetric — those two are the intended traps, and each one you wrongly include pushes your count to 7 or 8. Rule of thumb: full symmetry (tetrahedral CX4, linear, planar hexagonal) ⇒ nonpolar; break the symmetry with even one different atom ⇒ polar. And do not forget H2O2: it looks like it might cancel, but its skew dihedral keeps it firmly polar.
Answer: 6.
