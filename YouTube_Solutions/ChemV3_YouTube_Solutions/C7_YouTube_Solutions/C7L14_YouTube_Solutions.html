1 (i)Fill in the blank: The maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a solvent at a given temperature is known as ______.Definition of solubilityWhat Is Solubility and Molar Solubility - Chemical Equilibrium - Chemistry Class 11🟡 Similarsolubility
Concept The word wanted is the name of the limiting quantity itself. When solute is added to a solvent at fixed temperature, it dissolves only up to a ceiling; beyond that, extra solid just sits at the bottom in dynamic equilibrium with the dissolved part. That ceiling is called the solubility. The trap is to write “solubility product”, which is an equilibrium constant, not an amount.
Key relation solubility = maximum amount of solute dissolved per fixed amount of solvent at a given T (saturated solution) The phrase “maximum amount … at a given temperature” is the textbook definition word for word.
Solution
1. Read the stem: it asks for the name of the maximum amount of solute that dissolves at a given temperature.
2. A solution holding exactly that maximum is saturated: solid ⇌ dissolved solute is at equilibrium, so the dissolved amount cannot rise further.
3. The amount dissolved in a saturated solution is defined as the solubility of the solute in that solvent at that temperature. It is usually quoted in g L−1 or g per 100 g of solvent.
4. “Temperature” appears because solubility changes with T; most solids dissolve more on heating, so the value is always quoted with T.
5. Rule out the near-misses: molar solubility is the same idea in mol L−1; solubility product Ksp is a product of ion concentrations, not an amount.
6. Example from the lecture: AgCl, Ksp = 1.5625 × 10−10 gives S = 1.25 × 10−5 mol L−1, and 143.5 × 1.25 × 10−5 = 1.79 × 10−3 g L−1 is its solubility.
7. So the blank is filled by solubility.

   molar solubility  : same, in mol/L
   Ksp               : product of ion conc.

2. “Molar” in chemistry always signals mol L−1 (molarity), so the fixed quantity is one litre.
3. Its unit is therefore mol L−1, and it is the S used in Ksp = (xS)x(yS)y. Ksp is written with molar concentrations, so S must be molar too.

6. For a dilute aqueous solution, one litre of solution ≈ one litre of solvent, which is why the book’s wording “litre of the solvent” is acceptable.
7. So the blank is liter (litre).

Concept Ksp is defined only for sparingly (slightly) soluble ionic salts. For such a salt the saturated solution is so dilute that ion activities ≈ concentrations, and the solid’s activity is 1, so the equilibrium constant reduces to a plain product of ion concentrations. The trap is to think of “slightly ionised”: these salts are fully ionised, just barely dissolved.

Solution
1. The stem calls Ksp the equilibrium constant for the solubility equilibrium of a slightly ______ ionic compound.
2. A solubility equilibrium needs undissolved solid in contact with its ions, i.e. a saturated solution of a salt that dissolves only a little.

1 (iv)Fill in the blank: If the ionic product of a salt is less than the solubility product, the solution is ______.Ionic product vs KspSolubility Product vs Ionic Product| Precipitate Formation|Ksp vs IP|#Chemistrycubicle🟡 Similarunsaturated
Concept Compare the ionic product Qsp (same expression as Ksp, but with the current concentrations) with Ksp. If Qsp < Ksp, the solution can still dissolve more solid, so it is unsaturated. The trap is mixing up the direction: precipitation needs Qsp > Ksp.
Key relation Qsp < Ksp: unsaturated; Qsp = Ksp: saturated; Qsp > Ksp: supersaturated, precipitates This is the usual Q-versus-K test applied to a dissolution equilibrium.
Solution
1. Ionic product of a salt AB: Qsp = [A+][B−] at any moment, not necessarily at equilibrium.
2. At equilibrium (saturated solution), Qsp = Ksp exactly.
3. If Qsp < Ksp, the reaction AB(s) → A+ + B− still runs forward, so more solid can dissolve. Q < K always drives a reaction toward products.
4. A solution that can dissolve more solute is by definition unsaturated, and no precipitate forms.
5. Numerical check with AgCl: mix to give [Ag+] = [Cl−] = 10−6 M; Qsp = 10−6 × 10−6 = 10−12 < 1.56 × 10−10, so it is unsaturated and stays clear.
6. If instead Qsp > Ksp, the excess ions precipitate until Qsp falls back to Ksp.
7. So the blank is unsaturated.
 Qsp < Ksp  -> unsaturated (more dissolves)
 Qsp = Ksp  -> saturated
 Qsp > Ksp  -> precipitation

 AgCl, [Ag+] = [Cl-] = 1e-6 M
 Qsp = 1e-6 x 1e-6 = 1e-12
 Ksp = 1.56e-10 ; Qsp < Ksp
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = unsaturated               <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember “Q above K, precipitate”. In precipitation questions, always dilute both solutions first (concentrations change on mixing), then compute Qsp. Forgetting the dilution is the classic trap that flips the verdict near the boundary.
Answer: unsaturated.
2 (i)True or false: Solubility and molar solubility are the same concept.Solubility vs molar solubilityWhat Is Solubility and Molar Solubility - Chemical Equilibrium - Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept Solubility and molar solubility describe the same saturated solution but are different quantities: solubility is usually a mass per volume (g L−1) or per 100 g of solvent, while molar solubility is in mol L−1. They differ by the molar mass, so the statement that they are “the same concept” is false.
Key relation solubility (g L−1) = molar solubility S (mol L−1) × M (g mol−1) Only molar solubility enters Ksp expressions; mass solubility must be converted first.
Solution
1. Solubility: the maximum amount (mass) of solute that dissolves in a given quantity of solvent at a given temperature; units g L−1 or g/100 g.
2. Molar solubility: the maximum number of moles that dissolve per litre of solution; units mol L−1.
3. They are linked by molar mass, not equal. Numerically they coincide only if M = 1 g mol−1, which never happens for a salt.
4. AgCl example: S = √(1.5625 × 10−10) = 1.25 × 10−5 mol L−1.
5. Its solubility = S × M = 1.25 × 10−5 × 143.5 = 1.79 × 10−3 g L−1, a number about 143 times larger.
6. Ksp = S2 works only with the molar value; using 1.79 × 10−3 would give a Ksp wrong by a factor of 143.52.
7. Hence the two are distinct (though related) quantities: the statement is False.

2 (ii)True or false: The solubility product remains constant with changes in temperature.Ksp temperature dependenceSolubility & Solubility Product - Equilibrium | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6 | CBSE🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept Ksp is an equilibrium constant, and every equilibrium constant depends on temperature through the van’t Hoff relation. Dissolution of most salts is endothermic, so Ksp usually rises on heating. A statement that Ksp stays constant when T changes is therefore false; it is constant only at a fixed temperature.
Key relation log(K2/K1) = (ΔH°/2.303R)(1/T1 − 1/T2) For ΔH°soln > 0, T2 > T1 gives K2 > K1.
Solution
1. Ksp is the equilibrium constant of AxBy(s) ⇌ xAy+ + yBx−.
2. An equilibrium constant changes only with temperature; it does not change with concentration, pressure or catalyst.
3. The van’t Hoff equation shows K changes with T whenever ΔH° ≠ 0. Dissolution always has some enthalpy change (lattice energy vs hydration energy).
4. For most sparingly soluble salts (AgCl, BaSO4, PbCl2) dissolution is endothermic, so Ksp increases with T.
5. Example: PbCl2 is noticeably more soluble in hot water, which is why it is separated from AgCl in qualitative analysis by boiling.
6. So Ksp is constant only at a given temperature, and must always be quoted with T (e.g. “at 25 °C”).
7. Hence the statement is False.

2 (v)True or false: The solubility of a solute does not depend on the nature of the solvent.Solubility depends on solventFactors Affecting Solubility Nature Of Solute & Solvent Class - 12 | Chemistry | NEET JEE AIIMS🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept Solubility depends strongly on the solvent: “like dissolves like”. Ionic and polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents (high dielectric constant, strong ion–dipole hydration), while non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents. So saying solubility does not depend on the nature of the solvent is false.
Key relation dissolution occurs when solvation energy ≳ lattice energy; Coulomb force between ions ∝ 1/εr A high-dielectric solvent like water (εr ≈ 80) weakens ion–ion attraction about 80 times.
Solution
1. Solubility of a solute is the result of the competition between solute–solute forces (lattice energy) and solute–solvent forces (solvation energy).
2. Solvation energy depends on the solvent: its polarity, dielectric constant and ability to H-bond.
3. Water (εr ≈ 80) hydrates ions strongly, so NaCl dissolves about 36 g per 100 g of water. Ion–dipole attraction pays for breaking the lattice.
4. In benzene or CCl4 (εr ≈ 2) NaCl is practically insoluble, because there is no ion–dipole stabilisation.
5. Conversely, I2 and naphthalene dissolve readily in CCl4 but hardly in water.
6. So the same solute has very different solubilities in different solvents: solubility is a property of the solute–solvent pair at a given T.
7. Hence the statement is False.

Concept Fe(OH)3 releases OH− on dissolving, so its solubility depends strongly on [OH−] (common-ion effect). Adding acid (lowering pH) neutralises OH− and pulls the equilibrium towards dissolution; raising pH does the opposite. The Ksp value itself is fixed at 298 K.
Key relation Fe(OH)3 ⇄ Fe3+ + 3OH−; [Fe3+] = Ksp/[OH−]3 At fixed Ksp, the allowed [Fe3+] rises steeply (cube) as [OH−] falls.
Solution
1. Ksp = [Fe3+][OH−]3 = 6×10−38, so [Fe3+] = 6×10−38/[OH−]3.
2. At pH 7, [OH−] = 10−7 and [Fe3+] = 6×10−38/10−21 = 6×10−17 M.
3. At pH 3, [OH−] = 10−11 and [Fe3+] = 6×10−38/10−33 = 6×10−5 M, a 1012-fold rise. Decreasing pH increases solubility: (c).
4. (a) Increasing pH raises [OH−], a common ion, so solubility falls. Wrong.
5. (b) pH 7.0 is just one fixed condition, not a change that increases solubility. Wrong.
6. (d) Exposure to air adds nothing to Fe(III) (already fully oxidised); any slight CO2 uptake would act only by lowering pH, which is option (c) itself. So (c) is the intended single answer.
