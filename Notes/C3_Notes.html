L-1

Mole Concept — Atomic, Molecular & Formula Mass

  • Atomic mass = how many times an atom is heavier than 1/12 of a 12C atom; measured on the amu (u) scale.
  • 1 amu = 1.66056 × 10⁻²⁴ g; 12C is the standard because most elements then come out near whole numbers.
  • Average atomic mass = abundance-weighted mean of the isotope masses (usually fractional).
  • Molecular mass = Σ atomic masses in a molecule; formula mass is used for ionic solids (no discrete molecules).
  • Expressed in grams these become gram atomic / gram molecular / gram formula mass.
average atomic massĀ = ΣPiAi / 100 (Pi = % abundance, Ai = isotopic mass)
Actual mass of an atom vs gram atomic mass
The actual mass of one atom is tiny — one O atom = 2.66 × 10⁻²³ g — while the gram atomic mass of oxygen is 16 g (the mass of a whole mole, NA atoms). Never confuse "mass of one atom" with "gram atom".
⚠ TrapAverage atomic mass is a weighted average, not a simple average. For N (99.33% ¹⁴N, 0.67% ¹⁵N) it is 14.007 u — very close to 14, not 14.5.

Book Example · L-1
If 1/6 (in place of 1/12) of the mass of a carbon atom is taken as the relative atomic mass unit, what is the effect on the mass of one mole of a substance?
New amu = 2 × old amu. Mass of 1 mole = amu × NA, but NA now becomes NA/2.
Mass of 1 mole = (2 amu) × (NA/2) = amu × NA.
Mass of one mole remains unchanged.
Book Example · L-1
If 1/24 of C-12 is considered as 1 amu, what is the change in the atomic mass of iron? (Present-scale Fe = 56)
1/24 mass of C = ½ amu, so 1 amu is now smaller by a factor of 2.
Atomic mass = (mass of atom)/(new amu) = (mass of atom)/(½ amu) = 2 × old value.
Atomic mass doubles: 56 × 2 = 112 amu.
Book Example · L-1
Calculate the average atomic mass of naturally occurring argon: ³⁶Ar (36 g, 0.34%), ³⁸Ar (38 g, 0.06%), ⁴⁰Ar (40 g, 99.6%).
Ā = [(36 × 0.34) + (38 × 0.06) + (40 × 99.6)] / 100 = (12.24 + 2.28 + 3984)/100 = 3998.52/100.
≈ 39.99 g mol⁻¹
Book Example · L-1
Boron occurs as two isotopes of atomic mass 10 and 11. If the average atomic mass is 10.8, find the % abundance of each.
Let %B¹⁰ = x, %B¹¹ = 100 − x. Then [10x + 11(100 − x)]/100 = 10.8.
10x + 1100 − 11x = 1080 ⇒ −x = −20 ⇒ x = 20.
B¹⁰ = 20 %, B¹¹ = 80 %
L-2

Application of the Mole Concept

  • A mole (Ostwald) = amount of substance containing as many entities as atoms in exactly 12 g of 12C = 6.022 × 10²³ entities.
  • Works for atoms, molecules and ionic formula units alike (1 mol NaCl = NA Na⁺ + NA Cl⁻).
  • Molar mass (g) is numerically equal to the atomic / molecular / formula mass in u.
the master conversionsn = w/M · N (entities) = n × NA · 1 mol gas = 22.4 L at NTP
counting sub-particlesNo. of electrons = (w/M) × NA × (e⁻ per molecule) [same for p, n]
💡 TipTo find atoms/electrons: get moles first, multiply by NA, then by the count of that particle in one formula unit.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-2
Calculate the mass of 100 atoms of magnesium in grams. (Mg = 24)
6.022 × 10²³ atoms weigh 24 g ⇒ 1 atom = 24/(6.022 × 10²³) g.
Mass of 100 atoms = [24/(6.022 × 10²³)] × 100.
≈ 3.985 × 10⁻²¹ g
Book Example · L-2
Calculate the number of molecules and atoms in 3.6 g of H₂O.
n = 3.6/18 = 0.2 mol ⇒ molecules = 0.2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.2044 × 10²³.
Each H₂O has 3 atoms ⇒ total atoms = 1.2044 × 10²³ × 3.
1.2044 × 10²³ molecules; 3.6132 × 10²³ atoms
Book Example · L-2
Calculate the number of electrons in 1 g of CaCO₃.
Molecules in 1 g = (1 × 6.022 × 10²³)/100 = 6.022 × 10²¹.
Electrons per CaCO₃ = 20 + 6 + 24 = 50 ⇒ total = 6.022 × 10²¹ × 50.
≈ 3.0118 × 10²³ electrons (301.18 × 10²¹)
L-3

Determination of Atomic Mass · Vapour Density · % Composition

Determining atomic mass

  • Dulong & Petit's law: atomic mass × specific heat ≈ 6.4 (approx, for solid metals). Not valid for light elements (B, C, Si).
  • Cannizzaro's method: atomic mass = the smallest mass of the element found in a molecular mass of any of its compounds.
  • Law of isomorphism: isomorphous compounds (e.g. MgSO₄·7H₂O, ZnSO₄·7H₂O, FeSO₄·7H₂O) have the same crystal shape and elements of the same valency.
Dulong–PetitAtomic mass ≈ 6.4 / specific heat · Atomic mass = Equivalent weight × valency

Mole–volume & vapour density

  • Avogadro's hypothesis: equal volumes of all gases (same T, P) contain equal numbers of molecules.
  • Vapour density (V.D.) = density of gas / density of H₂ (same T, P).
vapour densityMolecular mass = 2 × V.D. · Density at STP = (Molar mass)/22.4 L
percentage composition% of element = (mass of that element / molar mass) × 100
⚠ TrapDulong–Petit gives only an approximate atomic mass; the exact value = equivalent weight × (nearest whole-number valency).

L-4

Empirical & Molecular Formula

  • Empirical formula (E.F.): simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • Molecular formula (M.F.): actual number of atoms per molecule.
  • Structural formula: shows how atoms are bonded.
  • Steps from % composition: % → ÷ atomic mass → simple mole ratio → ÷ smallest → clear to whole numbers.
E.F. → M.F.M.F. = n × E.F. where n = (molar mass) / (empirical-formula mass) · Molar mass = 2 × V.D.
💡 TipIf the % values don't add to 100, the remainder is usually taken as oxygen.

Divide each subscript set by its HCF.
CH, CH₂, HO, Na₂CO₃, BH₃, NO₂, H₃PO₄, CH, N₂O₅

Find the empirical formula of a compound with 26.6% K, 35.4% Cr, 38.1% O. (K = 39.1, Cr = 52, O = 16)
Moles: K = 26.6/39.1 = 0.68, Cr = 35.4/52 = 0.68, O = 38.1/16 = 2.38.

L-5

Stoichiometric Calculations

  • A balanced equation gives mole ratio = molecule ratio = volume ratio (gases) and fixes the mass ratio.
  • Coefficients are stoichiometric coefficients (a "1" is not written). A balanced equation conserves mass and atoms.
  • General method: balance → write moles/masses (or 22.4 L for a gas at STP) under each species → use unitary method.
example ratio (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃)1 mol : 3 mol : 2 mol = 28 g : 6 g : 34 g = 1 vol : 3 vol : 2 vol
Four equivalent ways to solve a stoichiometry problem
(1) Mass ratio — scale the balanced-equation masses. (2) Mole ratio — convert to moles, apply the coefficient ratio, convert back. (3) Proportion — set (mass A / mass B) = (eqn mass A / eqn mass B). (4) Factor-label — chain unit conversions. All give the same answer.

Book Example · L-5
What mass of NH₃ is produced if 140 g N₂ reacts? (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃)
28 g N₂ → 34 g NH₃, so 140 g N₂ → (34/28) × 140.
170 g NH₃
Book Example · L-5
How many grams of O₂ are needed to react completely with 0.200 g H₂ to form water, and how much water forms? (O₂ + 2H₂ → 2H₂O)
4 g H₂ needs 32 g O₂ ⇒ 0.200 g H₂ needs (32/4) × 0.200 = 1.6 g O₂.
4 g H₂ gives 36 g H₂O ⇒ 0.200 g gives (36/4) × 0.200 = 1.8 g.
1.6 g O₂ and 1.8 g H₂O
Book Example · L-5
What volume of O₂ at NTP is produced by 6.125 g KClO₃? (2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂)
2 × 122.5 = 245 g KClO₃ → 3 × 22.4 L O₂.
6.125 g → (22.4 × 3 × 6.125)/245 = 411.6/245.

Book Example · L-5
What mass of zinc (Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂) is needed to make enough H₂ for 4 mol NH₃ (3H₂ + N₂ → 2NH₃)?
2 mol NH₃ needs 3 mol H₂ ⇒ 4 mol NH₃ needs (3/2) × 4 = 6 mol H₂.
1 mol H₂ needs 65.39 g Zn ⇒ 6 mol H₂ needs 65.39 × 6.
≈ 261.56 g Zn
L-6

Limiting & Excess Reagent

  • Limiting reagent: the reactant fully consumed — it decides how much product forms.
  • Excess reagent: the reactant left over after the reaction stops.
  • To find it: convert each reactant to moles, divide by its coefficient — the smallest value is limiting.
★ Key ideaAmount of product depends only on the limiting reagent. Adding more excess reagent does not increase the yield.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-6
20 g CaCO₃ is treated with 20 g HCl. How much CO₂ is generated? (CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂)
100 g CaCO₃ needs 73 g HCl ⇒ 20 g CaCO₃ needs (73 × 20)/100 = 14.6 g HCl. Available = 20 g, so HCl is in excess ⇒ CaCO₃ is limiting.
100 g CaCO₃ → 44 g CO₂ ⇒ 20 g → (44/100) × 20.
8.8 g CO₂
Book Example · L-6
100 g CaCO₃ reacts with 70 g H₃PO₄ (3CaCO₃ + 2H₃PO₄ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 3H₂O + 3CO₂). Find (a) grams of calcium phosphate produced and (b) grams of excess reagent left.
300 g CaCO₃ needs 196 g H₃PO₄ ⇒ 100 g needs 65.33 g. Available H₃PO₄ = 70 g > 65.33 g ⇒ CaCO₃ is limiting.
300 g CaCO₃ → 310 g Ca₃(PO₄)₂ ⇒ 100 g → (310 × 100)/300 = 103.33 g. Unreacted H₃PO₄ = 70 − 65.33.
(a) 103.33 g Ca₃(PO₄)₂ (b) 4.67 g H₃PO₄ left

ppt / ppm / ppb & Molarity

trace concentrationsppt = (parts solute/parts solution) × 10³ · ppm × 10⁶ · ppb × 10⁹ ⇒ ppb = ppm × 10³ = ppt × 10⁶
molarityM = (moles solute)/(litres solution) = (wB × 1000)/(MB × VmL)
molarity ↔ density & strengthM = (mass% × d × 10)/molar mass · Strength S (g/L) = M × molar mass
⚠ TrapMolarity is defined per litre of solution, not solvent, and it changes with temperature (volume expands). Molality does not.

L-9

Normality & Equivalent Mass

  • Normality (N) = gram-equivalents of solute per litre of solution.
  • Equivalent mass of an acid = mol mass / basicity; of a base = mol mass / acidity; of a salt = mol mass / total positive valency.
normalityN = (wB × 1000)/(EB × VmL) · N = M × valence factor
dilution & mixingN₁V₁ = N₂V₂ · N₁V₁ + N₂V₂ = N₃(V₁ + V₂)
💡 TipFor H₂SO₄, N = 2M (basicity 2); for Ca(OH)₂, N = 2M (acidity 2); for HCl/NaOH, N = M.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-9
Find the normality of a 500 mL solution containing 3.65 g HCl.
Eq. wt of HCl = 36.5. N = (3.65 × 1000)/(36.5 × 500).
0.2 N
Book Example · L-9
Calculate the normality of a solution having 15 g-equivalents dissolved in 750 mL of solution.
N = (15 × 1000)/750.
20 N
Book Example · L-9
Calculate the resultant normality when 20 mL of 5 N HCl is diluted to 1 L.
N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ ⇒ 5 × 20 = N₂ × 1000 ⇒ N₂ = 100/1000.
0.1 N
L-10

Mole Fraction & Molality

mole fractionxsolute = n/(n + N), xsolvent = N/(n + N), xsolute + xsolvent = 1
molalitym = (moles solute)/(kg solvent) = (w × 1000)/(MB × Wsolvent,g)
★ Temperature-independentMole fraction and molality use only masses/moles, so (unlike molarity) they do not change with temperature.
Handy inter-conversion
Molality from mole fraction: m = [xsolute/(1 − xsolute)] × (1000/M'solvent). Molarity ↔ molality: M = (m·d·1000)/(1000 + m·MB) where d = solution density.

L-11

Significant Figures

Counting rules

  • All non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant.
  • Leading zeros (0.0084 → 2 s.f.) are not significant; trailing zeros after a decimal (0.00800 → 3 s.f.) are.
  • Trailing zeros in a whole number may/may not be significant — use scientific notation to be clear.

Rounding & calculations

  • Drop < 5 → keep; drop > 5 → raise by 1; drop exactly 5 → round to the even digit.
  • ×, ÷: answer has as many s.f. as the operand with the fewest. +, −: answer keeps the fewest decimal places.

L-12

Laws of Chemical Combination

LawStatement
Conservation of massMass of reactants = mass of products (+ unreacted reactants).
Constant (definite) compositionA compound always has the same elements in a fixed mass ratio, regardless of source (CO₂ always C:O = 3:8).
Multiple proportionsMasses of one element combining with a fixed mass of another are in a simple whole-number ratio (CO vs CO₂ → O ratio 1:2).
Reciprocal proportionsMasses of B and C that each combine with a fixed mass of A are in the same ratio (or a simple multiple) as when B and C combine together.
💡 Reciprocal proportionsH combines with 23 parts Na (NaH) and 35.5 parts Cl (HCl) — the same 23:35.5 ratio in which Na and Cl combine to give NaCl.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-12
6.3 g NaHCO₃ is added to 15.0 g CH₃COOH; the residue weighs 18.0 g. What mass of CO₂ is released? (NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂)
By conservation of mass: reactants = 6.3 + 15.0 = 21.3 g = residue + CO₂ = 18.0 + x.
x = 21.3 − 18.0.
3.3 g CO₂
Book Example · L-12
Carbon and oxygen form two compounds. Carbon content is 42.9% in one and 27.3% in the other. Show that the data agree with the law of multiple proportions.
Compound 1: 42.9 g C with 57.1 g O ⇒ C per 1 g O = 42.9/57.1 = 0.75 g.
Compound 2: 27.3 g C with 72.7 g O ⇒ C per 1 g O = 27.3/72.7 = 0.375 g. Ratio 0.75 : 0.375 = 2 : 1.
Simple ratio 2 : 1 ⇒ law of multiple proportions holds.
Book Example · L-12
Dinitrogen and dioxygen form compounds with these masses — (i) 14 g N : 16 g O, (ii) 14 g : 32 g, (iii) 28 g : 32 g, (iv) 28 g : 80 g. Show they obey the law of multiple proportions.
Fix N at 14 g: O masses become 16 : 32 : 16 : 40.
Divide by 8 ⇒ 2 : 4 : 2 : 5, a simple whole-number ratio.
Ratio 2 : 4 : 2 : 5 ⇒ law of multiple proportions confirmed.
