1A 1.00 m diameter solid steel sphere is lowered to a depth of 10,000 m in a deep ocean trench. By how much does its diameter shrink? (a) 0.21 mm (b) 0.23 mm (c) 0.18 mm (d) 0.25 mmBulk modulus · diameter shrink at depthElasticity | physics | class 11 | A sphere contracts in volume by | sprinter Institute | Neet | Jee 🟡 Similara0.21 mm
Concept A solid steel sphere taken deep into the ocean feels the same hydrostatic pressure on all sides, so it shrinks in volume through its bulk modulus B. Because the compression is isotropic, every linear dimension — including the diameter — shrinks by one-third of the volume strain.
Formula ΔV/V = −P/B ⇒ Δd/d = (1/3)(P/B). A volume strain splits equally among the three dimensions, so linear strain is one-third of volume strain.
Solution
1. Hydrostatic pressure at depth h: P = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 10000 = 1×108 Pa.
2. Bulk modulus of steel B ≈ 1.6×1011 Pa, so volume strain ΔV/V = P/B = 1×108/1.6×1011 = 6.25×10−4.Only the magnitude matters for the shrink.
3. Diameter strain = one-third of this: Δd/d = 6.25×10−4/3 = 2.08×10−4.
4. With d = 1.00 m: Δd = 2.08×10−4 × 1.00 = 2.08×10−4 m.
5. Convert: Δd = 0.208 mm ≈ 0.21 mm, matching option (a).
6. The other options (0.23, 0.18, 0.25 mm) correspond to wrong values of B or to using P/B instead of P/3B.
⚡ JEE Trick For a sphere under all-round pressure the linear strain is always one-third of the volume strain — dividing by 3 is the step most students forget.
Answer: (a) 0.21 mm
2A uniform pressure P is exerted on all sides of a solid cube at temperature t°C. By what amount should the temperature of the cube be raised in order to bring its volume back to the volume it had before the pressure was applied, if the bulk modulus and coefficient of volume expansion of the material are B and γ respectively?Bulk modulus vs thermal expansionBulk Modulus Explained | Pressure & Volume | JEE Physics! 🟡 SimilarΔθ = P/(γB)
Concept Pressure P squeezes the cube, cutting its volume by P/B. To push the volume back to the original value we heat the cube, letting thermal expansion (coefficient γ) exactly cancel the pressure compression. Setting the two fractional volume changes equal gives the required temperature rise.
Formula γΔθ = P/B ⇒ Δθ = P/(γB). Thermal volume strain γΔθ must undo the pressure volume strain P/B.
Solution
1. Pressure reduces volume by a fraction (ΔV/V)press = −P/B.
2. Heating raises volume by (ΔV/V)temp = γΔθ.γ is the coefficient of volume (cubical) expansion.
3. To restore the original volume the two must cancel: γΔθ − P/B = 0.
4. Solve for the temperature rise: Δθ = P/(γB).
5. Check dimensions: P/B is dimensionless (a strain) and dividing by γ (per °C) gives a temperature — consistent.
6. Larger B (stiffer) or larger γ (expands easily) both need a smaller Δθ to recover the volume.Stiff, easily-expanding materials self-correct with little heating.
7. Note the cube’s shape does not matter — only the fractional volume change is being cancelled, so the same result holds for any solid.
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question says “heat it until the deformation is undone,” equate the thermal strain to the mechanical strain — never add them.
Answer: Δθ = P/(γB)

Concept The rod carries an axial pull F, but section AB is cut at an angle θ to the force line. The true area of that slanted cut is larger than A, and the force F resolves into one component along the plane (shearing) and one perpendicular to it (normal). Concept A mass placed on the piston raises the liquid pressure uniformly. That extra pressure squeezes the embedded sphere through its bulk modulus, shrinking its volume — and because the squeeze is isotropic, the radius shrinks by one-third of the volume strain.
Formula ΔP = Mg/A ; dV/V = −ΔP/B ; dR/R = (1/3)dV/V. Added weight / piston area is the pressure rise transmitted to the sphere.
Solution
1. Mass M on a piston of area A raises the liquid pressure by ΔP = Mg/A.
2. The liquid transmits this equally to every point of the sphere (Pascal’s law).Hydrostatic — so the sphere stays a sphere.
3. Volume strain of the sphere: dV/V = −ΔP/B = −Mg/(AB).
4. For a sphere V ∝ R³, so dV/V = 3 dR/R.
5. Therefore dR/R = (1/3)(dV/V) = −Mg/(3AB).
6. The fractional shrink in radius is dR/R = Mg/(3AB) in magnitude.The minus sign only signals compression; the size of the change is Mg/(3AB).
7. Notice the piston area A appears twice in effect — a bigger piston both lowers the pressure rise and leaves more room, so the sphere barely shrinks when A is large.
8. The container radius never enters: only the pressure the piston delivers, Mg/A, and the sphere’s own stiffness B control the result.
⚡ JEE Trick The chain “extra force → pressure (F/A) → volume strain (P/B) → linear strain (÷3)” solves almost every “sphere compressed by a load” problem in one line.

Concept A plank pushed by a uniform force on its end face accelerates, so each section carries only the force needed to accelerate the material ahead of it. The stress is largest at the pushed face (F/A) and falls linearly to zero at the free end, so the average strain is half the maximum.
Formula ε(x) = stress(x)/Y ; mean = (1/2)(F/AY). Stress drops linearly along an accelerating body, so the mean is half the peak.
Solution
1. Acceleration of the whole plank of mass M: a = F/M.
2. At the free end there is no material ahead, so stress = 0; at the pushed face the full F/A acts.The force at a section only accelerates the mass beyond it.
3. Stress varies linearly from F/A to 0, giving mean stress σavg = F/(2A).
4. Mean strain: εavg = σavg/Y = F/(2AY).
5. Hence the overall strain produced is Δl/l = (1/2)(F/AY).
6. If the plank were merely held still (static), stress would be uniform F/A and the strain would be twice as big.A free-to-move body always carries half the strain of a clamped one under the same end force.
7. The result is independent of the plank’s mass and length — only the applied force, area and Young’s modulus survive in the final strain.
⚡ JEE Trick An accelerating bar under an end force carries a linearly varying stress — always take the average (half the peak), never the peak, for the overall strain.
