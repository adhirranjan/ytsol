1A solution of known normality is diluted to two times. Which changes during dilution? (Equivalent / Moles / Milli-equivalent / Normality)Effect of dilutionNormality, dilution & mixing of solutions🟡 SimilardNormality of solution
Concept Dilution means adding pure solvent (water) to a solution. Water carries no solute, so the amount of dissolved substance — its number of moles and its number of gram-equivalents — is exactly the same before and after; nothing is added or removed from the solute itself. What does change is the volume, which grows. Since concentration terms (molarity, normality) are amount ÷ volume, a larger volume with the same amount means a smaller concentration.
Formula N = equivalents ÷ V(L) and milli-equivalents = N × V(mL) = equivalents × 1000. Equivalents and milli-equivalents are fixed counts of solute; only V is altered by dilution, so only N (a ratio containing V) responds. The invariance is captured by N₁V₁ = N₂V₂.
Solution
1. List each quantity and ask "does adding water change it?"Water adds volume only — it contributes no solute particles.
2. Equivalents of solute: unchanged (no solute added or lost).
3. Moles of solute: unchanged (same reason).
4. Milli-equivalents (= equivalents × 1000): unchanged, since equivalents are unchanged.
5. Normality = equivalents ÷ volume: volume doubles while equivalents stay fixed, so N is halved — it is the only quantity that changes.
 quantity          on diluting ×2      changes?
 equivalents        same                no
 moles              same                no
 milli-equivalents  same                no
 normality          N → N/2             YES  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick On dilution the equivalents/moles are conserved (that is the whole basis of N₁V₁ = N₂V₂); only the per-volume concentration drops. Diluting "two times" halves N. Trap: "milli-equivalent" tempts students because it contains volume in its N×V form — but meq = equivalents×1000 is a fixed count, so it does not change.
Answer: (d) Normality of solution.

4Normality of solution from mixing 0.45 N and 0.60 N NaOH in ratio 2:1 by volumeMixing normalitiesMixing 0.45 N and 0.60 N NaOH in ratio 2:1🟢 Exactb0.5 N
Concept When two solutions of the same solute are mixed, nothing reacts — the gram-equivalents simply add, and the volumes add. The mixture's normality is therefore total equivalents ÷ total volume, which works out to the volume-weighted average of the two normalities. The 2:1 ratio means the weaker (0.45 N) solution dominates the average.
Formula N_mix = (N₁V₁ + N₂V₂) ÷ (V₁ + V₂). Numerator = total equivalents contributed by each portion; denominator = final volume (volumes are additive for dilute solutions).
Solution
1. Ratio 2 : 1 by volume — take the simplest volumes V₁ = 2 L of 0.45 N and V₂ = 1 L of 0.60 N.Any actual volumes in a 2:1 ratio give the same answer, so choose easy numbers.
2. Equivalents from the first: 0.45 × 2 = 0.90 eq.
3. Equivalents from the second: 0.60 × 1 = 0.60 eq.
4. Total equivalents = 0.90 + 0.60 = 1.50 eq; total volume = 2 + 1 = 3 L.
5. N_mix = 1.50 ÷ 3 = 0.5 N — option (b).
6. Reasonableness: 0.5 lies between 0.45 and 0.60, closer to 0.45 (the larger share) ✓.
 portion      N       V     equivalents
 NaOH (1)     0.45    2 L   0.90
 NaOH (2)     0.60    1 L   0.60
 ─────────────────────────────────────
 mix          1.50 eq ÷ 3 L = 0.5 N  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Same-solute mixing = weighted average: (2×0.45 + 1×0.60)/3 = 0.5 in one line. The answer must lie between the two normalities and nearer the bigger volume's value — instantly eliminates any option outside 0.45–0.60. Trap: plain averaging (0.525) ignores the 2:1 weighting.
Answer: (b) 0.5 N.
5Normality from mixing 4 g NaOH, 500 mL of 1 M HCl and 10 mL of 49% H₂SO₄ (sp. gr. 1.1), made to 1 LMixing acids & baseNormality & equivalent weight in acid–base mixing🟡 Similara0.51
Concept When several acids and bases are mixed, they neutralise on a gram-equivalent basis, one equivalent of acid cancelling one equivalent of base. So convert every component to its number of equivalents, add up all acid equivalents and all base equivalents, and take the difference. The net equivalents of whichever is in excess, divided by the final total volume, gives the resulting normality.
Formula equivalents = mass ÷ Eq mass; for a solution equivalents = M × basicity × V(L). Eq mass = molar mass ÷ (basicity for acids, acidity for bases). Data: NaOH Eq = 40; HCl monobasic (Eq 36.5); H₂SO₄ dibasic (Eq 98/2 = 49). For the 49% acid, mass of solution = volume × specific gravity, then ×49% gives mass of pure H₂SO₄.
Solution
1. NaOH (base): equivalents = 4 g ÷ 40 = 0.10 eq.Eq mass of NaOH = molar mass 40 ÷ acidity 1 = 40.
2. HCl (acid): 500 mL of 1 M = 1 × 0.5 L = 0.5 mol; HCl is monobasic so equivalents = 0.5 × 1 = 0.50 eq.
3. H₂SO₄ (acid): mass of the 10 mL solution = volume × sp. gr. = 10 × 1.1 = 11 g.Specific gravity 1.1 means each mL weighs 1.1 g.
4. Pure H₂SO₄ in it = 49% of 11 g = 0.49 × 11 = 5.39 g.
5. Its equivalents = 5.39 ÷ 49 = 0.11 eq.Eq mass of H₂SO₄ = 98 ÷ 2 = 49.
6. Total acid = 0.50 + 0.11 = 0.61 eq; total base = 0.10 eq.
7. Net (acid in excess) = 0.61 − 0.10 = 0.51 eq, in a final volume of 1 L.
8. Normality = net equivalents ÷ volume = 0.51 ÷ 1 = 0.51 N (acidic).
 species    amount → equivalents            type
 NaOH       4 ÷ 40            = 0.10 eq      base
 HCl        1 M × 0.5 L       = 0.50 eq      acid
 H₂SO₄      (0.49×11) ÷ 49    = 0.11 eq      acid
 ───────────────────────────────────────────
 acid − base = (0.50+0.11) − 0.10 = 0.51 eq in 1 L ⇒ N = 0.51
⚡ JEE Trick Never average normalities — add equivalents, then subtract acid−base and divide by the total volume. For a "%×specific-gravity" acid, get grams as volume × sp.gr. × %/100 before dividing by Eq mass. Trap: using H₂SO₄'s molar mass 98 instead of Eq mass 49 halves its contribution — it is dibasic, so Eq mass = 49.
Answer: (a) 0.51.

Concept At STP one mole of any ideal gas occupies the same volume — the molar volume, 22.4 L (22.4 dm³). The identity of the gas (here CO₂) is irrelevant to the volume; it only matters that we count moles. So each part is a single multiplication of the mole number by 22.4 L.

Solution
1. (i) n = 5 mol ⇒ V = 5 × 22.4 = 112 L.
2. (ii) n = 0.5 mol ⇒ V = 0.5 × 22.4 = 11.2 L.
3. Cross-check by proportion: 0.5 mol is 1/10 of 5 mol, and 11.2 L is exactly 1/10 of 112 L ✓.
4. Note the mass is never needed — for gas volumes at STP, moles alone decide.44 g/mol for CO₂ would only be needed if the data were given in grams.

1What does basicity of an acid mean?Basicity of an acidBasicity of an acid — basic concept🟡 SimilaraNo. of H⁺ ions it can donate
Concept Basicity counts the number of replaceable (ionisable) hydrogen ions that one molecule of an acid can give up in a neutralisation reaction. It matters because basicity is exactly the factor that turns molar mass into equivalent mass (Eq mass = molar mass ÷ basicity) and molarity into normality (N = M × basicity). Only the H atoms that actually ionise count — an H bonded directly to the central atom (not to oxygen) is usually not replaceable.
Key relation basicity = number of H⁺ ions one acid molecule can donate, hence Eq mass = molar mass ÷ basicity. Each replaceable H⁺ neutralises one OH⁻, so basicity sets how many equivalents each mole of acid supplies.
Solution
1. Read the definition: basicity = how many H⁺ an acid molecule can release — option (a).
2. Count replaceable H for common acids: HCl → 1, H₂SO₄ → 2, H₃PO₄ → 3.Monobasic, dibasic, tribasic respectively.
3. Note the catch: in H₃PO₃ only 2 of the 3 H ionise (one H is bonded to P), so its basicity is 2, not 3.Only O–H hydrogens are donated; the P–H hydrogen is not.
4. Rule out distractors: it is not the number of OH⁻ (that is acidity of a base), nor the molar mass, nor the number of atoms.
 acid      replaceable H⁺    basicity
 HCl        1                 1  (monobasic)
 H₂SO₄      2                 2  (dibasic)
 H₃PO₄      3                 3  (tribasic)
 H₃PO₃      2 (one P–H)       2  (dibasic!)
⚡ JEE Trick Basicity = the acid's replaceable H⁺ count, and it is the divisor in Eq mass = M ÷ basicity. Memorise the odd ones: H₃PO₃ is dibasic, H₃PO₂ (hypophosphorous) is monobasic — count only O–H hydrogens, never P–H. Trap: reading "basicity" as a property of a base — here it describes the acid.
Answer: (a) No. of H⁺ ions it can donate.

4Equivalent mass of a salt is calculated asEquivalent mass of a saltEquivalent weight of acids, bases and salts🟡 SimilaraMolar mass / total positive valency
Concept Equivalent mass is always molar mass divided by an "n-factor" that says how many units of reaction one formula supplies. For an acid the n-factor is basicity, for a base it is acidity, and for a salt it is the total positive charge — i.e. the number of cations times each cation's charge (equal to the total negative charge). So the equivalent mass of a salt is its molar mass divided by the total positive valency.
Key relation Eq mass of salt = molar mass ÷ (total positive valency), where total positive valency = (number of cations) × (charge on each cation). This equals the total charge exchanged when the salt reacts, which is what "one equivalent" measures.
Solution
1. Recall the general rule: Eq mass = molar mass ÷ n-factor.
2. For a salt the n-factor is the total positive charge carried by its cations — option (a).
3. Apply to Na₂CO₃: molar mass = 106; two Na⁺ ⇒ total positive valency = 2; Eq mass = 106 ÷ 2 = 53.
4. Apply to AlCl₃: molar mass ≈ 133.5; one Al³⁺ ⇒ total positive valency = 3; Eq mass = 133.5 ÷ 3 = 44.5.
 salt        molar mass   total +ve valency   Eq mass
 NaCl         58.5         1 (Na⁺)             58.5
 Na₂CO₃       106          2 (2×Na⁺)           53
 AlCl₃        133.5        3 (Al³⁺)            44.5
⚡ JEE Trick n-factor by type: acid → basicity, base → acidity, salt → total cation charge. For a salt just multiply "how many cations × charge each." Trap: using only the number of cations (2 for Na₂CO₃) is right there, but for AlCl₃ it is the charge 3, not the count 1 — always use total charge.
Answer: (a) Molar mass / total positive valency.
5Dilution formula in terms of normality (N₁V₁ = N₂V₂)Dilution formulaNormality, dilution & mixing of solutions🟡 SimilaraN₁V₁ = N₂V₂
Concept The dilution law is just a statement that the amount of solute is conserved when you add water. The number of gram-equivalents present is N × V, and adding solvent changes neither the equivalents nor which molecules are there — only the volume. So the equivalents before dilution must equal the equivalents after, giving the compact rule N₁V₁ = N₂V₂.
Key relation N₁V₁ = N₂V₂, i.e. equivalents before = equivalents after. N×V = (eq/L)×L = equivalents; conserving this quantity across dilution gives the equality. The molarity analogue is M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. Both V's must be in the same unit.
Solution
1. Write the equivalents in the concentrated solution: eq = N₁ × V₁.
2. Write the equivalents after dilution: eq = N₂ × V₂.
3. Adding water adds no solute, so equivalents are conserved: N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ — option (a).
4. Example: to what volume must 100 mL of 2 N be diluted to get 0.5 N? V₂ = N₁V₁ ÷ N₂ = (2×100) ÷ 0.5 = 400 mL.Concentration dropped 4×, so volume must rise 4×.
 conserved quantity:  equivalents = N × V
 before = after   ⇒   N₁V₁ = N₂V₂
 e.g.  2 N × 100 mL = 0.5 N × V₂  ⇒  V₂ = 400 mL
⚡ JEE Trick N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ works with V in mL or L as long as both sides match — no need to convert. Same idea in molarity is M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. Trap: the formula holds only for pure dilution (same solute); for mixing two different acids/bases you must add equivalents instead.
Answer: (a) N₁V₁ = N₂V₂.
