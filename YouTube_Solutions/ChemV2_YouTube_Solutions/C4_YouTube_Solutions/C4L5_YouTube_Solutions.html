1Electron gain enthalpy becomes positive when an electron is added to which of the following? (a) Halogens (b) Noble gases (c) Sulphur (d) OxygenPositive ΔegHWhich set of elements shows a positive electron gain enthalpy🟡 SimilarbNoble gases
Concept Electron gain enthalpy ΔegH is the enthalpy change when one electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. Its sign tells you the direction of energy flow: negative means the atom accepts the electron willingly and energy is released (exothermic), positive means energy must be forced in (endothermic). A positive value therefore identifies an atom with nowhere comfortable to put the extra electron. Noble gases are exactly that case: their valence shell is already complete (ns²np⁶), so the incoming electron cannot enter the valence shell at all — it is pushed into the next principal quantum level, far from the nucleus and heavily shielded.
Key relation X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g); ΔH = ΔegH ΔegH < 0 ⇒ energy released ⇒ the anion is more stable than the atom; ΔegH > 0 ⇒ energy absorbed ⇒ the anion is less stable than the atom. For a noble gas: Ne(1s²2s²2p⁶) + e⁻ → Ne⁻(…2p⁶3s¹) the electron is forced into the empty 3s orbital of a brand-new shell — that is why ΔegH is large and positive.
Solution
1. Ask what makes ΔegH positive: the added electron must go somewhere unfavourable — either into a new shell, or into an already stable filled/half-filled arrangement.
2. Test option (b) Noble gases: the configuration ns²np⁶ is completely filled, so the electron enters the next shell, (n+1)s.A huge increase in distance from the nucleus plus complete screening by the closed octet ⇒ net repulsion ⇒ energy must be supplied. ΔegH is large and positive.
3. Eliminate (a) Halogens: ns²np⁵ is one electron short of a noble-gas octet, so the electron is grabbed eagerly — ΔegH is the most negative of all families (Cl = −349 kJ mol⁻¹).
4. Eliminate (c) Sulphur: 3s²3p⁴, two short of the octet, ΔegH = −200 kJ mol⁻¹ — strongly negative.
5. Eliminate (d) Oxygen: 2s²2p⁴, ΔegH = −141 kJ mol⁻¹ — again negative (though less so than S, because the small 2p shell repels the incoming electron).

⚡ JEE Trick Only three families give a positive ΔegH, and the reason is always "no room in a stable shell": group 18 (ns²np⁶, completely filled), group 2 (ns², filled s-subshell) and group 15 (ns²np³, half-filled p). Memorise those three and every "positive electron gain enthalpy" MCQ is instant. Concept Electron gain enthalpy is quoted per mole of atoms — the unit kJ mol⁻¹ already carries that information. The energy is therefore an extensive quantity: scale the tabulated value by the number of moles actually reacting. There is no chemistry left to do once you see this; the only trap is imagining that "0.25 mol" changes the enthalpy per mole (it does not — ΔegH is an intensive property of the element).
Formula Q = n × ΔegH n = number of moles of gaseous atoms each gaining one electron, ΔegH = molar electron gain enthalpy in kJ mol⁻¹; the negative sign of ΔegH carries straight through to Q, marking the process as exothermic.
Solution
1. Write the process for one mole: O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g); ΔegH = −142 kJ mol⁻¹.
2. Identify n = 0.25 mol of O atoms, each gaining exactly one electron."Each gain one electron" means the first electron gain enthalpy applies throughout — no second-electron step is involved.
3. Multiply: Q = 0.25 × (−142) = −35.5 kJ.
4. Interpret the sign: negative ⇒ 35.5 kJ of energy is released.
5. Kill the distractors by inspection: (b) −71 kJ = 0.5 mol; (c) −142 kJ = 1 mol; (d) −284 kJ = 2 mol. Each is the answer to a different mole count.

3Fluorine has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine because: (a) Fluorine has greater nuclear charge (b) Fluorine has a larger atomic size (c) Incoming electron faces strong repulsion in the compact 2p subshell of F (d) Fluorine has more shielding electronsF vs Cl anomalyThe electron affinity of fluorine is less than that of chlorine because — MCQ solved🟢 ExactcIncoming electron faces strong repulsion in the compact 2p subshell of F
Concept Down group 17 the general rule is "size increases ⇒ ΔegH becomes less negative", which would make F the most negative. Experiment says otherwise: Cl (−349) beats F (−328). The reason is that fluorine is too small. Its seven valence electrons are crammed into the compact 2p subshell, which has no extra room to spread into. An eighth electron entering that tiny volume suffers severe inter-electronic repulsion, and this repulsion cancels much of the energy the nucleus would otherwise release. In Cl the electron enters the roomier 3p subshell, repulsion is much smaller, and more net energy is released.
Key relation ΔegH (net) = (nucleus–electron attraction) − (electron–electron repulsion) Attraction favours the small atom; repulsion penalises the small atom. In F the repulsion term wins the competition, so the net release is smaller than in Cl. Observed values: F −328, Cl −349, Br −325, I −295 kJ mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Note the two competing effects for the incoming electron: nuclear attraction (helps) and repulsion from electrons already present (hinders).
2. In F the added electron enters n = 2, a shell of very small radius (F ≈ 72 pm) already holding 7 electrons.Electron density is extremely high, so electron–electron repulsion is abnormally large.
3. In Cl the electron enters n = 3 (Cl ≈ 99 pm), a physically larger and more diffuse subshell.The same seven valence electrons occupy a much bigger volume, so repulsion is far weaker and more net energy is released.
4. Hence ΔegH(Cl) = −349 is more negative than ΔegH(F) = −328 — precisely the wording of option (c).
5. Reject the others: (a) F does have a higher effective nuclear charge per unit size, but that would make its ΔegH more negative — it argues the wrong way; (b) is factually false, F is the smallest halogen; (d) is false, F has only the 1s² core, the least shielding of all halogens.

 F        2p                72         −328             VERY HIGH (compact 2p)
 Cl       3p                99         −349  ← most     moderate
 Br       4p               114         −325             low
 I        5p               133         −295             lowest
 ⇒ F is anomalous because of 2p compactness    <-- answer (c)
⚡ JEE Trick The whole of period 2 (N, O, F) is anomalous for exactly the same reason — small size ⇒ high electron density ⇒ extra repulsion — so their ΔegH is less negative than their period-3 partners (P, S, Cl). Learn the pattern O < S, F < Cl (in magnitude) as one fact. Trap: option (a) sounds authoritative because F really does have the largest Zeff/size ratio; but it would increase the release, so it cannot explain a smaller release.
Answer: (c) Incoming electron faces strong repulsion in the compact 2p subshell of F.
4Arrange N, O, P, S in increasing negativity (less negative → more negative) of electron gain enthalpy: (a) N < P < O < S (b) N < O < P < S (c) P < N < S < O (d) N < O < S < POrdering N, O, P, SArrange N, P, O and S in order of increasing negative electron gain enthalpy🟢 ExactaN < P < O < S
Concept This classic NCERT ordering needs two ideas at once. (i) The group-15 anomaly: N and P have a half-filled ns²np³ shell, which is extra stable, so they resist an extra electron — their ΔegH is barely negative (N is actually positive). (ii) The period-2 anomaly: within a group, the period-2 member (N, O) is so compact that repulsion cuts the release, making it less negative than its period-3 partner (P, S). Apply the group-15 rule first to separate {N, P} from {O, S}, then the size rule inside each pair.
Key values N ≈ +7, P ≈ −74, O ≈ −141, S ≈ −200 kJ mol⁻¹ N is positive because 2p³ is half-filled and tiny; P is negative but small in magnitude for the same half-filled reason; O and S are group-16, needing two electrons, so both are solidly negative — with S more negative than O by the period-2 compactness argument.
Solution
1. Split by group: N, P are group 15 (half-filled p³, reluctant); O, S are group 16 (p⁴, eager).Group-16 atoms gain an electron on the way to a stable octet, whereas group-15 atoms would destroy a half-filled set.
2. Inside group 15: N (period 2, r ≈ 65 pm) is far more compact than P (period 3, r ≈ 110 pm), so N suffers more repulsion ⇒ N is less negative than P. Order so far: N < P.
3. Inside group 16: by the same compactness argument O (period 2) is less negative than S (period 3). Order: O < S.
4. Splice the two chains using magnitudes: N (+7) < P (−74) < O (−141) < S (−200).Reading left to right the values become progressively more negative, which is what "increasing negativity" asks for.
5. So the order is N < P < O < S — option (a). Option (b) wrongly puts O before P; (c) wrongly makes O the most negative; (d) wrongly makes P the most negative.

5Consider O(g) → O⁻(g) (ΔH₁) and O⁻(g) → O²⁻(g) (ΔH₂). Which statement is correct? (a) ΔH₁ and ΔH₂ are both negative (b) ΔH₁ is negative but ΔH₂ is positive due to electron–electron repulsion (c) ΔH₂ is negative because oxide ion is more stable (d) ΔH₂ is smaller in magnitude than ΔH₁Successive ΔegHIs the second electron gain enthalpy always negative or always positive?🟡 SimilarbΔH₁ is negative but ΔH₂ is positive due to electron–electron repulsion
Concept The first electron is added to a neutral atom: the nucleus attracts it and (for most elements) energy is released, so ΔegH₁ < 0. The second electron is added to an already negative ion: now the incoming electron is repelled by a full unit of negative charge before it ever feels the nucleus. That electrostatic repulsion must be overcome by supplying energy, so ΔegH₂ is always positive — for every element, without exception. This is why O²⁻ is never formed spontaneously in the gas phase; it exists in oxides only because the huge lattice enthalpy pays the bill.
Key relations O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g); ΔegH₁ = −141 kJ mol⁻¹ exothermic — the neutral atom attracts the electron. O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g); ΔegH₂ = +780 kJ mol⁻¹ endothermic — work must be done against the repulsion between e⁻ and the O⁻ anion. Note the magnitude is much larger, not smaller.
Solution
1. Step 1 (ΔH₁): a neutral O atom with 2s²2p⁴ takes an electron to give 2p⁵ — nuclear attraction dominates, energy is released ⇒ ΔH₁ is negative.
2. Step 2 (ΔH₂): the reactant is now O⁻, carrying a full −1 charge. Bringing another electron up to it costs electrostatic work.Coulomb repulsion between two like charges must be overcome; the nucleus is also fully screened by nine electrons.
3. Therefore ΔH₂ is positive (endothermic) — exactly what option (b) states, and for exactly the stated reason (electron–electron repulsion).
4. Reject (a): it claims ΔH₂ is negative — contradicted by the +780 kJ mol⁻¹ figure printed in the theory.
5. Reject (c): O²⁻ is not more stable than O⁻ in isolation; it is stabilised only inside an ionic lattice.
6. Reject (d): |ΔH₂| = 780 is far larger than |ΔH₁| = 141, so "smaller in magnitude" is false.

 2nd electron gain           O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g)         +780        ENDOTHERMIC
 overall O → O²⁻             sum                         +639        endothermic

⚡ JEE Trick Learn one absolute rule: the second (and every later) electron gain enthalpy is positive for every element — no exceptions, no memorisation of individual values needed. It instantly settles all questions of the form "is ΔegH₂ positive or negative?" and all "why is O²⁻/S²⁻ not formed in the gas phase?" items. Trap: option (c) sounds chemically wise ("oxide ion is stable") — and O²⁻ is the common form in solids — but the question is about an isolated gaseous step, where lattice enthalpy plays no part.
Answer: (b) ΔH₁ is negative but ΔH₂ is positive due to electron–electron repulsion.

1Define electron gain enthalpy.Definition of ΔegHElectron gain enthalpy — definition and meaning (Khan Academy, Grade 11)🟡 Similar—
Concept A good definition of a periodic property must pin down four things: what happens (an electron is added), to what (an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state), what is produced (a uninegative gaseous ion), and what is measured (the accompanying enthalpy change). Every word matters. "Isolated" and "gaseous" exclude any neighbouring atoms, so that only the atom's own nucleus and electrons decide the energy — this is what makes ΔegH a genuine atomic property, unlike electronegativity which needs a bond.
Statement When an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom in its ground state to convert it into a negative ion, the energy change accompanying the process is called the electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH). Written as a process: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g); ΔH = ΔegH. Units: eV per atom, or kJ per mole of atoms.
Solution
1. Give the sentence exactly as above — the marking points are "isolated gaseous atom", "ground state", "negative ion" and "energy change".
2. Support it with the chemical equation X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g); ΔH = ΔegH.The equation earns marks on its own and fixes the direction of the change beyond doubt.
3. State the units: electron volt per atom or kilojoule per mole of atoms.
4. Explain the sign convention: ΔegH is negative when energy is released (the usual case, e.g. halogens) and positive when energy must be absorbed (noble gases, group 2, group 15).
5. Give one worked example each way: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g); ΔegH = −349 kJ mol⁻¹ (exothermic) and the second gain O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g); ΔegH₂ = +780 kJ mol⁻¹ (endothermic).
 DEFINITION  X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) ;  ΔH = Δ(eg)H
 conditions  isolated · gaseous · ground state · neutral atom
 units       eV atom⁻¹   or   kJ mol⁻¹
 sign        negative → energy RELEASED   (Cl : −349 kJ mol⁻¹)
             positive → energy ABSORBED   (noble gases; O⁻ + e⁻ : +780)

⚡ JEE Trick Write the equation first, sentence second — the equation alone contains "gaseous", "one electron" and "negative ion", so the words then flow automatically and you cannot drop a marking point. Trap: writing "energy released" instead of "energy change". ΔegH is not always a release; for noble gases energy is absorbed, so the neutral phrase "energy change" is the safe one. (Electron affinity, by contrast, is defined as the energy released, so EA = −ΔegH.)
Key point: ΔegH is the enthalpy change when one electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state to form a uninegative gaseous ion — X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g); measured in eV atom⁻¹ or kJ mol⁻¹, negative when energy is released and positive when it is absorbed.
2Which group elements have the highest and the lowest electron gain enthalpies?Highest & lowest ΔegHElectron gain enthalpy — complete treatment including group extremes🟡 Similar—
Concept "Highest electron gain enthalpy" in the everyday sense means the largest energy release, i.e. the most negative ΔegH. That belongs to the family that is closest to a noble-gas configuration but not yet there — the halogens, group 17 (ns²np⁵), which need exactly one electron. "Lowest" means the least favourable, i.e. the most positive ΔegH, and that belongs to the family that already has the noble-gas configuration — the noble gases, group 18 (ns²np⁶). Notice how the two answers are simply two sides of the same octet argument.
Key relation Halogen: ns²np⁵ + e⁻ → ns²np⁶ one electron converts the atom into the ultra-stable octet, so a large amount of energy is released — ΔegH is the most negative in the whole table. Noble gas: ns²np⁶ + e⁻ → ns²np⁶ (n+1)s¹ the octet is already complete, so the electron must start a new shell — energy has to be supplied and ΔegH is large and positive.
Solution
1. Highest (most negative) — group 17, the halogens. Configuration ns²np⁵, one electron short of the octet, with a high effective nuclear charge and small size.Values: F −328, Cl −349, Br −325, I −295 kJ mol⁻¹.
2. Name the single champion within that group: chlorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy of any element (−349 kJ mol⁻¹), not fluorine — because the incoming electron meets less repulsion in Cl's larger 3p subshell.
3. Lowest (most positive) — group 18, the noble gases. Configuration ns²np⁶ is complete, so an extra electron is forced into the next principal shell.ΔegH is positive for every noble gas, the process being strongly endothermic.
4. Mention the two secondary "low" families that examiners like: group 2 (ns², filled s-subshell) and group 15 (ns²np³, half-filled p) also have low or positive ΔegH because of their stable configurations.
5. Summarise the whole trend in one line: ΔegH becomes more negative across a period (up to group 17) and less negative down a group, with group 18 the sharp exception at the end of every period.

3Explain the trends or variation in electron gain enthalpy in the periodic table.Variation of ΔegHFactors affecting electron gain enthalpy and its variation across the periodic table🟢 Exact—
Concept ΔegH is controlled by a tug-of-war between nuclear attraction (pulls the incoming electron in, releases energy) and size + inter-electronic repulsion (pushes it away, absorbs energy). Across a period the nuclear charge rises while the shell number stays fixed, so attraction wins and ΔegH becomes more negative. Down a group the shell number rises, distance and shielding grow, so attraction weakens and ΔegH becomes less negative. Every "exception" in this chapter is just one of these two terms behaving unusually.
Key relations Along a period (→): Zeff ↑ , size ↓ ⇒ ΔegH more negative The added electron sits nearer a more highly charged nucleus, so more energy is released. Down a group (↓): n ↑ , size ↑ , shielding ↑ ⇒ ΔegH less negative The added electron sits far from the nucleus and is screened by more inner shells, so less energy is released.
Solution
1. Variation along a period. Moving left to right, the effective nuclear charge increases and the atomic size decreases, so the added electron on average lies closer to the nucleus and is more strongly attracted. Hence ΔegH becomes more negative from left to right, reaching its extreme at group 17.
2. Exception within a period. ΔegH of group 2 (ns², filled) and group 15 (ns²np³, half-filled) is unexpectedly low because of the extra stability of those configurations; that of group 18 is positive because the valence shell is complete and the electron must enter a new shell.
3. Variation down a group. Going down, the atomic size increases, so the distance between the nucleus and the incoming electron increases and the shielding by inner shells grows. The force of attraction falls and ΔegH becomes less negative down a group.
4. Exception within a group. The second-period elements N, O, F have less negative ΔegH than their third-period successors P, S, Cl.In period 2 the electron enters the small n = 2 level, where it meets much greater repulsion from the electrons already there; in period 3 it enters the roomier n = 3 level, where repulsion is far smaller. This is why chlorine — not fluorine — has the most negative electron gain enthalpy in the periodic table.
5. Worked illustration (the NCERT example): among P, S, Cl and F, the order of ΔegH is P < S < F < Cl — most negative for chlorine, least negative for phosphorus.P is least negative because of its half-filled 3p³; F falls behind Cl because of 2p compactness.

   exception      gp 2 (ns²) & gp 15 (ns²np³) low ; gp 18 POSITIVE
 ↓ down group     n ↑ , size ↑ , shielding ↑      LESS negative
   exception      N,O,F (period 2) less negative than P,S,Cl (period 3)

 net record       Cl = −349 kJ mol⁻¹  (most negative in the whole table)

⚡ JEE Trick Answer this in a fixed four-block shape — period trend, period exception, group trend, group exception — and you can never leave out the marks-carrying half. One sentence covers both exceptions: "stability of filled/half-filled shells spoils the period trend; compactness of the n = 2 shell spoils the group trend." Trap: describing the group trend as "increases down the group". ΔegH increases algebraically (becomes less negative) but the energy released decreases — always say "less negative" to avoid ambiguity.
Key point: across a period ΔegH becomes more negative (Zeff ↑, size ↓), except that groups 2 and 15 are low and group 18 is positive; down a group ΔegH becomes less negative (size ↑, shielding ↑), except that N, O, F are less negative than P, S, Cl — which is why Cl has the most negative ΔegH in the table.
4Fluorine has less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine. Give reasons. OR Fluorine has less electron affinity than chlorine. Give reasons.F vs Cl — reasonsExplain why the electron gain enthalpy of fluorine is less negative than that of chlorine🟢 Exact—
Concept Fluorine sits above chlorine in group 17, so the size rule ("smaller atom ⇒ more negative ΔegH") predicts F should win. It does not. The resolution is that ΔegH is a net quantity: energy released by nucleus–electron attraction minus energy spent overcoming repulsion from the electrons already present. Fluorine is so small (r ≈ 72 pm) that its 2p subshell is extremely crowded, and the repulsion penalty is unusually large. Chlorine's 3p subshell is bigger and more diffuse, the penalty is much smaller, and the net release ends up larger.
Key relation |ΔegH| = (attraction term) − (inter-electronic repulsion term) In F the second term is abnormally large because 7 valence electrons occupy the tiny 2p subshell; in Cl the same 7 electrons occupy a far larger 3p volume. Observed: F = −328, Cl = −349 kJ mol⁻¹ — a gap of 21 kJ mol⁻¹ in Cl's favour.
Solution
1. State the fact: ΔegH(F) = −328 kJ mol⁻¹ while ΔegH(Cl) = −349 kJ mol⁻¹, so Cl releases more energy — it has the more negative value.
2. Give the primary reason: fluorine has a very small atomic size, so its valence shell already has a very high electron density.Seven electrons squeezed into the compact 2p subshell repel each other strongly even before the eighth arrives.
3. Explain the consequence: the incoming electron experiences strong inter-electronic repulsion as it enters the small n = 2 level, and this repulsion cancels a large part of the energy released by nuclear attraction.
4. Contrast with chlorine: the electron enters the larger, more diffuse 3p subshell of the n = 3 level, where repulsion is much weaker, so the net energy released is greater.
5. Generalise (worth an extra mark): the same argument makes O less negative than S and N less negative than P — the whole second period is anomalous for this reason.
6. Add the standard rider: fluorine is nevertheless a stronger oxidising agent than chlorine in aqueous solution, because of its low bond-dissociation enthalpy and the very high hydration enthalpy of F⁻ — do not confuse that with ΔegH.

Concept The four options are four different atomic energies, and the way to keep them apart is to ask two questions about each: is an electron added or removed? and is the species an isolated gaseous atom? Electron gain enthalpy is the only one that adds an electron to an isolated gaseous atom. Note also the careful wording "released or absorbed" — ΔegH can be either sign, so a definition that says only "released" would strictly be the definition of electron affinity.
Key relations ΔegH: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) electron ADDED to a neutral gaseous atom. Ionisation enthalpy: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻ electron REMOVED; always endothermic. Enthalpy of atomisation: ½X₂(g) → X(g) a bond is broken; no electron transfer at all.
Solution
1. Read the definition the chapter gives: "when an electron is added to a gaseous atom in its ground state to convert it into a negative ion, the energy change accompanying the process is called the electron gain enthalpy".
2. Match it word for word with option (b) — "energy released or absorbed when an electron is added to a gaseous atom". Both the direction (added) and the sign-neutral wording (released or absorbed) are right.
3. Reject (a) "energy to remove electron" — that is ionisation enthalpy, the exact reverse process.
4. Reject (c) "ionisation energy" — the same quantity as (a), just named directly.
5. Reject (d) "enthalpy of atomisation" — the energy to break a molecule into free gaseous atoms; no electron changes hands.
 quantity                 process                       electron   sign
 electron gain enthalpy   X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)             ADDED      ± (usually −)
 ionisation enthalpy      X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻             removed    always +
 enthalpy of atomisation  ½X₂(g) → X(g)                 none       always +
 ⇒ only the first matches "an electron is added"    <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Tag each energy with a one-word verb: gain enthalpy = ADD, ionisation enthalpy = REMOVE, atomisation = BREAK, electronegativity = SHARE (in a bond). Trap: option (b)'s phrase "released or absorbed" looks vague and students reject it for that reason — but it is precisely correct, because ΔegH is negative for halogens and positive for noble gases.
Answer: (b) Energy released or absorbed when an electron is added to a gaseous atom.
2What affects electron gain enthalpy the most? (a) Atomic size (b) Nuclear charge (c) Electronic configuration (d) All of theseFactors affecting ΔegHAtomic size, nuclear charge and electronic configuration — the three factors that affect E.A.🟢 ExactdAll of these
Concept The theory section of this lecture lists the factors affecting electron gain enthalpy under exactly three headings — (a) atomic size, (b) nuclear charge, (c) electronic configuration — so the question is testing whether you noticed that all three are named. Physically this makes sense: the energy released depends on how strongly the nucleus pulls (nuclear charge), how far away the electron ends up (atomic size), and whether there is a comfortable orbital waiting for it (electronic configuration).
Key relations size ↑ ⇒ ΔegH less negative the added electron sits farther from the nucleus, so attraction weakens. nuclear charge ↑ ⇒ ΔegH more negative a stronger pull on the incoming electron releases more energy. half-filled or fully-filled valence shell ⇒ ΔegH low or positive the extra stability of such configurations must first be destroyed, which costs energy.
Solution
1. Atomic size. As size increases the distance between the nucleus and the outermost shell increases, the force of attraction on the incoming electron decreases, and ΔegH becomes less negative.This is why ΔegH becomes less negative down a group.
2. Nuclear charge. As nuclear charge increases the attraction between the nucleus and the incoming electron increases and ΔegH becomes more negative.This is why ΔegH becomes more negative across a period.
3. Electronic configuration. For elements with half-filled or fully-filled outer shells, that extra stability means energy must be supplied to add an electron, so ΔegH is positive.Examples given in the theory: noble gases (ns²np⁶) and group 15 elements (ns²np³).
4. Since the chapter names all three as genuine factors, no single one can be selected — the correct option is (d) All of these.
5. Sanity check with one element: chlorine has moderate size, high nuclear charge and a one-short-of-octet 3p⁵ configuration; all three push in the same direction, which is exactly why Cl holds the record at −349 kJ mol⁻¹.

3Across a period, electron gain enthalpy becomes: (a) More negative (b) Less negative (c) Positive (d) ConstantPeriod trendElectron gain enthalpy — trend across a period and down a group🟡 SimilaraMore negative
Concept Along a period the principal quantum number of the valence shell is fixed, but each step to the right adds one proton to the nucleus. Shielding by electrons in the same shell is poor, so the effective nuclear charge Zeff rises steadily and the atom contracts. Both changes work the same way on an incoming electron: it ends up closer to a more highly charged nucleus, so more energy is released. Hence ΔegH becomes progressively more negative from left to right, up to the halogens.
Key relation left → right: Zeff ↑ and atomic size ↓ ⇒ ΔegH more negative The added electron "on an average would be closer to the nucleus, i.e. strongly attracted by the nucleus" — the exact wording of the theory. The exception is group 18, where the shell is full and ΔegH flips positive.
Solution
1. Fix what changes across a period: nuclear charge increases by one proton per step, but no new shell is added.
2. Deduce the two consequences: effective nuclear charge increases and atomic radius decreases.
3. Apply them to the incoming electron: it is held closer and pulled harder, so more energy is released ⇒ ΔegH becomes more negative — option (a).
4. Verify on period 3: Na −53, Mg (positive), Al −43, Si −134, P −74, S −200, Cl −349.The overall drift is clearly downwards (more negative); the wobbles at Mg (filled 3s²) and P (half-filled 3p³) are the stability exceptions the theory warns about.
5. Reject the rest: (b) "less negative" is the group trend, not the period trend; (c) "positive" applies only to group 18 at the very end of the period; (d) "constant" contradicts the steadily rising Zeff.

4Down a group, electron gain enthalpy becomes: (a) More negative (b) Less negative (c) Positive (d) Highly negativeGroup trendElectron gain enthalpy — trends in the periodic table (Class 11)🟡 SimilarbLess negative
Concept Descending a group adds a whole new principal shell at every step. Two things follow: the incoming electron ends up much farther from the nucleus, and it is screened by one more complete inner shell. Although the nuclear charge also grows, the extra protons are almost entirely cancelled by the extra shielding, so the net pull on an added electron weakens. Less energy is released, i.e. ΔegH becomes less negative.
Key relation down a group: n ↑ , radius ↑ , shielding ↑ ⇒ attraction ↓ ⇒ ΔegH less negative The theory states it directly: "the distance between the nucleus and incoming electron increases … thus, the value of electron gain enthalpy becomes less negative."
Solution
1. Identify what changes down a group: a new shell is added, so n and the atomic radius both increase, and the number of shielding inner shells increases.
2. Deduce the effect: the force of attraction between the nucleus and the incoming electron decreases.
3. Conclude: less energy is released, so ΔegH becomes less negative — option (b).
4. Verify with group 17: F −328, Cl −349, Br −325, I −295.From Cl downwards the values march steadily towards zero, exactly as predicted. F is the one exception (it should be the most negative but is not) because of the compactness of its 2p subshell.
5. Reject the rest: (a) is the period trend; (c) "positive" happens only for group 18 and other filled/half-filled configurations, not as a general group trend; (d) "highly negative" is the opposite of the correct direction.

5Chlorine has more negative EGE than fluorine due to: (a) Higher mass (b) Less repulsion in 3rd shell (c) More protons (d) Similar sizeCl vs F reasonChlorine has more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine — why?🟢 ExactbLess repulsion in 3rd shell
Concept This is the same anomaly as Suggested-Q3, but phrased from chlorine's side. The energy actually released on adding an electron is the difference between what nuclear attraction gives and what electron–electron repulsion takes away. Chlorine's added electron goes into the n = 3 level, which is physically much larger than fluorine's n = 2. A bigger box means the electrons spread out, repulsion is small, and very little of the attraction energy is wasted — so Cl ends up with the most negative ΔegH in the entire periodic table.
Key relation ΔegH(Cl) = −349 < ΔegH(F) = −328 kJ mol⁻¹ Because the repulsion penalty in the roomy 3p subshell is small, whereas in F's compact 2p subshell it is large. The gap, 21 kJ mol⁻¹, is entirely a repulsion effect — nuclear attraction alone would favour F.
Solution
1. Compare the shells the incoming electron enters: F → 2p (radius ≈ 72 pm) versus Cl → 3p (radius ≈ 99 pm).
2. In the n = 3 level the valence electrons occupy a much larger volume, so the electron density is lower and the repulsion felt by the incoming electron is much smaller.That is precisely the wording of option (b): "less repulsion in 3rd shell".
3. Less repulsion means less of the released energy is cancelled, so the net ΔegH of Cl is more negative than that of F.
4. Reject (a) "higher mass" — atomic mass plays no part in electronic energies; this is a pure distractor.
5. Reject (c) "more protons" — Cl does have more protons (17 vs 9), but they are matched by more inner shielding shells; Zeff at the valence shell is actually lower in Cl, so this argument would predict the wrong answer.
6. Reject (d) "similar size" — F (72 pm) and Cl (99 pm) are markedly different in size, and in any case "similar size" explains nothing.

 Cl       3p         99       lower     → small repulsion   −349  ← record

⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question asks "why is the period-3 element more negative than its period-2 partner", the answer is always less inter-electronic repulsion in the larger shell — it works for F/Cl, O/S and N/P without change. Trap: "more protons" (option c) sounds like a proper Zeff argument, and Zeff is the right tool for period trends; but within a group the extra protons are cancelled by extra shielding, so it is the wrong tool here.
Answer: (b) Less repulsion in 3rd shell.

Concept Enthalpy is a state function, so the overall enthalpy change of a two-step process is simply the algebraic sum of the two steps (Hess's law). Forming O²⁻ from a neutral O atom is exactly such a process: first an exothermic capture, then a strongly endothermic one. Because the second step costs far more than the first releases, the net change is positive — which is the whole chemical point of this question: the oxide ion is not formed spontaneously in the gas phase, and exists only because lattice enthalpy pays the difference in solids like MgO.
Formula ΔegH(total) = ΔegH₁ + ΔegH₂ Hess's law — add the signed values, never the magnitudes. Step 1: O(g)+e⁻→O⁻(g), ΔH₁ = −141. Step 2: O⁻(g)+e⁻→O²⁻(g), ΔH₂ = +844 (positive because an electron must be pushed onto an already negative ion).

Concept The question supplies the two enthalpy values and asks for the reason behind the sign change. The mechanism is purely electrostatic: once the first electron has been captured, the species carries a full −1 charge. A second electron approaching it is repelled by that charge long before it can be attracted by the nucleus, and work must be done to overcome the repulsion. In plain words, the O⁻ ion resists taking another electron — which is precisely the wording of option (c).
Key relations O(g) + e⁻ → O⁻(g); ΔH = −142 kJ mol⁻¹ neutral atom, nucleus attracts, exothermic. O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g); ΔH = +748 kJ mol⁻¹ anion repels the incoming electron, so energy must be supplied — endothermic. Overall: O(g) + 2e⁻ → O²⁻(g); ΔH = +606 kJ mol⁻¹
Solution
1. Identify what changes between the two steps: the reactant goes from neutral O to negatively charged O⁻.
2. Step 1 is exothermic because a neutral atom's nucleus is not screened by any net negative charge and can attract the incoming electron.
3. Step 2 is endothermic because the incoming electron must be pushed against the Coulomb repulsion of the −1 charge already present."To overcome this repulsion, energy has to be supplied" — the exact statement in the theory of this lecture.
4. That mechanism is described by option (c): the O⁻ ion resists the addition of another electron.
5. Reject (a): O⁻ is larger than O, but size alone would make the second addition merely weaker, not endothermic — the sign flip needs the charge argument.
6. Reject (b) "oxygen has higher electron affinity" — this explains why step 1 is exothermic, not why step 2 is endothermic; it answers the wrong half of the question.
7. Reject (d) "oxygen is more electronegative" — electronegativity concerns bonded atoms and is irrelevant to an isolated gas-phase electron transfer.

8Why do noble gases have positive electron gain enthalpy? (a) It is difficult to add an electron due to small size (b) It is difficult to add an electron due to high electronegativity (c) It is difficult to add an electron due to stable configuration (d) It is difficult to add an electron due to high electron affinityReason for positive ΔegHElectron gain enthalpy and its trends — the noble-gas exception explained🟡 SimilarcIt is difficult to add an electron due to stable configuration
Concept Noble gases already possess the arrangement every other atom is striving for — a completely filled ns²np⁶ valence shell (1s² for helium). There is literally no vacancy left in that shell, so an added electron must be accommodated in the next higher principal quantum level, far from the nucleus and fully screened by the closed octet. The resulting anion is highly unstable, so a large amount of energy must be supplied: ΔegH is positive. The single-word cause is "stable (fully filled) configuration".
Key relation ns²np⁶ + e⁻ → ns²np⁶(n+1)s¹ ; ΔH > 0 The theory states it directly: "the electron enters in the next higher principal quantum level leading to a highly unstable configuration and for this a large amount of energy is required. That is why electron gain enthalpy of noble gases has a large positive value."
Solution
1. Write the noble-gas valence configuration: ns²np⁶ — completely filled, hence chemically inert.
2. Ask where an added electron could go: not into the full valence shell, so it must enter the (n+1)s orbital of a new shell.
3. Assess that placement: much greater distance from the nucleus, complete shielding by the closed octet, and repulsion from eight valence electrons ⇒ the anion is highly unstable.
4. Conclude: energy must be supplied, so ΔegH is large and positive, and the cause is the stable, fully filled configuration ⇒ option (c).
5. Reject (a) "small size" — size varies enormously across the group (He is tiny, Rn is large), yet every noble gas has a positive ΔegH, so size cannot be the cause.
6. Reject (b) "high electronegativity" — noble gases are conventionally assigned no electronegativity at all, since they form virtually no bonds.
7. Reject (d) "high electron affinity" — self-contradictory: a high electron affinity would mean the atom accepts electrons eagerly, which is the opposite of a positive ΔegH.
