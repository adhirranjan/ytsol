★ Core ideaFriction is a self-adjusting contact force: it takes whatever value (up to μsN) is needed to prevent relative sliding, and always opposes the tendency of relative motion — not necessarily the motion of the body itself.
L-1

Nature, Origin & Types of Friction

  • Origin: surfaces that look smooth are microscopically rough; real contact happens only at tiny high spots (asperities) that cold-weld together. Friction is the force resisting the shearing of these micro-welds — it is electromagnetic in nature.
  • Friction is a contact force acting tangential to the surface; the normal reaction N is its perpendicular partner. The two combine into a single contact force.

Static, limiting & kinetic friction

static (self-adjusting)0 ≤ fs ≤ μsN   (equals the applied pull until it reaches the limit)
limitingfs,max = μsN  ·  kinetic fk = μkN  (μs > μk)
  • Static friction acts when there is no relative sliding; it grows to match the applied force up to the limiting value μsN.
  • Limiting friction is the maximum static value — reached the instant motion is about to begin.
  • Kinetic (sliding) friction acts once sliding starts; it is essentially constant and less than limiting friction, so a body jerks forward the moment it "breaks free".
Why μs > μk
At rest the asperities have time to settle and form stronger micro-welds, so more force is needed to shear them (μsN). Once sliding, the surfaces skate over the tops of the asperities — contact time per weld is tiny, welds are weaker, so the resisting force drops to μkN. This is exactly why it is harder to start pushing furniture than to keep it moving.

Applied force vs friction graph

  • As applied force P rises from 0: friction f = P (line at 45°, static region) until f reaches μsN.
  • At the "hump" (P = μsN) motion begins; friction then drops to the constant kinetic value μkN and stays flat.
💡 Picture itStraight 45° line up to a peak (limiting friction) → sudden small drop → horizontal line (kinetic friction). The peak-to-plateau gap is the μs − μk difference.
⚠ TrapStatic friction is not always μsN. That is only its maximum. If a 10 N push meets a block that can resist up to 30 N, friction is 10 N, not 30 N.
Worked examples
Example · L-1
A 5 kg block rests on a floor with μs = 0.4, μk = 0.3. Find the friction force when the applied horizontal push is (a) 12 N, (b) 25 N. (g = 10)
N = mg = 50 N ⇒ limiting friction = μsN = 0.4 × 50 = 20 N.
(a) Push 12 N < 20 N ⇒ block stays put; static friction = applied force = 12 N.
(b) Push 25 N > 20 N ⇒ block slides; friction = μkN = 0.3 × 50 = 15 N.
(a) 12 N (static) (b) 15 N (kinetic)
Example · L-1
For the same 5 kg block (μs = 0.4, μk = 0.3), what is the acceleration just after a 25 N push is applied? (g = 10)
Once sliding, net force = 25 − fk = 25 − 15 = 10 N.
a = Fnet/m = 10/5.
a = 2 m s⁻²
Example · L-1
A 2 kg book is pressed against a vertical wall by a horizontal force of 30 N; μs = 0.5. Does it slide down? (g = 10)
Normal reaction N = applied force = 30 N ⇒ max static friction = 0.5 × 30 = 15 N (acting up).
Weight to be held = mg = 20 N > 15 N.
Yes — required friction (20 N) exceeds the limit (15 N), so the book slides down.
L-2

Laws of Friction · Coefficient · Angle of Friction & Repose

Laws of friction

  • Limiting/kinetic friction ∝ normal reaction N:  f = μN.
  • Friction is independent of the apparent area of contact (a brick pulled on its face or its edge needs the same force).
  • Kinetic friction is (nearly) independent of the relative speed of sliding.
  • μ depends only on the nature of the two surfaces in contact.
Why area does not matter (but N does)
Friction depends on the real microscopic contact area, not the apparent one. Spreading the load over a larger face lowers the pressure, so each asperity is squashed less and the real contact area per unit stays smaller — the product stays the same. Push harder (raise N) and every micro-weld is squashed flatter, increasing real contact area, so friction grows with N. Hence f ∝ N, not ∝ apparent area.

Coefficient of friction

definitionμ = f / N  (dimensionless)  ·  μs = fs,max/N,   μk = fk/N

Angle of friction λ

angle of frictiontan λ = fs,max / N = μs
  • λ is the angle the total contact force (resultant of N and limiting friction) makes with the normal when motion just impends.

Angle of repose θr

angle of reposetan θr = μs  ⇒  θr = λ
  • θr is the maximum incline angle at which a block just stays at rest without sliding.
Why angle of repose = angle of friction
On an incline of angle θ the component of weight along the surface is mg sinθ and the normal reaction is N = mg cosθ. At the verge of sliding, mg sinθ = μsN = μs mg cosθ ⇒ tan θr = μs. Since tan λ = μs as well, the two angles are numerically equal: θr = λ.
★ RememberThree quantities are one and the same number:  μs = tan λ = tan θr. Measuring the tipping angle of a plank gives μs directly.
Worked examples
Example · L-2
A block just begins to slide when a plank is tilted to 30°. Find the coefficient of static friction.
Angle of repose θr = 30°; μs = tan θr = tan 30°.
μs = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577
Example · L-2
The angle of friction between two surfaces is 45°. A 4 kg block rests on the lower surface (horizontal). What horizontal force will just make it move? (g = 10)
μs = tan λ = tan 45° = 1. N = mg = 40 N.
F = μsN = 1 × 40.
F = 40 N
Example · L-2
A body of mass m is placed on a rough horizontal surface with μs = 0.75. Find the angle of friction and the minimum incline at which the body would start to slide.
tan λ = μs = 0.75 ⇒ λ = tan⁻¹(0.75) ≈ 36.9°.
Angle of repose = angle of friction ⇒ θr = λ.
λ = θr ≈ 36.9°
L-3

Friction on an Inclined Plane

forces on incline (angle θ)along: mg sinθ  ·  normal: N = mg cosθ  ·  friction limit: μs mg cosθ

Will it slide?

sliding conditionslides down if  tan θ > μs  (stays if tan θ ≤ μs)
acceleration down a rough inclinea = g(sinθ − μk cosθ)
Setting up the incline
Resolve weight into mg sinθ (down the slope) and mg cosθ (into the slope). The surface supplies N = mg cosθ, so limiting friction = μs mg cosθ. If the driving component mg sinθ exceeds this limit the block slides; while sliding, kinetic friction μk mg cosθ acts up the slope, giving net force mg sinθ − μk mg cosθ and hence a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ). On a frictionless incline this reduces to a = g sinθ.

Holding or pushing along the incline

force applied UP the inclineto just start it moving up:  F = mg sinθ + μs mg cosθ
minimum to prevent sliding down:  F = mg sinθ − μs mg cosθ
💡 Sign ruleFriction always opposes the tendency of motion: pushing a block up ⇒ friction acts down; a block about to slide down ⇒ friction acts up. Flip the sign of the μ mg cosθ term accordingly.
⚠ TrapUse μs for "just about to move / stays at rest" questions and μk for "already sliding / find acceleration" questions. Mixing them is the classic error.
Worked examples
Example · L-3
A block slides down a 37° incline with μk = 0.25. Find its acceleration. (sin 37° = 0.6, cos 37° = 0.8, g = 10)
a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ) = 10(0.6 − 0.25 × 0.8).
= 10(0.6 − 0.2) = 10 × 0.4.
a = 4 m s⁻²
Example · L-3
A 10 kg block rests on a 30° incline. If μs = 0.5, does it slide? What friction force acts on it? (g = 10, tan 30° ≈ 0.577)
tan 30° = 0.577 > μs = 0.5 ⇒ it would slide... check: mg sinθ = 100 × 0.5 = 50 N; limiting f = μsmg cosθ = 0.5 × 100 × 0.866 = 43.3 N.
Driving 50 N > limit 43.3 N ⇒ slides. Once moving, kinetic friction acts (here take f as the limiting/kinetic value ≈ 43.3 N up the slope).
It slides; friction ≈ 43.3 N acting up the incline
Example · L-3
A 4 kg block is on a 30° incline with μs = 0.2. Find the minimum force, applied parallel to the incline, needed to prevent it from sliding down. (g = 10, cos 30° = 0.866)
F = mg sinθ − μs mg cosθ (friction helps by acting up).
= 40 × 0.5 − 0.2 × 40 × 0.866 = 20 − 6.93.
F ≈ 13.1 N (up the incline)
L-4

Minimum Pull, Stacked Blocks & Rolling Friction

Least force to move a block on a horizontal surface

pull at angle θ above horizontalF = μmg / (cosθ + μ sinθ)  ·  minimum when tanθ = μ
minimum pulling forceFmin = μmg / √(1 + μ²)  (at θ = λ, the angle of friction)
Why F = μmg/√(1+μ²)
Pulling at angle θ above the horizontal lifts the block slightly, reducing N = mg − F sinθ, so friction to overcome is μ(mg − F sinθ). At the verge of motion F cosθ = μ(mg − F sinθ) ⇒ F = μmg/(cosθ + μ sinθ). The denominator is largest (so F smallest) when its derivative vanishes, i.e. tanθ = μ = tan λ. Substituting gives cosθ + μ sinθ = √(1 + μ²), hence Fmin = μmg/√(1 + μ²). Pulling at the angle of friction always beats pushing horizontally.
★ Take-awayPull a load at the angle of friction λ (= tan⁻¹μ), never horizontally — you need the least force, μmg/√(1+μ²).

Stacked (two-block) problems

  • Friction between the blocks drives the upper block; if it cannot supply enough force, the upper block slips.
  • For blocks moving together, the max common acceleration is set by the friction available on the upper block: amax = μ12g.

Rolling friction

  • Rolling friction is far smaller than sliding friction (μrolling ≪ μk) because a rolling body has no sliding at the contact point — only a tiny deformation of the surfaces resists motion.
  • This is why ball bearings, wheels and rollers are used: replacing sliding by rolling drastically cuts the resisting force.
💡 Orderμs > μk > μrolling — hardest to start, easier to slide, easiest to roll.
Worked examples
Example · L-4
A 10 kg block sits on a floor with μ = 0.5. Find the minimum force (and its direction) needed to just move it. (g = 10)
Fmin = μmg/√(1 + μ²) = (0.5 × 10 × 10)/√(1 + 0.25).
= 50/√1.25 = 50/1.118. Applied at θ = tan⁻¹(0.5) ≈ 26.6° above horizontal.
Fmin ≈ 44.7 N at ≈ 26.6° above the horizontal
Example · L-4
Block A (2 kg) rests on block B (3 kg) on a smooth floor. μ between A and B is 0.4. What is the maximum horizontal force on B for the two to move together, and the common acceleration then? (g = 10)
Max friction on A = μ mA g = 0.4 × 2 × 10 = 8 N ⇒ amax = 8/2 = 4 m s⁻².
For both moving together at amax: F = (mA + mB)a = 5 × 4.
Fmax = 20 N; common acceleration = 4 m s⁻²
Example · L-4
A horizontal force of 60 N pushes a 12 kg block moving at constant velocity across a floor. Find μk. (g = 10)
Constant velocity ⇒ applied force = kinetic friction ⇒ 60 = μkN = μk(120).
μk = 60/120.
μk = 0.5
★ STRONG BOX

High-Yield Traps & Quick Recall

  • Friction opposes the tendency of relative motion, not the body's velocity — it can even drive a body forward (e.g. the friction that accelerates a car's driving wheels acts forward).
  • Static friction does no net work in rolling without slipping (contact point is instantaneously at rest).
  • μ has no units and no dimensions; typical values 0.1–1, but it can exceed 1 (rubber on rough concrete).
  • Friction is independent of apparent contact area and (for kinetic) of sliding speed; it depends on N and surface nature only.
  • Three equal numbers: μs = tan(angle of friction) = tan(angle of repose).
⚠ TrapOn an incline, N = mg cosθ, not mg. Forgetting the cosθ is the single most common friction-problem mistake.
★ Fast checksFrictionless incline: a = g sinθ. Block just at rest on incline: tan θ = μs. Minimum pull on the flat: μmg/√(1+μ²).
Worked examples
Example · Strong Box
A body takes twice as long to slide down a rough 45° incline as it would on a smooth 45° incline of the same length. Find μk.
Smooth: a₁ = g sin45°. Rough: a₂ = g(sin45° − μ cos45°). Same length s, time ratio t₂/t₁ = 2 ⇒ a₁/a₂ = 4 (since s = ½at²).
sin45°/(sin45° − μ cos45°) = 4 ⇒ 1/(1 − μ) = 4 ⇒ 1 − μ = 1/4.
μk = 0.75
Example · Strong Box
A 5 kg block moving at 10 m s⁻¹ on a rough floor (μk = 0.4) comes to rest. How far does it travel? (g = 10)
Deceleration a = μkg = 0.4 × 10 = 4 m s⁻².
v² = u² − 2as ⇒ 0 = 100 − 2(4)s ⇒ s = 100/8.
s = 12.5 m
