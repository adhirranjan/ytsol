L-1

Chemical Equilibrium & its Dynamic Nature

Reversible vs irreversible reactions

  • Reversible reaction: reactants are not fully converted to products; proceeds in both directions (⇌), can be started from either side, is never complete, and attains equilibrium (mixture always holds both reactants & products). Examples: CH₃COOH + NaOH ⇌ CH₃COONa + H₂O, PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g), esterification, H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂O(g) in a closed vessel.
  • Irreversible reaction: reactants completely converted to products; proceeds one way (→), goes to completion, never attains equilibrium. Examples: strong acid + strong base neutralisation, precipitation (AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃), combustion, thermal decomposition in an open vessel.

Equilibrium & its dynamic nature

  • At equilibrium the system loses its tendency to change — pressure, temperature, composition become constant without external stimulation.
  • Dynamic: rates of forward and backward reactions are equal, so there is no net change, yet both reactions continue at the molecular level. For A ⇌ B, rf = rb at equilibrium though molecules keep interconverting.
condition for equilibriumrate of forward reaction (rf) = rate of backward reaction (rb)

Types of equilibrium

  • Physical equilibrium — only phase changes, no chemical change: liquid–vapour (H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂O(g)), solid–liquid at m.p. (H₂O(s) ⇌ H₂O(l)), solid–vapour (I₂, camphor, NH₄Cl sublimation), saturated solution (solute–solvent), gas dissolved in solvent (Henry's law).
  • Chemical equilibrium — established in a chemical process, e.g. H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) ⇌ 2HCl(g).
  • Chemical equilibria are further homogeneous (all species in one phase, e.g. N₂+3H₂⇌2NH₃) or heterogeneous (species in different phases, e.g. CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g); all physical equilibria are heterogeneous).
    • General characteristics of equilibria involving physical processes
    (1) Some observable property becomes constant. (2) Attained only in a closed vessel for systems involving gases. (3) Dynamic — two opposing processes at equal rates. (4) At equilibrium there is a constant concentration expression, e.g. for CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq), [CO₂(aq)]/[CO₂(g)] = constant at constant T — this is the equilibrium constant, whose magnitude measures the extent of the process.
    ⚠ TrapAt equilibrium concentrations become constant, not equal. "Concentrations of reactants = concentrations of products" is false; only the rates are equal.

    L-2

    Law of Mass Action & the Equilibrium Constant

    Approaching equilibrium (A ⇌ B)

    • Forward rate rf = kf[A] falls as [A] drops; backward rate rb = kb[B] rises as [B] builds. When rf = rb, concentrations stop changing — chemical equilibrium.
    • Characteristics: attained only in reversible reactions, only in a closed system; all measurable properties constant; dynamic; approachable from either side; unaffected by a catalyst (it only speeds up attainment).
    at equilibrium (A ⇌ B)kf[A] = kb[B] ⇒ K = kf/kb = [B]/[A] = constant

    Law of mass action & Kc

    • Active mass = molar concentration (mol L⁻¹) for solutions/gases; for pure solids & liquids it is constant (taken as unity). Active mass = w×1000/(Mw×VmL).
    • Law of mass action (Guldberg & Waage, 1864): rate ∝ product of active masses each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.
    law of chemical equilibrium (aA+bB ⇌ cC+dD)Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b = kf/kb
    • Kc has a definite value for a given reaction at a given temperature, independent of: initial concentrations, presence of a catalyst, direction of approach, and presence of inert materials.

    Kc, Kp & units

    • Kc uses molar concentrations (gas & solution phase); Kp uses partial pressures (gas phase only).
    unitsUnit of Kc = (mol/L)Δn = MΔn ; Unit of Kp = (atm)Δng ; Δn = moles(product − reactant)
    💡 ConventionFor a heterogeneous system write Kp (gases); homogeneous liquid → Kc; homogeneous gaseous → both. Solids, pure liquids and the solvent are taken as unity and dropped from the expression: for CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), Kc = [CO₂], Kp = pCO₂.

    Kp–Kc Relation & Applications of Mass Action

    Relation between Kp and Kc

    • Assuming ideal gases (PV = nRT ⇒ P = CRT, so pA = [A]RT), substitute into Kp to get the master relation.
    master relationKp = Kc(RT)Δng , Δng = (moles gaseous products) − (moles gaseous reactants)
    CaseResultExample
    Δng = 0Kp = Kc (both unitless)N₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2NO ; H₂+I₂⇌2HI
    Δng > 0RT>1 ⇒ Kp>Kc ; RT<1 ⇒ Kp<KcPCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂
    Δng < 0RT>1 ⇒ Kp<Kc ; RT<1 ⇒ Kp>KcN₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
    T = 1/R ≈ 12.2 KKp = Kc for any Δng—

    How the value of K changes with the way a reaction is written

    • Reversed reaction: K′ = 1/K.
    • Multiplied by n: K′ = Kn (e.g. halved coefficients ⇒ K′ = √K).
    • Added reactions: K = K₁ × K₂. Subtracted reactions: K = K₁ / K₂.
    • K depends only on temperature (Van't Hoff). Catalyst raises kf and kb equally, so K is unchanged.
    Van't Hoff equationKeq = A·e−ΔH/RT ⇒ ln(K₂/K₁) = (ΔH/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    💡 Temperature effectEndothermic (ΔH>0): K increases with T. Exothermic (ΔH<0): K decreases with T.
    Applications of law of mass action
    Type-I (Δn=0): H₂+I₂⇌2HI, Kc = 4x²/[(a−x)(b−x)] = 4x²/(1−x)² when a=b=1; Kp=Kc, unitless. Also N₂+O₂⇌2NO and SO₂+NO₂⇌SO₃+NO. Type-II (Δn>0): PCl₅⇌PCl₃+Cl₂, Kc = x²/[(a−x)V] = x²/[(1−x)V]; N₂O₄⇌2NO₂; 2N₂O₅⇌4NO₂+O₂. Type-III (Δn<0): N₂+3H₂⇌2NH₃, Kc = 4x²V²/[(a−x)(b−3x)³] = 4x²V²/[27(1−x)⁴] when a=1,b=3; 2SO₂+O₂⇌2SO₃. Liquid: esterification Kc = x²/[(a−x)(b−x)] (no volume term, unitless). Heterogeneous: drop pure solids — CaCO₃(s)⇌CaO(s)+CO₂(g), Kp=pCO₂; NH₄HS(s)⇌NH₃(g)+H₂S(g), Kp=pNH₃·pH₂S.

    (i) Reaction reversed ⇒ K′ = 1/K = 1/100 = 0.01.

    L-4

    Reaction Quotient, Free Energy & Direction of Reaction

    Applications of the equilibrium constant

    • Extent of reaction: Kc > 10³ ⇒ products predominate (near completion); Kc < 10⁻³ ⇒ reactants predominate; 10⁻³–10³ ⇒ both present appreciably. K says nothing about the rate at which equilibrium is reached.

    Reaction quotient Q & direction

    • Q has the same form as K but is evaluated at any instant (Qc with concentrations, Qp with pressures).
    predicting directionQc < Kc → forward (more product) · Qc > Kc → reverse · Qc = Kc → at equilibrium
    Worked illustration
    For H₂+I₂ ⇌ 2HI with [H₂]=0.1, [I₂]=0.2, [HI]=0.4 M at 783 K: Qc = (0.4)²/(0.1×0.2) = 8. Since Kc = 46 > Qc, the reaction moves right (forms more HI) until Qc = Kc.

    Calculating equilibrium concentrations (ICE method)

    • Step 1 balance; Step 2 build an Initial–Change–Equilibrium table in terms of x; Step 3 substitute in K and solve for x (pick the chemically sensible root); Step 4 back-substitute; Step 5 verify.

    Equilibrium constant & free energy (Van't Hoff isotherm)

    van't Hoff reaction isothermΔG = ΔG° + 2.303 RT log Q ; at equilibrium ΔG = 0, Q = K ⇒ ΔG° = −2.303 RT log K
    • K > 1 ⇒ ΔG° < 0 (forward favoured); K < 1 ⇒ ΔG° > 0 (backward favoured); K = 1 ⇒ ΔG° = 0.
    Worked examples from the module
    Book Example · L-4
    Calculate ΔG° for 3/2 O₂(g) ⇌ O₃(g) at 298 K, given Kp = 2.47 × 10⁻²⁹.
    ΔG° = −2.303 RT log Kp = −2.303 × 8.314 × 298 × log(2.47 × 10⁻²⁹).
    log(2.47 × 10⁻²⁹) = −28.61 ⇒ ΔG° = −(5706)(−28.61) = +1.632 × 10⁵ J.
    ΔG° ≈ +163.2 kJ mol⁻¹ (non-spontaneous, as expected)
    ⚠ Book misprintThe module prints ΔG° = −163.2 kJ mol⁻¹, which has the wrong sign. Since Kp = 2.47 × 10⁻²⁹ ≪ 1 (log negative), ΔG° = −2.303 RT log Kp must be positive: +163.2 kJ mol⁻¹. Ozone formation from O₂ is non-spontaneous, so a negative ΔG° is impossible here.
    Book Example · L-4

    L-5

    Le Chatelier's Principle

    ★ StatementWhen a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, pressure/volume or temperature, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that opposes (minimises) the change.

    Effect of concentration

    • Add reactant / remove product ⇒ Q < K ⇒ shifts forward (more product).
    • Add product / remove reactant ⇒ Q > K ⇒ shifts backward (more reactant).

    Effect of pressure (P ∝ 1/V)

    • Increase pressure (decrease volume) ⇒ shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
    • Decrease pressure (increase volume) ⇒ shifts toward the side with more moles of gas.
    • If Δng = 0 ⇒ pressure change has no effect (Q stays equal to K).

    Effect of temperature

    • Raising T shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction (absorbs heat); lowering T shifts toward the exothermic direction. Temperature is the only factor that also changes the value of K.

    Effect of an inert (non-reacting) gas

    • At constant volume: no effect — partial pressures/concentrations of reacting species unchanged, Q = K.
    • At constant pressure: volume rises, so shifts toward the side with more moles of gas (Δn≠0). Catalyst never shifts equilibrium — it only reaches it faster.
    Le Chatelier applied to physical equilibria
    Endothermic dissolution (NH₄Cl, urea, glucose; ΔH>0): solubility increases with T. Exothermic dissolution (NaOH; ΔH<0): solubility decreases with T. Boiling point rises with pressure (Water⇌Vapour: ↑P favours the smaller-volume liquid). Ice⇌Water: ↑P favours water (Vice>Vwater), so m.p. of ice is lowered by pressure. Gas dissolution (Henry's law): ↑P favours dissolution. For CaCO₃(s)⇌CaO(s)+CO₂(g), adding solid does nothing, but increasing volume shifts right to keep pCO₂ constant.

    Degree of Dissociation & Vapour Density

    • Degree of dissociation α = fraction of one mole that has dissociated at equilibrium (0 ≤ α ≤ 1). For a dissociation with an increase in number of moles, the molar mass / vapour density of the mixture falls as α rises.
    from ideal gas lawm = ρRT/P ; V.D. = m/2 ; at fixed V, T the mixture density is fixed ⇒ V.D. ∝ 1/(total moles)
    moles ratio ↔ densities(total moles at eq)/(moles taken initially) = (V.D.)initial/(V.D.)eq = Minitial/Meq
    degree of dissociation (A ⇌ n B)α = (MT − MO) / [MO(n−1)] = (DT − DO) / [DO(n−1)]
    • MT, DT = theoretical (undissociated) molar mass / vapour density; MO, DO = observed values at equilibrium. Special case N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ (n=2): α = (D − d)/d, where D = initial and d = equilibrium V.D.
    • For an association nA ⇌ An: α = (MT − MO) / [MO(1/n − 1)].

    Simultaneous & sequential equilibria

    • Simultaneous equilibrium: two or more equilibria in one vessel share a common species; its concentration is the total from all equilibria — e.g. A(s) ⇌ X(g)+Y(g) and B(s) ⇌ Z(g)+Y(g), where [Y] = (x+y).
    • Sequential equilibrium: product of one step is reactant of the next — A(s) ⇌ B(g)+C(g) then C(g) ⇌ D(g): Kc1 = (x)(x−y), Kc2 = y/(x−y).
    💡 NoteThe vapour-density method gives α only for reactions where the number of gaseous moles changes (Δn≠0) and Kp can be written. It cannot be applied to solids or to Δn = 0 reactions.
    L-7

    Ionic Equilibrium — Electrolytes & Degree of Ionisation

    • Electrolyte: a substance that furnishes ions in solution and conducts current. Non-electrolyte: does not ionise (urea, sucrose, CCl₄, C₂H₅OH in water).
    ClassificationDescription & examples
    Strong electrolyte (α ≈ 1)Almost completely ionised, highly conducting. Strong acids (H₂SO₄, HCl, HNO₃, HClO₄, HBr, HI); strong bases (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂, CsOH); all soluble salts (NaCl, KCl, CuSO₄).
    Weak electrolyte (α < 1)Partially ionised, poorly conducting. Weak acids (CH₃COOH, HCN, HCOOH, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₃); weak bases (NH₄OH, Cu(OH)₂, Zn(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃).
    True electrolyteIonic even as pure solid/melt — conducts in fused state (NaCl, KCl).
    Potential electrolyteNon-conducting when pure but ionises in a high-dielectric solvent (HCl in H₂O).
    ⚠ TrapStrong/weak is solvent-dependent: NaCl is strong in water but weak in alcohol/benzene; CH₃COOH is weak in water but strong in liquid NH₃. Always specify the solvent.

    Degree of dissociation / ionisation (α)

    definitionα = (moles of electrolyte dissociated) / (moles of electrolyte taken initially)
    • Factors: (1) Nature of electrolyte — weak 1–10%, strong 90–100%. (2) Nature of solvent — higher dielectric constant ⇒ higher α; water (78.39) is the best ordinary solvent. (3) Dilution — α increases on dilution, → 1 at infinite dilution. (4) Temperature — dissociation is endothermic, so α rises with T (weak electrolytes; strong barely affected). (5) Common ion — a common ion from a strong electrolyte suppresses α.
    • α is a thermodynamic (not kinetic) property.
    EffectResult on NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
    Common-ion effect (add NH₄Cl → NH₄⁺)[NH₄⁺] rises ⇒ equilibrium shifts backward ⇒ α decreases.
    Odd-ion effect (add HCl → H⁺)H⁺ removes OH⁻ ⇒ [OH⁻] falls ⇒ equilibrium shifts forward ⇒ α increases.

    Ionic equilibrium & levelling effect

    • Ionic equilibrium: dynamic balance between undissociated electrolyte and its ions: AxBy(s) ⇌ xAy+ + yBx−.
    • Levelling effect: all strong acids react almost completely with water to give H₃O⁺, so they appear equally strong (levelled to H₃O⁺); strong bases are levelled to OH⁻. Their relative strengths cannot be compared in water.

    Acid–Base Theories & Strength Trends

    ConceptAcidBase
    Arrheniusfurnishes H⁺ (H₃O⁺) in waterfurnishes OH⁻ in water
    Brønsted–Lowryproton (H⁺) donorproton acceptor
    Lewiselectron-pair acceptor (electrophile)electron-pair donor (nucleophile)

    Arrhenius concept

    • Explains acidic non-metal oxides (CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, N₂O₃, P₂O₃) and basic metal oxides (CaO, Na₂O, NH₃). Water is amphoteric (H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻); neutralisation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
    • Strength: weak acid HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]; weak base BOH ⇌ B⁺ + OH⁻, Kb = [B⁺][OH⁻]/[BOH]. Larger Ka/Kb ⇒ stronger.
    • Basicity of an acid = number of replaceable H⁺ (mono/di/tribasic). Note H₃PO₃ is dibasic and H₃PO₂ is monobasic (one/two P–H bonds are non-ionisable). Acidity of a base = number of OH⁻.
    • Limitations: fails for non-aqueous media (dry HCl), solvent-free neutralisation (NH₃+HCl→NH₄Cl), salts like AlCl₃/BF₃; and H⁺ actually exists as hydrated H₃O⁺.

    Brønsted–Lowry concept

    • Every acid–base reaction has two conjugate pairs: Acid₁ + Base₂ ⇌ Base₁ + Acid₂. Conjugate base = acid − H⁺; conjugate acid = base + H⁺.
    • Amphiprotic species donate or accept a proton (H₂O, HCO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, NH₃). Autoprotolysis: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻.
    • Strength: stronger acid ⇒ weaker conjugate base. For oxy-acids of the same central atom, higher oxidation number ⇒ stronger (HClO₄>HClO₃>HClO₂>HClO).

    Lewis concept

    • Acid = electron-pair acceptor (BF₃, incomplete-octet molecules, molecules with empty d-orbitals, cations, electron-sextet O/S). Base = electron-pair donor (NH₃, amines, anions, multiple-bond molecules). Product = a coordinate adduct.
    • Lewis acidity of BX₃: observed BF₃<BCl₃<BBr₃<BI₃ (opposite to electronegativity) because B–F π back-bonding is strongest, most saturating B's deficiency. Limitation: cannot rank protonic acids (HCl, H₂SO₄) and predicts slow neutralisation.
    General strength trends
    Covalent hydrides (acidity): across a period rises with electronegativity (CH₄<NH₃<H₂O<HF); down a group rises with size (HF<HCl<HBr<HI, H₂O<H₂S<H₂Se<H₂Te). Oxy-acids: stronger with higher electronegativity/oxidation number of the central atom (H₂SO₃<H₂SO₄, HNO₂<HNO₃). Basic strength of alkali/alkaline-earth hydroxides increases down the group (LiOH<NaOH<KOH<RbOH<CsOH); for group-15 hydrides NH₃>PH₃>AsH₃>SbH₃.

    L-9

    Water, Kw, the pH Scale & Ostwald's Law

    Properties of water

    • Molarity of water = 1000/18 = 55.55 M (density 1 g/cc).
    • H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻, K = [H⁺][OH⁻]/[H₂O]; since [H₂O] is constant, Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Dissociation is endothermic, so Kw rises with temperature.
    • Degree of dissociation of water α = [H⁺]/55.55 = 10⁻⁷/55.55 ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁹. True ionisation constant K = Kw/55.55 = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ ⇒ pK = 15.74.
    ionic product of waterKw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ (25 °C) · in pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ M

    The pH scale (Sørensen)

    pH definitionpH = −log[H⁺] · pOH = −log[OH⁻] · pH + pOH = 14 (25 °C)
    • pH = 7 neutral; pH < 7 acidic; pH > 7 basic (all at 25 °C). The scale runs 0–14 for common solutions.

    pH of strong-acid / strong-base solutions

    • If [H⁺] from the acid > 10⁻⁶ M, water's contribution is negligible ⇒ [H⁺] = normality of the acid. Below 10⁻⁶ M the water term must be added.
    • Mixtures: two strong acids [H⁺] = (N₁V₁ + N₂V₂)/(V₁+V₂); acid + base, if N₁V₁ > N₂V₂ → acidic with [H⁺] = (N₁V₁ − N₂V₂)/(V₁+V₂), else basic.

    pH of a weak acid — Ostwald's dilution law

    Ostwald dilution law (HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻)Ka = cα²/(1−α) ≈ cα² (α≪1) ⇒ α = √(Ka/c) · [H⁺] = √(Ka·c) · pH = ½(pKa − log c)
    • Valid only when α < 0.1 (≈10%); otherwise solve the full quadratic Ka = cα²/(1−α). On dilution c↓ ⇒ α↑ and [H⁺]↓ ⇒ pH↑.
    • Weak + strong acid: strong acid contributes C₁; weak acid's α is suppressed (common-ion), total [H⁺] = C₁ + C₂α. Two weak acids: [H⁺] = √(C₁Ka1 + C₂Ka2).
    Polyprotic acids, isohydric solutions & relative strength
    Polyprotic (diprotic H₂A): since Ka2 ≪ Ka1, essentially all H⁺ comes from the first ionisation — treat like a monoprotic acid using Ka1. Isohydric solutions have equal [H⁺] on mixing, so Ka1C₁ = Ka2C₂. Relative strength of two weak acids of equal concentration = √(Ka1/Ka2). Modern unit: pKa = −log Ka (higher pKa ⇒ weaker acid).
    ⚠ TrapBelow 10⁻⁶ M, a "strong acid" solution is not given by pH = −log(conc). For 10⁻⁸ M HCl the water H⁺ dominates and pH ≈ 6.98 (weakly acidic), never 8.

    Book Example · L-9
    Calculate the pH of a 10⁻⁸ M HCl solution.
    Water H⁺ cannot be neglected: let water give x, then [H⁺] = 10⁻⁸ + x, [OH⁻] = x, and Kw = x(x + 10⁻⁸) = 10⁻¹⁴.
