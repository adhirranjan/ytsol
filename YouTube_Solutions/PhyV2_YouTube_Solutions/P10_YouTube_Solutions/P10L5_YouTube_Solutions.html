1In the context of the “Globe of Death”, what primarily prevents the motorcyclist from falling? (a) Air drag (b) Engine force (c) Friction and normal reaction (d) Electrostatic force — (Level-1)What holds the rider up in the Globe of DeathDeath Well | Application of Circular Motion | Rotational Dynamics Class 12 HSC | Lecture 4🟡 SimilarcFriction and normal reaction
Concept In the globe (or well) of death the rider circles the inside of a vertical wall. The wall can only push inwards, perpendicular to itself, so the normal reaction is horizontal and supplies the centripetal force — it cannot hold the rider up. What stops the rider sliding down is the vertical friction between tyre and wall, and because friction is capped at μN, a large N (i.e. a high speed) is what makes a large friction possible.
Formula Horizontal: N = mv²/r. Vertical: f = mg with f ≤ μN, giving vmin = √(rg/μ). The two forces do different jobs: N turns the rider, f carries the weight — which is why the answer names both.
Solution
1. Draw the rider on the inside of the vertical wall: weight mg down, normal reaction N horizontally inwards, friction f vertically up along the wall.
2. Horizontally there is an acceleration v²/r towards the axis, and only N points that way, so N = mv²/r.Friction is parallel to the wall, hence vertical here, and contributes nothing radially.
3. Vertically there is no acceleration, so f = mg exactly — the friction is static and takes whatever value the weight demands, up to μN.
4. Hence (c) friction and normal reaction is the pair that keeps the rider up and on the circle.
5. (a) air drag is negligible and in any case acts backwards along the motion, not upwards; (b) engine force only maintains speed against drag; (d) electrostatic force is irrelevant to a steel wall and rubber tyre.Notice the trap in (b): the engine keeps the speed up, and speed does matter — but only indirectly, through N.
6. That indirect link is the real physics: f ≤ μN = μmv²/r must be at least mg, so the rider must exceed vmin = √(rg/μ). Ride too slowly and the same two forces are present but friction runs out.

If the surface of the bowl is smooth, and the angle made by the radius through the block with the vertical is θ, find the angular speed at which the bowl is rotating. — (Level-3)Block rotating inside a smooth hemispherical bowlA hemispherical bowl of radius R si set rotating abouv its axis of symmetry whichis kept vertica...🟢 Exactω = √(g/(R cosθ))
Concept A ball riding round the inside of a smooth spinning bowl sits at a fixed latitude. Because the bowl is a sphere, the normal reaction points exactly along the radius towards the bowl's centre — a fact that makes the algebra collapse. The horizontal circle the ball traces has radius R sinθ, and that same sinθ cancels out of the horizontal equation, leaving a very clean result.
Formula Vertical N cosθ = mg; horizontal N sinθ = mω²(R sinθ) ⇒ N = mω²R, hence ω = √[g/(R cosθ)]. The bowl is smooth, so N and mg are the only forces — two equations, two unknowns, no friction term.
Solution
1. Locate the ball: the radius through it makes angle θ with the vertical, so its horizontal circle has radius r = R sinθ.
2. The normal reaction on the ball points from the ball towards the bowl's centre, i.e. up and inwards, making angle θ with the vertical.On a spherical surface the normal is always radial — this is the one geometric fact the whole problem turns on.
3. Vertical (no acceleration): N cosθ = mg.
4. Horizontal (centripetal): N sinθ = mω²r = mω²R sinθ, and dividing out sinθ gives N = mω²R — independent of θ altogether.
5. Feed that into step 3: mω²R cosθ = mg ⇒ ω = √[g/(R cosθ)].Mass cancels, so a marble and a ball-bearing settle at exactly the same latitude for a given spin rate.
6. Limits: at ω = √(g/R) we get cosθ = 1, the ball at the very bottom; as ω grows, cosθ = g/(ω²R) shrinks and the ball climbs towards the rim. Below √(g/R) no tilted position exists.

⚡ JEE Trick Tell the two curved-surface results apart by the shape. A sphere/bowl: normal is radial, sinθ cancels, ω = √(g/R cosθ) and N = mω²R. A cone/funnel: nothing cancels that way, and you get v² = gh measured from the vertex. Both appear in this lecture — check whether the surface is curved or straight-sided before writing a single equation.
Answer: ω = √[g/(R cosθ)].

Concept A pendulum released from rest at angle θ swings down and is fastest at the bottom, so the tension is greatest there too. Raising the release angle raises the bottom speed and hence the peak tension; the string survives only while that peak stays under the breaking value. So the whole question reduces to one inequality on the bottom-point tension.

Concept A ball riding the inside of a rotating funnel is a cone problem: gravity and the wall's normal reaction alone must hold it up and turn it. Because the cone's slope is fixed, the circle's radius is tied to the speed — go faster and the ball climbs to a wider radius. The brim is therefore reached at the maximum speed, and that fixes the brim radius. The frequency question then turns on how f varies with v, which is the opposite way round from what most students expect.
Formula For semi-vertical angle α: r = v²tanα/g and f = v/(2πr) = g/(2πv tanα). r grows as v² but the circumference grows with it, so f actually falls as v rises — the source of the “upper or lower limit?” twist.
Solution
1. Data: α = 37° so sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8 and tan37° = 0.75; vmax = 2 m/s; g = 10 m/s².

3. Brim radius: rmax = (2)²(0.75)/10 = 3/10 = 0.3 m.
4. Frequency there: f = v/(2πr) = 2/(2π × 0.3) = 1/(0.3π) = 1.06 ≈ 1 rev/s.
5. Is that an upper or a lower limit? Substituting r: f = g/(2πv tanα), so f decreases as v increases. The largest allowed v therefore gives the smallest f — this is a lower limit on the frequency.Spin slower than 1 rev/s and the required radius exceeds the brim, so the ball flies out over the edge.
6. There is no upper limit in the ideal model: a faster spin simply parks the ball at a smaller radius, deeper in the funnel, which the funnel can always accommodate.

Concept The wall of the well is vertical, so the normal reaction it exerts is horizontal. A horizontal force can bend the rider's path but can do nothing about gravity. The only force with an upward component available at the contact is friction acting along the wall, and it is that friction — not the reaction, not any loss of weight — that keeps the rider from sliding down.
Formula Horizontal: N = mv²/r. Vertical: f = mg, subject to f ≤ μN. Two perpendicular equations, two different forces — separating them is the whole skill in well-of-death questions.
Solution
1. Free body: weight mg downwards, N horizontally inwards from the wall, static friction f vertically upwards along the wall.
2. Vertically the rider neither rises nor falls, so f = mg exactly. Friction is therefore doing the job of balancing the weight — option (d).Static friction is self-adjusting: it takes precisely the value mg, provided that value does not exceed μN.
3. (a) “the force of gravity disappears” is nonsense — gravity is unchanged and is exactly what friction is opposing.
4. (b) “he loses weight somehow” confuses weight with apparent weight; mg is fixed by mass and g and does not depend on how fast he rides.
5. (c) blames the surrounding air, which is negligible and in any case would act backwards along the motion, not upwards.Air resistance opposes velocity; the velocity here is horizontal, so drag cannot support the rider.
6. Speed enters only indirectly: f ≤ μN = μmv²/r must reach mg, so v ≥ √(rg/μ). Ride slower and friction simply runs out — the mechanism is still friction.

Concept Inside the well of death the rider follows the inner surface of a cylinder at a fixed height, tracing the same horizontal circle over and over at essentially constant speed. That is the textbook definition of uniform circular motion — a curved path of constant radius with an acceleration always directed at the axis. The vocabulary of the other options belongs to quite different motions.
Formula ac = v²/r = ω²r, directed towards the axis, supplied here by N = mv²/r. Constant r and constant v are exactly what make the motion “uniform circular”.
Solution
1. Geometry: the wall is a cylinder of fixed radius r, and the rider stays at one height, so the path is a horizontal circle of radius r.
2. Kinematics: the speed is held constant (the engine balances rolling resistance), so only the direction of the velocity changes — the hallmark of circular motion.Uniform speed does not mean zero acceleration; the direction change alone gives ac = v²/r.
3. Hence the answer is (d) circular motion.
4. (a) linear motion would require a straight path — ruled out by the cylindrical wall.

Concept The cage is carried round a vertical circle at the constant speed v = √(gr), and the cage is gimballed so the man always stands upright on his weighing machine. The reading is the normal force from the machine, obtained from the vertical equation at each point — and because v²/r = g exactly, the numbers come out startlingly simple: 2w at the bottom, 0 at the top and w at the sides.
Formula With v²/r = g: bottom N − w = w, top w − N = w, sides (centripetal horizontal) N = w. At the sides the centripetal acceleration is horizontal, so it never enters the vertical equation — the reading is just the weight.
Solution
1. Fix the geometry from the figure: A is the lowest point, E the highest, and C and G the two ends of the horizontal diameter.
2. At A the acceleration is upwards, so NA − w = (w/g)(v²/r) = w ⇒ NA = 2w.
3. At E the acceleration is downwards, so w − NE = w ⇒ NE = 0 — the man is momentarily weightless at the top.v = √(gr) is exactly the critical speed, which is why the top reading falls precisely to zero.
4. At C and G the acceleration is horizontal, so the vertical equation is simply N = w. Statement (b) “reading at G = w” is TRUE.
5. Now test the rest: (a) NA − NE = 2w − 0 = 2w, TRUE; (c) NE/NA = 0/2w = 0, TRUE; (d) NA/NC = 2w/w = 2, TRUE.
