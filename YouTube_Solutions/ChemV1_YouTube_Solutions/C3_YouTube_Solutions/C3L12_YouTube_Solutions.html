13 g of a hydrocarbon on combustion in excess of oxygen produces 8 g of CO₂ and 5.4 g of H₂O. The data illustrates the law of (Level-1) (a) conservation of mass (b) multiple proportions (c) constant proportions (d) reciprocal proportionsConservation of mass, combustion3g hydrocarbon burnt: 8.8g CO2 + 5.4g H2O - which law🟢 Exactaconservation of mass ⚠
Concept Burn a hydrocarbon and every C atom leaves in CO₂, every H atom in H₂O. So weigh the elements back out of the products: if the C and H recovered add up to the hydrocarbon you started with, no mass was created or destroyed — that is the law of conservation of mass. Do not confuse it with constant proportions, which needs the same compound from two sources.
Formula m(C) = m(CO₂) × 12/44 and m(H) = m(H₂O) × 2/18; then check m(C) + m(H) = m(hydrocarbon). 44 g CO₂ carries 12 g C and 18 g H₂O carries 2 g H, so each mass fraction is just the element's share of the molar mass.
Solution
1. Mass of C = 8.8 × 12/44 = 2.4 g.All carbon of the fuel is now locked in the CO₂.
2. Mass of H = 5.4 × 2/18 = 0.6 g.All hydrogen of the fuel is now locked in the H₂O.
3. C + H recovered = 2.4 + 0.6 = 3.0 g = the 3 g of hydrocarbon taken.
4. Every gram is accounted for, so the data illustrate conservation of mass — option (a).
5. Printed typo: the book gives 8 g of CO₂. Then C = 8 × 12/44 = 2.18 g and 2.18 + 0.6 = 2.78 g, i.e. 0.22 g short of 3 g — as printed the data would break the law it asks about.
6. The identical problem at Level-4 Q7 reads 8.8 g of CO₂, which balances exactly; the key letter (a) is unaffected.
 product    mass    element  mass recovered
 CO2        8.8 g   C        8.8 x 12/44 = 2.4
 H2O        5.4 g   H        5.4 x  2/18 = 0.6
                    total    2.4 + 0.6 = 3.0 g
 fuel taken 3.0 g            3.0 = 3.0 -> balanced
 as printed 8.0 g   C = 2.18 2.78 < 3.0 -> deficit
⚡ JEE Trick One combustion, one compound, masses that add up → conservation of mass. Multiple proportions is out: it needs two or more compounds of the same element pair. Constant proportions is out: it needs the same compound from two sources or routes. Reciprocal proportions is out: it needs three elements and two separate ratios. Whenever a question hands you the reactant mass and every product mass, it is testing the balance sheet.
Answer: (a) conservation of mass.

3Carbon combines with hydrogen to form three compounds A, B and C. The percentages of hydrogen in A, B and C are 25, 14.3 and 7.7 respectively. Which law of chemical combination is illustrated? (Level-3) (a) Law of multiple proportions (b) Law of constant proportions (c) Law of reciprocal proportions (d) Law of conservation of massMultiple proportions, hydrocarbonsCarbon + hydrogen 3 compounds: 25, 14.3, 7.7% H🟢 ExactaLaw of multiple proportions
Concept Two elements (C and H) forming three different compounds is the fingerprint of the law of multiple proportions: fix the mass of one element and the masses of the other that combine with it must stand in a simple whole-number ratio. To test it, convert each percentage into "grams of C per 1 g of H". The trap is answering constant proportions — that law compares one single compound from different sources, not three different compounds.
Formula m(C) per 1 g H = %C / %H = (100 − %H)/%H. Only C and H are present, so %C = 100 − %H; dividing by %H fixes hydrogen at 1 g.
Solution
1. Compound A: %H = 25, %C = 75 ⇒ C per 1 g H = 75/25 = 3 g.
2. Compound B: %H = 14.3, %C = 85.7 ⇒ 85.7/14.3 = 6 g.85.7/14.3 ≈ 5.99, i.e. 6 within rounding of the given percentages.
3. Compound C: %H = 7.7, %C = 92.3 ⇒ 92.3/7.7 = 12 g.
4. Masses of C combining with a fixed 1 g of H = 3 : 6 : 12 = 1 : 2 : 4 — a simple whole-number ratio ⇒ law of multiple proportions, option (a).
5. Identify them for confidence: CH₄ has 4/16 = 25% H, C₂H₄ has 4/28 = 14.3% H, C₂H₂ has 2/26 = 7.7% H — methane, ethene, ethyne.Real compounds confirm the arithmetic is not a coincidence.
 compound  %H     %C     C per 1 g H     ratio
 A (CH4)   25.0   75.0   75/25   = 3      1
 B (C2H4)  14.3   85.7   85.7/14.3 = 6    2
 C (C2H2)   7.7   92.3   92.3/7.7 = 12    4

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the tell-tale percentages: 25, 14.3 and 7.7% H are the JEE signatures of CH₄, C₂H₄ and C₂H₂. Count the compounds to pick the law: many compounds, same two elements → multiple; one compound, many sources → constant; three elements cross-linked through a common one → reciprocal. Here "three compounds A, B, C" settles it before you compute anything — the arithmetic is only confirmation.
Answer: (a) Law of multiple proportions.
4A sample of CaCO₃ has Ca = 40%, C = 12% and O = 48%. If the law of constant proportions is true, then the mass of Ca in 5 g of CaCO₃ from another source will be : (Level-4) (a) 2.0 g (b) 0.2 g (c) 0.02 g (d) 20.0 gConstant proportions, percent compositionCaCO3 Ca=40% - mass of Ca by constant proportions🟢 Exacta2.0 g
Concept The law of constant (definite) proportions says a given compound always holds the same elements in the same fixed mass ratio, whatever its source or route of preparation. So the percentages quoted for one sample of CaCO₃ apply unchanged to CaCO₃ from any other source — the phrase "from another source" is the whole point. Its usual rival, multiple proportions, needs two different compounds of the same pair.
Formula mass of element = (% of element ÷ 100) × mass of sample. Percentage composition is fixed for a compound, so it acts as a constant multiplier on whatever sample mass you are given.
Solution
1. Given composition: Ca = 40%, C = 12%, O = 48% (they sum to 100%, so the data are consistent).
2. By constant proportions the second sample is also 40% Ca.Source cannot change the composition of a pure compound.
3. Mass of Ca = 0.40 × 5 = 2.0 g — option (a).The percentage is a fixed multiplier, so it scales directly with sample mass.
4. Sanity check: 2.0 g out of 5 g is two-fifths, and 40% is exactly two-fifths.
5. Cross-check with real masses: Ca/CaCO₃ = 40/100 = 40% ✓, so the quoted data really are CaCO₃.
6. Distractors 0.2 g and 0.02 g are decimal slips; 20.0 g exceeds the 5 g sample and is impossible.
 element   %      mass in 5 g of CaCO3
 Ca        40     0.40 x 5 = 2.0 g
 C         12     0.12 x 5 = 0.6 g
 O         48     0.48 x 5 = 2.4 g
 total     100    2.0 + 0.6 + 2.4 = 5.0 g -> checks
 answer           Ca = 2.0 g, source-independent
⚡ JEE Trick "If the law of constant proportions is true" is a hint, not a hurdle: just multiply the percentage by the new mass. Guard rail — the answer can never exceed the sample mass, which kills (d) 20 g instantly. And read the units: 40% of 5 g is 2.0 g, so (b) and (c) are pure decimal traps. Multiple proportions would need a second compound of Ca and C or O, which is nowhere in the question.
Answer: (a) 2.0 g.

Concept Two compounds, one element pair (Sn and O) — that is the signature of the law of multiple proportions. Fix the mass of one element, here 1 g of tin, and the masses of oxygen combining with it must stand in a simple whole-number ratio. Its rival, constant proportions, compares the same compound from two sources; here the two compositions differ, so it cannot apply.
Formula O per 1 g Sn = %O ÷ %Sn, then form ratio = (O per Sn)A ÷ (O per Sn)B. Dividing by the tin percentage normalises both compounds to the same fixed mass of tin, which is exactly what the law demands before ratios are compared.

2. Compound A: O per 1 g Sn = 21.23/78.77 = 0.2695 g.
3. Compound B: O per 1 g Sn = 11.88/88.12 = 0.1348 g.

1Find the mass of 1 molecule of oxygen (O₂) in amu (u) and in grams.Molecular mass, mole conceptMass of one molecule of O2 (amu and grams)🟢 Exact—
Concept The mass of one molecule in amu (u) is simply its molecular mass — add up the atomic masses. To get the same mass in grams, remember that one mole (6.022 × 10²³ molecules) of O₂ weighs 32 g, so one molecule weighs 32 g divided by Avogadro's number. Equivalently, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g. The trap is quoting 16 u (one atom) instead of 32 u (the O₂ molecule).
Formula m(1 molecule) = M / NA a mole packs NA identical molecules into M grams, so one molecule carries M/NA. Data: O = 16 u, NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g.
Solution
1. Molecular mass of O₂ = 2 × 16 = 32 u — that is already the mass of one molecule in amu.
2. Molar mass ⇒ 1 mol O₂ = 32 g contains 6.022 × 10²³ molecules.The gram–mole bridge: numerically same 32, but for a mole, not a molecule.
3. Mass of 1 molecule = 32 / (6.022 × 10²³) = 5.31 × 10⁻²³ g.
4. Cross-check via the u→g conversion: 32 u × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g/u = 5.31 × 10⁻²³ g ✓ — both routes agree.1 u = 1/NA g by definition, so the two routes must match.
5. State both forms: 32 u = 5.31 × 10⁻²³ g per O₂ molecule.

 check via 1 u         32 x 1.66e-24              5.31e-23 g
 m(O2 molecule) = 32 u = 5.31 x 10^-23 g   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Molecular mass in u and mass of one molecule are the same number — u is defined so that this works; converting to grams is always one division by NA. Handy constant to carry: 1/6.022 ≈ 0.166, so M/NA = M × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g instantly. Traps: writing 16 u (forgetting O₂ is diatomic) and slipping a power of ten — the mass of any small molecule must land near 10⁻²³–10⁻²² g.
Answer: mass of one O₂ molecule = 32 u = 5.31 × 10⁻²³ g.

Concept Three unit systems, one skill: route everything through the atom count. Moles multiply directly by NA; a mass in u divides by the atomic mass in u to give the number of atoms directly (no Avogadro needed!); a mass in grams must first become moles, then multiply by NA. The trap is treating "52 u" like "52 g" — they differ by a factor of NA.

3. (iii) 52 g of He: n = 52/4 = 13 mol.Same division as (ii), but grams put you on the mole scale.
4. N = 13 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 7.829 × 10²⁴ atoms.

4. Round as the board expects: N ≈ 1.81 × 10²⁴ molecules of NH₃.

1Which law states that the mass of reactants equals the mass of products? (a) Law of Constant Composition (b) Law of Conservation of Mass (c) Law of Reciprocal Proportions (d) Law of Multiple ProportionsConservation of mass, definitionLaw of conservation of mass - definition🟡 SimilarbLaw of Conservation of Mass
Concept The four laws of chemical combination are told apart by what they count. Conservation of mass counts a total: whatever mass enters a chemical change leaves it, so mass of reactants = mass of products. The other three count ratios, never sums. The law most often swapped in here is constant composition, which fixes a ratio inside one compound.
Formula total m(reactants) = total m(products), e.g. 40 g A + 60 g B → 100 g AB. Atoms are only rearranged in a reaction, never created or destroyed, so the mass ledger must balance.
Solution
1. Test: the stem equates totals — mass in = mass out. Only a sum-law fits.
2. (a) Constant Composition: one compound, same fixed ratio whatever its source — a ratio, not a total. Reject.
3. (c) Reciprocal Proportions: three elements — B and C each weighed against a fixed mass of A. No sum. Reject.
4. (d) Multiple Proportions: same two elements, two compounds — O per 12 g C = 16 and 32 g, i.e. 1:2. A ratio again. Reject.A 1:2 says nothing about mass balancing.
5. (b) Conservation of Mass: total reactant mass = total product mass — the stem verbatim. Keep.Lavoisier's law, proved by weighing sealed vessels.
6. Check: 2 g H2 + 16 g O → 18 g H2O; 2 + 16 = 18 — only (b) fits.

Concept The law of conservation of mass says the total mass of products is exactly equal to the total mass of the reactants — never greater, never less. In a Statement-type question, test each statement on its own truth first; a false statement can explain nothing, so the pairing options collapse at once.
Formula Σ mass(reactants) = Σ mass(products). A reaction only rearranges atoms, so every atom present at the start is still present at the end — the total mass cannot grow.
Solution
1. Statement-I: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ on strong heating — standard thermal decomposition, so it is true.
2. Statement-II claims product mass is always greater than reactant mass. The law says equal, so it is false."Greater than" would mean matter is created out of nothing, which the law forbids.

9Suppose elements X and Y combine to form two compounds XY₂ and X₃Y₂ when 0.1 mole of former weigh 10 g while 0.05 mole of the latter weigh 9 g. What are the atomic weights of X and Y. (a) 40, 30 (b) 60, 40 (c) 20, 30 (d) 30, 20Molar mass, simultaneous equationsElements X and Y form XY2 and X3Y2; find atomic weights🟢 Exacta40, 30
Concept Each mole-and-mass pair fixes a molar mass: M = mass ÷ moles. Writing each molar mass in terms of the unknown atomic weights x (of X) and y (of Y) turns the chemistry into two simultaneous linear equations. Both compounds contain Y₂, so the 2y terms cancel on subtraction — the system is engineered to solve in one step.
Formula M(XY₂) = x + 2y, M(X₃Y₂) = 3x + 2y, with M = m/n. A formula's molar mass is the sum of its atoms' atomic weights; m/n converts the weighing data into those molar masses.
Solution
1. M(XY₂) = 10 g ÷ 0.1 mol = 100 g/mol ⇒ x + 2y = 100.
2. M(X₃Y₂) = 9 g ÷ 0.05 mol = 180 g/mol ⇒ 3x + 2y = 180.Divide mass by moles first — never mix the raw grams into the equations.
3. Subtract equation 1 from equation 2: (3x + 2y) − (x + 2y) = 180 − 100 ⇒ 2x = 80 ⇒ x = 40.The identical 2y in both formulas is why subtraction kills y immediately.
4. Back-substitute: 40 + 2y = 100 ⇒ 2y = 60 ⇒ y = 30.
5. Verify in the second compound: 3×40 + 2×30 = 120 + 60 = 180 ✓ — both data are honoured, so atomic weights are X = 40, Y = 30, option (a).
 compound  moles   mass    M = m/n   equation
 XY2       0.10    10 g    100       x + 2y = 100
 X3Y2      0.05     9 g    180       3x + 2y = 180
 subtract: 2x = 80  ⇒ x = 40
 then: 2y = 100 − 40 = 60 ⇒ y = 30

 check: 3(40) + 2(30) = 180 ✓
⚡ JEE Trick When two compounds share an identical block of atoms (here Y₂), subtract the molar-mass equations first — the shared block cancels and the answer drops out mentally: (180 − 100)/2 = 40. Faster still: test option (a) directly, 40 + 60 = 100 ✓ and 120 + 60 = 180 ✓, done. Solution
