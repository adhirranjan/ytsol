1Who discovered the electron, and from which experiment?Discovery of electronA-ELECTRON🟡 SimilarcJ. J. Thomson; cathode-ray discharge tube
Concept The electron was the first sub-atomic particle discovered, and it came out of experiments on electric discharge through gases at very low pressure. When a high voltage is applied across a sealed cathode-ray (discharge) tube, a stream of rays travels from the negative electrode (cathode) to the positive one. J. J. Thomson studied how these cathode rays bent in electric and magnetic fields and concluded they are beams of tiny negatively charged particles — electrons.
Key relation The identifying fact is that cathode rays are independent of the gas or the cathode metal: the same particle appears every time, so it must be a universal constituent of all matter. Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio e/m of these particles and got the same value regardless of the gas — proof of one common particle.
Solution
1. Set up the experiment: a sealed tube with two electrodes at low pressure, high voltage applied → rays stream from the cathode.These are the "cathode rays," named for the electrode they emerge from.
2. Observe the deflection: the rays bend toward the positive plate in an electric field → the particles carry negative charge.
3. Change the gas and the cathode metal — the rays stay identical → the particle is present in every atom.
4. Identify: the discoverer is J. J. Thomson, using the cathode-ray discharge tube — option (c).
 experiment            observation           conclusion
 cathode-ray tube      rays cathode→anode    negative rays exist
 electric field        bend toward +plate    negatively charged
 vary gas / cathode    rays unchanged        electron is universal
⚡ JEE Trick Tie each particle to its experiment: electron → Thomson, cathode rays; proton → Goldstein, anode/canal rays; neutron → Chadwick. Trap: options may swap Thomson (who found the electron) with Rutherford (nucleus) or Goldstein (proton) — anchor on "cathode ray = electron."
Answer: (c) J. J. Thomson; cathode-ray discharge tube.
2Goldstein's anode-ray: smallest positive particle has mass ≈ which atom?Discovery of protonA-PROTON🟡 SimilarbHydrogen
Concept Alongside cathode rays, a discharge tube with a perforated cathode also produces rays travelling the opposite way — from anode toward cathode — called anode rays (canal rays), studied by Goldstein. Unlike cathode rays, their charge-to-mass ratio depends on the gas filling the tube, meaning the positive particles differ from gas to gas. The lightest, smallest positive particle of all is obtained when the tube contains hydrogen, and that particle is the proton.
Key relation The particle with the largest e/m (hence smallest mass for unit positive charge) comes from hydrogen. Hydrogen is the lightest atom; strip its single electron and what remains — a bare H⁺, i.e. a proton — is the smallest possible positive particle.
Solution
1. Anode rays are positive particles formed when gas atoms lose electrons in the tube.
2. Their mass/charge varies with the gas, so the smallest particle is found by choosing the lightest gas.
3. The lightest gas is hydrogen; its positive particle H⁺ (a proton) has the least mass of any anode-ray particle.
4. Therefore the smallest positive particle has a mass ≈ that of a hydrogen atom — option (b).
 gas in tube    positive particle    relative mass
 hydrogen       H⁺ (proton)          1  ← smallest
 helium         He⁺                  4
 nitrogen       N⁺                   14
⚡ JEE Trick "Smallest / lightest positive particle" ⇒ think hydrogen ⇒ proton. Anode-ray properties depend on the gas (unlike cathode rays, which never do) — that contrast is a favourite exam point. Trap: don't pick "electron" — the electron is negative and comes from cathode rays.
Answer: (b) Hydrogen.
3Mass of nucleus ≈ whole atomic mass — what does it imply about mass/charge distribution?Nuclear modelA-RUTHERFORD🟡 SimilarbMass concentrated in the nucleus; charge over the whole atom
Concept In Rutherford's nuclear model the atom's mass is carried almost entirely by its protons and neutrons, which sit together in the tiny central nucleus. Electrons are about 1836 times lighter than a proton, so they add almost nothing to the total mass. Thus if the nucleus mass ≈ the whole atomic mass, the mass must be concentrated in the nucleus, while the electrons (the negative charge) occupy essentially the entire volume of the atom around it.
Key relation atomic mass ≈ mass of nucleus (protons + neutrons), because me ≈ mp/1836. Electrons are so light they contribute a negligible fraction of the mass, yet they spread over the full atomic radius (~10⁻⁸ cm), a hundred-thousand times the nuclear radius.
Solution
1. Compare masses: proton ≈ neutron ≈ 1 u; electron ≈ 1/1836 u ≈ 0.0005 u.Electron mass is negligible next to a nucleon.
2. So the sum of nucleon masses (the nucleus) already accounts for almost all the atomic mass — matching "mass of nucleus ≈ whole atomic mass."
3. Conclusion on mass: it is concentrated in the nucleus (a very small central region).
4. Conclusion on charge: the electrons that carry the atom's negative charge are distributed over the whole atomic volume — so charge is spread over the whole atom, giving option (b).
 particle    location            relative mass   share of atom mass
 protons     nucleus             1 u             ~half
 neutrons    nucleus             1 u             ~half
 electrons   whole atom volume   0.0005 u        negligible
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the two-scale picture: mass + positive charge → tiny nucleus; electrons (volume) → whole atom. The nuclear radius (~10⁻¹³ cm) is ~10⁵ times smaller than the atom (~10⁻⁸ cm), yet holds nearly all the mass. Trap: don't say mass is spread evenly — that is Thomson's discarded plum-pudding view.
Answer: (b) Mass concentrated in the nucleus; charge over the whole atom.
4Thomson embedded electrons in a +sphere — which later result disproved this?Thomson model disprovedA-THOMSON🟡 Similarbα-particle back-scattering (a concentrated nucleus)
Concept Thomson's plum-pudding model pictured the atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge with electrons studded inside like plums. If that were true, positive charge and mass would be spread evenly, so a fast heavy projectile should pass through with only tiny deflections. Rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment demolished this: a few α-particles bounced almost straight back, which is impossible against a diffuse charge and demands a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
Key relation large-angle (≈180°) back-scattering ⇒ concentrated positive nucleus. A back-scatter needs a head-on collision with something both massive and highly charged in a tiny volume — exactly what a diffuse Thomson sphere lacks.
Solution
1. State Thomson's prediction: uniform + charge ⇒ α-particles feel only weak, spread-out forces ⇒ nearly straight paths.
2. Rutherford's observation: most α pass through, but ~1 in 20,000 are deflected by more than 90°, some straight back.Such violent reversals cannot come from a smeared-out charge.
3. Only a concentrated positive mass (the nucleus) can turn an α-particle back → Thomson's even distribution is disproved.
4. The result that disproved Thomson is α-particle back-scattering — option (b).
 model       + charge spread   predicted α paths     matches data?
 Thomson     uniform sphere    all tiny deflections  ✗ (no back-scatter)
 Rutherford  tiny nucleus      few bounce back       ✓
⚡ JEE Trick The single fact "some α-particles bounced straight back" is what killed the plum-pudding model and revealed the nucleus. Trap: cathode rays / canal rays are about electrons & protons, not about disproving Thomson's structure — the structural blow is α-scattering.
Answer: (b) α-particle back-scattering (a concentrated nucleus).
5¹H, ²H, ³H all have one proton — which particle differs, and what does it show?IsotopesA-ISOTOPES🟡 SimilarcNeutrons — mass number depends on nucleons
Concept The three hydrogen species — protium ¹H, deuterium ²H, tritium ³H — are isotopes: atoms of the same element (same number of protons, hence same atomic number Z = 1) but different mass numbers. Since the proton count is fixed, the only thing that can vary between them is the number of neutrons. Adding neutrons raises the mass number A without changing the chemical identity, which is exactly what defines isotopes.
Key relation A = Z + N (mass number = protons + neutrons), with Z = 1 fixed for all three. With Z constant, any change in A must come entirely from N, the neutron number — so mass number "depends on nucleons," i.e. on the neutrons here.
Solution
1. All three have Z = 1 (one proton) → same element, hydrogen.
2. Compute neutrons from N = A − Z:
  • ¹H: N = 1 − 1 = 0 neutron (protium)
  • ²H: N = 2 − 1 = 1 neutron (deuterium)
  • ³H: N = 3 − 1 = 2 neutrons (tritium)
3. The particle that differs is the neutron, and it is the changing neutron count that changes the mass number — option (c).
 isotope   protons(Z)   neutrons(N)   mass number(A)
 ¹H         1            0             1
 ²H         1            1             2
 ³H         1            2             3
⚡ JEE Trick Isotopes = same Z, different A ⇒ they can only differ in neutrons. Electron count is tied to Z (for neutral atoms), so it cannot be the answer. Trap: don't say "protons differ" — that would change the element entirely, not make isotopes.
Answer: (c) Neutrons — mass number depends on nucleons.

1How was the neutron discovered by James Chadwick?Discovery of neutronA-NEUTRON🟡 Similar—
Concept The neutron was the last of the three main sub-atomic particles to be found, because it is electrically neutral and therefore invisible to electric and magnetic fields that were used to detect charged particles. In 1932, James Chadwick bombarded a thin sheet of beryllium with fast α-particles and detected a new, highly penetrating, uncharged radiation. Analysing its ability to knock protons out of paraffin, he showed the radiation was a stream of neutral particles of mass ≈ that of a proton — the neutron.
Key relation The nuclear reaction is ⁹Be + ⁴He → ¹²C + ¹n. Because the new particle carried no charge, it was undeflected by fields and could only be identified by the recoil (proton knock-out) it produced — a momentum/energy argument, not a charge measurement.
Solution
1. Bombard beryllium with α-particles from a radioactive source.
2. A penetrating radiation emerges that is not deflected by electric or magnetic fields → the particle is neutral.
3. Let this radiation strike paraffin (hydrogen-rich); it ejects protons with high energy.Only a particle of comparable mass to a proton can transfer so much energy in a collision — this fixes the neutron's mass ≈ 1 u.
4. Conclude: the new particle is the neutron — neutral, mass ≈ proton mass — completing the atomic model.
 particle   charge   mass       discoverer   how found
 electron   −1       ~0.0005 u  Thomson      field deflection
 proton     +1       ~1 u       Goldstein    anode rays
 neutron     0       ~1 u       Chadwick     Be + α, proton recoil
⚡ JEE Trick Neutron = Chadwick, 1932, Be + α, found late because it is neutral (fields cannot deflect it). Trap: don't credit Rutherford — he only predicted a neutral particle; Chadwick experimentally discovered it.
Key point: Chadwick bombarded beryllium with α-particles (⁹Be + ⁴He → ¹²C + ¹n), obtaining a neutral, highly penetrating radiation of mass ≈ a proton; its inability to be deflected by fields plus its proton-knock-out from paraffin identified it as the neutron.
2Name the sub-atomic particlesSub-atomic particlesA-SUBATOMIC🟡 Similar—
Concept Although the atom was once thought indivisible ("atom" means "uncuttable"), discharge-tube and scattering experiments proved it is built from smaller pieces called sub-atomic (fundamental) particles. The three that make up ordinary atoms are the electron, the proton, and the neutron. Protons and neutrons cluster in the central nucleus; electrons move in the space around it. Knowing their charge and relative mass is the foundation of every calculation on atomic structure.
Key relation atom = nucleus (protons + neutrons) + electrons. Charge: proton +1, electron −1, neutron 0; a neutral atom has equal protons and electrons so the charges cancel.
Solution
1. List the three sub-atomic particles: electron, proton, neutron.
2. Give each its charge and location:
  • Electron — charge −1 (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C), very light, moves outside the nucleus.
  • Proton — charge +1, mass ≈ 1 u, inside the nucleus.
  • Neutron — charge 0, mass ≈ 1 u, inside the nucleus.
3. Note the nucleons (proton + neutron) carry the mass; electrons balance the charge.
 particle   symbol   charge   relative mass   location
 electron   e⁻       −1       ~1/1836 u       outside nucleus
 proton     p⁺       +1       ~1 u            nucleus
 neutron    n        0        ~1 u            nucleus
⚡ JEE Trick Three particles, one mnemonic: e⁻ (−, light), p⁺ (+, heavy), n (0, heavy). Electron is the lightest and least massive; proton and neutron are ~1836× heavier and nearly equal. Trap: "sub-atomic particles" of a normal atom = these three — don't add positron/photon unless the question asks about fundamental particle physics.
Key point: the three sub-atomic particles are the electron (−1, negligible mass, outside the nucleus), the proton (+1, ~1 u, in the nucleus) and the neutron (0, ~1 u, in the nucleus).
3Who discovered (1) electron (2) proton and (3) neutron?Discovery of particlesA-SUBATOMIC🟡 Similar—
Concept Each sub-atomic particle is historically tied to a specific scientist and experiment. The electron came from J. J. Thomson's study of cathode rays; the proton from Goldstein's anode (canal) rays (its nature confirmed by Rutherford); and the neutron from Chadwick's beryllium bombardment by α-particles. Matching particle → discoverer → experiment is a very common one-mark question, so the three pairings are worth memorising as a set.
Key relation electron → Thomson ; proton → Goldstein ; neutron → Chadwick. The order also matches history: electron (1897), proton (~1886 rays / 1919 identified), neutron (1932) — the neutral particle came last because it is hardest to detect.
Solution
1. Electron — discovered by J. J. Thomson (cathode-ray discharge-tube experiment, 1897).
2. Proton — the positive particle seen in Goldstein's anode/canal rays; established as a fundamental positive particle (Rutherford).
3. Neutron — discovered by James Chadwick (α-particles on beryllium, 1932).
 particle   discoverer        experiment / rays
 electron   J. J. Thomson     cathode rays (discharge tube)
 proton     Goldstein         anode / canal rays
 neutron    James Chadwick    Be + α-particles
⚡ JEE Trick Lock the trio: Thomson–electron, Goldstein–proton, Chadwick–neutron. A useful cross-check is charge: negative→Thomson, positive→Goldstein, neutral→Chadwick. Trap: Rutherford discovered the nucleus (and named the proton), but the electron and neutron are Thomson's and Chadwick's respectively.
Key point: electron — J. J. Thomson (cathode rays); proton — Goldstein (anode/canal rays); neutron — James Chadwick (α-particle bombardment of beryllium).
4Match element (⁴⁰Ar, ¹⁴C, ⁴⁰K, ¹⁴N) → number of neutronsNeutron countA-APMN🟡 Similar—
Concept The neutron count of any nuclide is fixed by two numbers: the mass number A (the superscript, = protons + neutrons) and the atomic number Z (protons, read off from the element's identity in the periodic table). Subtract one from the other and you have the neutrons. The only fact you must supply yourself is each element's Z: Ar = 18, C = 6, K = 19, N = 7.
Key relation N = A − Z (neutrons = mass number − atomic number). A counts all nucleons; removing the Z protons leaves exactly the neutrons.
Solution
1. Write each element's atomic number Z: Ar = 18, C = 6, K = 19, N = 7.
2. Apply N = A − Z to each nuclide:
  • ⁴⁰Ar: N = 40 − 18 = 22
  • ¹⁴C: N = 14 − 6 = 8
  • ⁴⁰K: N = 40 − 19 = 21
  • ¹⁴N: N = 14 − 7 = 7
 nuclide   mass no. A   atomic no. Z   neutrons N = A − Z
 ⁴⁰Ar       40           18             22
 ¹⁴C        14           6              8
 ⁴⁰K        40           19             21
 ¹⁴N        14           7              7
⚡ JEE Trick Always N = A − Z; the superscript is A, and Z you read from the periodic table. Notice ⁴⁰Ar & ⁴⁰K are isobars (same A = 40, different Z) while ¹⁴C & ¹⁴N are another isobar pair. Trap: don't confuse the mass number (top-left superscript) with Z — subtract in the right order.
Key point: using N = A − Z — ⁴⁰Ar → 22, ¹⁴C → 8, ⁴⁰K → 21, ¹⁴N → 7 neutrons.

2. A few deflect through large angles → they passed near a concentrated positive charge.
3. Very few bounce back → that positive charge is massive and packed into a tiny volume — the nucleus.
4. Overall conclusion: the atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus — option (c).

4Which statement is correct about canal rays?Canal/anode raysA-PROTON🟡 SimilarcPositively charged

They stream through the holes ("canals") in the opposite direction to cathode rays. Because they are made of positive ions — atoms that have lost electrons — they bend toward the negative plate, proving they carry positive charge. Key relation canal rays → positive ions ⇒ positively charged, gas-dependent. They deflect toward the cathode/negative plate in a field — the signature of positive charge — and their e/m changes with the gas because different gases give different ions.

1. Origin: gas atoms lose electrons in the tube, forming positive ions that pass through the perforated cathode as canal rays.
2. In an electric field they bend toward the negative plate → they are positively charged.
3. Their charge/mass ratio varies with the gas, showing the particles differ from gas to gas (contrast with cathode rays).
4. The correct statement is that canal rays are positively charged — option (c).
 property             cathode rays     canal (anode) rays
 charge               negative         positive  ✓

 direction            cathode→anode    anode→cathode (thru holes)
⚡ JEE Trick Canal / anode / positive rays = positive ions, gas-dependent; cathode rays = electrons, gas-independent. Trap: an option saying "independent of the gas" describes cathode rays — canal-ray mass/charge does depend on the gas used.

2. The cathode rays' properties (e/m) stayed the same in every trial.A gas-independent result signals a particle shared by all atoms.
3. Therefore the particle — the electron — is a universal component of all matter, option (b).

Concept Charge in nature comes in whole-number multiples of one smallest unit, the elementary charge e. Solution
1. The proton's charge is one elementary charge, positive: +e.
2. In SI units e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C.
3. So the charge on one proton = +1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C — option (a).
 particle   charge in units of e   charge in coulomb

 electron   −1                     −1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C

Concept Isotopes are atoms of the same element, so they must have the same number of protons (same Z) — that is what makes them the same element with the same chemistry. What differs is the number of neutrons, which changes the mass number A. In a neutral isotope the electron count also matches the protons, so the defining difference at the particle level is purely the neutrons.
Key relation isotopes: protons equal, neutrons differ (⇒ different A). Same Z fixes the element and its electrons; different N changes only the mass, not the chemistry.
Solution
1. Isotopes belong to one element ⇒ same number of protons (same Z).
2. Their mass numbers differ ⇒ the neutron counts differ (since A = Z + N and Z is fixed).
3. So the correct particle-level difference is "same protons, different neutrons" — option (a).
4. Example: ¹²C (6p, 6n) and ¹⁴C (6p, 8n) — protons identical, neutrons differ.
 isotope   protons   neutrons   same element?
 ¹²C        6         6          yes (Z = 6)
 ¹⁴C        6         8          yes (Z = 6)
 ⇒ same protons, different neutrons
⚡ JEE Trick Isotopes: protons same, neutrons different (so A differs, chemistry stays the same). Trap: options saying "different protons" describe different elements, and "different electrons" would describe ions — the isotope difference is neutrons only.
Answer: (a) Same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
