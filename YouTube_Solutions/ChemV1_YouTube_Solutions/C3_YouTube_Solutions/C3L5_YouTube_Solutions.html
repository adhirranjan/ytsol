1In the balanced equation N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, identify the correct mole ratio of reactants and product: 1:1:1 / 1:2:2 / 1:3:2 / 1:3:3Mole ratio from balanced equationMole Ratio for N₂ + H₂ = NH₃🟡 Similarc1:3:2
Concept The coefficients placed in front of each species in a balanced equation are exactly the mole ratio in which those species react and form. They are not chosen freely — they are fixed by the law of conservation of mass, which demands the same number of atoms of every element on both sides. So once the equation is balanced, reading off the coefficients gives the ratio directly.
Formula mole ratio = coefficient(N₂) : coefficient(H₂) : coefficient(NH₃). A coefficient counts how many moles of that substance take part, so lining up the three coefficients gives the reacting proportion. Balance check: atoms of each element must match on the two sides.
Solution
1. Write the equation with its coefficients: 1N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.
2. Verify it is balanced by counting atoms on each side:If any element is unequal the coefficients are wrong and the ratio would be meaningless.
  • N: left = 1×2 = 2; right = 2×1 = 2 ✓
  • H: left = 3×2 = 6; right = 2×3 = 6 ✓
3. Read the coefficients in order N₂ : H₂ : NH₃ = 1 : 3 : 2, matching option (c).
 species   coefficient   atoms it supplies
 N₂        1             2 N
 H₂        3             6 H
 NH₃       2             2 N + 6 H   (balances both sides)
 ⇒ mole ratio  N₂ : H₂ : NH₃ = 1 : 3 : 2
⚡ JEE Trick The balanced coefficients are the mole ratio — no calculation needed once the equation is balanced. Trap: options like 1:2:2 or 1:3:3 come from mis-balancing hydrogen; always re-count H (6 each side) before trusting a ratio.
Answer: (c) 1:3:2.
2How many moles of CO₂ are produced when 44 g of CO₂ is formed in the decomposition CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂?Grams → molesStoichiometric calculations — mole to mass🟢 Exactb1 mol
Concept To convert a mass of a substance into an amount in moles you only need its molar mass — how many grams sit in one mole. The balanced equation is not even required here, because the question already tells you the mass of CO₂ itself; you simply weigh it in "mole units." This mass→mole step is the backbone of every stoichiometry problem.
Formula n = m ÷ M: moles n = given mass m (g) ÷ molar mass M (g mol⁻¹). Dividing grams by grams-per-mole cancels grams and leaves a pure number of moles. For CO₂: M = 12 + 2×16.
Solution
1. Molar mass of CO₂ = 12 + (2×16) = 12 + 32 = 44 g mol⁻¹.One carbon (12) plus two oxygens (16 each = 32).
2. Apply n = m ÷ M with m = 44 g: n = 44 ÷ 44.
3. n = 1 mol, which is option (b).The given mass equals exactly one molar mass, so it is precisely one mole.
 quantity     value
 mass m       44 g
 molar mass M 44 g mol⁻¹
 moles n      44 / 44 = 1 mol
⚡ JEE Trick When the given mass numerically equals the molar mass, the answer is 1 mole on sight — no long division. Trap: the decomposition equation CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ is a distractor; the question asks only about the CO₂ mass you already have, so no reactant amount is needed.
Answer: (b) 1 mol.

The product H₂S is given by mass, so first convert it to moles; then the balanced equation's coefficients scale that up to moles of H₂SO₄; finally molarity (mol per litre) turns those moles into a solution volume. Each arrow uses exactly one relationship, so keeping them in order prevents mistakes.
Formula n = m ÷ M, then coefficient ratio n(H₂SO₄) = n(H₂S) × 5/1, then V = n ÷ C where C = molarity (mol L⁻¹). Molarity C = moles ÷ litres, so litres = moles ÷ molarity. Equation: 8KI + 5H₂SO₄ → 4K₂SO₄ + 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O.
Solution
1. Molar mass of H₂S = 2×1 + 32 = 34 g mol⁻¹.Two H (1 each) plus one S (32).
2. Moles of H₂S formed: n = 34.0 ÷ 34 = 1 mol.
3. Use the coefficient ratio H₂SO₄ : H₂S = 5 : 1, so moles of H₂SO₄ needed = 1 × 5 = 5 mol.The equation makes 1 mol H₂S for every 5 mol H₂SO₄ consumed.
4. Convert moles of H₂SO₄ to volume of 0.20 M solution: V = n ÷ C = 5 ÷ 0.20.
5. V = 25 L, option (c).Dividing 5 mol by 0.20 mol L⁻¹ gives 25 L.

 moles H₂S         34.0 g ÷ 34 g/mol     1 mol
 moles H₂SO₄       1 mol × (5/1)         5 mol

⚡ JEE Trick Always run solution-stoichiometry as mass → mol → (coefficient) → mol → volume; never skip the coefficient step (the 5:1 here quintuples the acid). Trap: 0.20 M is a small concentration, so a large volume (25 L) is expected — dividing by 0.20 is the same as ×5, easy to fumble as ÷5.

Concept When a decomposition is only partially complete, the product is formed only from the fraction that actually reacted — the untouched CaCO₃ contributes nothing. So the route is: total moles of CaCO₃ → apply the 80% decomposition factor → convert the decomposed moles to CaO through the 1:1 ratio of CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. The classic error is computing the 100% answer and forgetting to scale it down.

Solution
1. Moles of CaCO₃ taken = 10 ÷ 100 = 0.10 mol.
2. Only 80% decomposes: n(reacted) = 0.10 × 0.80 = 0.08 mol.The remaining 0.02 mol stays as unreacted CaCO₃ in the residue.
3. From the 1:1 ratio, n(CaO) = 0.08 × 1 = 0.08 mol.
4. Mass of CaO = n × M = 0.08 × 56 = 4.48 g, option (b).
5. Sanity check: full decomposition would give 0.10 × 56 = 5.6 g; 80% of 5.6 = 4.48 g ✓.Because the ratio is 1:1, the % factor can be applied at any stage — both routes agree.
 quantity                 value
 CaCO₃ taken              10 g ÷ 100 = 0.10 mol
 decomposed (80%)         0.10 × 0.80 = 0.08 mol

 mass of CaO              0.08 × 56 = 4.48 g   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For "x% decomposes" problems, compute the 100% answer and multiply by x/100 at the end — one clean scaling step. Trap: option-setters always include the full-decomposition value (5.6 g) as a distractor; also don't apply 80% to the CO₂ escaping and then double-count it against the CaO.

Concept When one reactant is limiting, it alone fixes how much product forms; any excess of the other reactant simply does not react. To find the "% of Mg reacted" we compare the Mg that actually reacted (found from the H₂ produced) with the Mg originally supplied.

1. Moles of Mg supplied = 12 ÷ 24 = 0.50 mol (MMg = 24).

4. = 0.5 × 100 = 50%, option (b).Exactly half the supplied Mg was used up before HCl ran out.

1Calculate the mass of 100 molecules of water (H = 1, O = 16)Mass of moleculesMass of one molecule of water (H₂O)🟡 Similar—
Concept A single molecule is far too light to weigh directly, so its mass is found by dividing the molar mass (mass of one whole mole) by Avogadro's number NA — the number of molecules in that mole. Once the mass of one molecule is known, the mass of any counted number of molecules is a simple multiplication. The route is molar mass → mass of one molecule → mass of N molecules.
Key relation mass of one molecule = M ÷ NA, then mass of k molecules = k × (M ÷ NA). Here M is the molar mass in g mol⁻¹ and NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹. M ÷ NA shares one mole's mass among its NA molecules, giving the mass of just one.
Solution
1. Molar mass of H₂O = 2×1 + 16 = 18 g mol⁻¹.Two hydrogens (1 each) plus one oxygen (16).
2. Mass of one H₂O molecule = 18 ÷ 6.022×10²³ = 2.99×10⁻²³ g.
3. Mass of 100 molecules = 100 × 2.99×10⁻²³.
4. = 2.99×10⁻²¹ g (equivalently 1800 ÷ 6.022×10²³ g).Multiplying by 100 shifts the power of ten from 10⁻²³ to 10⁻²¹.
 step                 operation                 result
 molar mass H₂O       2(1) + 16                 18 g/mol
 mass of 1 molecule   18 / 6.022×10²³           2.99×10⁻²³ g
 mass of 100          100 × 2.99×10⁻²³          2.99×10⁻²¹ g
⚡ JEE Trick "Mass of one molecule" is always molar mass ÷ NA; for k molecules just carry the k through. Trap: keep track of the power of ten — multiplying 10⁻²³ by 100 (=10²) gives 10⁻²¹, not 10⁻²⁵.
Key point: mass of 100 H₂O molecules = 100 × (18 ÷ NA) = 100 × 2.99×10⁻²³ g = 2.99×10⁻²¹ g.
2What is STP condition? What is the volume of one mole of a gas at STP?STP / molar volumeSTP — 1 mol = 22.4 L for any ideal gas🟡 Similar—
Concept Because the volume of a gas depends on temperature and pressure, chemists agree on a fixed reference set of conditions — STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure) — so that gas volumes can be compared and used in calculations. A key consequence of Avogadro's law (equal volumes of gases at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules) is that one mole of any ideal gas occupies the same volume at STP.
Key relation STP: T = 273.15 K (0 °C), P = 1 atm and at these conditions Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹. Vm, the molar volume, is the same for every ideal gas because it depends only on how many molecules are present, not on their identity. (The modern IUPAC STP of 1 bar gives 22.7 L; the textbook value is 22.4 L at 1 atm.)
Solution
1. State the standard conditions: temperature = 0 °C = 273 K, pressure = 1 atm (101.325 kPa).
2. Apply Avogadro's law: at fixed T and P, a fixed number of molecules occupies a fixed volume, independent of the gas.One mole is a fixed number (NA) of molecules, so its volume is fixed too.
3. That fixed volume for one mole at STP is 22.4 litres.
 condition       standard value
 temperature     273 K  (0 °C)
 pressure        1 atm  (101.325 kPa)
 volume of 1 mol 22.4 L   (any ideal gas)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the pair "STP = 0 °C, 1 atm ⇒ 1 mol = 22.4 L" — it converts moles↔volume in one step (×22.4 or ÷22.4). Trap: 22.4 L holds only at STP; at any other T or P you must use PV = nRT instead of the shortcut.
Key point: STP is 0 °C (273 K) and 1 atm; under these conditions one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
3What is meant by molar volume of a gas?Molar volumeMolar volume of gases: 22.4 L at STP🟡 Similar—
Concept Molar volume is the volume occupied by exactly one mole of a gas under stated conditions. It is the gas-phase counterpart of molar mass: just as molar mass is "grams per mole," molar volume is "litres per mole." Its usefulness comes from Avogadro's law — since one mole always contains NA molecules, one mole of any ideal gas occupies the same volume at the same temperature and pressure.
Key relation Vm = V ÷ n (litres per mole); at STP Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹. Because the volume depends only on the number of molecules, not their kind, Vm is identical for H₂, O₂, CO₂, … at the same T and P.
Solution
1. Define it: molar volume = volume of one mole of gas = V ÷ n.
2. By Avogadro's law, one mole (NA molecules) of any ideal gas occupies the same volume at a given T and P.Gas volume is set by molecule count and conditions, not by the gas's identity.
3. At STP (0 °C, 1 atm) this common volume is 22.4 L, so Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹.
 gas (1 mole at STP)   volume
 H₂                    22.4 L
 O₂                    22.4 L
 CO₂                   22.4 L
 ⇒ molar volume is the same 22.4 L for any ideal gas
⚡ JEE Trick Molar volume = 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP for every ideal gas — use it to jump moles↔volume (n = V ÷ 22.4). Trap: it is a per-mole volume, not per gram; a heavy gas and a light gas both give 22.4 L per mole even though their masses differ.
Key point: molar volume is the volume of one mole of a gas (V ÷ n); at STP it equals 22.4 L mol⁻¹ for any ideal gas.

Concept A mole is a counting unit: one mole of ANY substance contains exactly Avogadro's number, NA = 6.022×10²³, of its formula units — regardless of how heavy each unit is. 4. Contrast (the intended lesson): masses are 17 g vs 63 g — a 17:63 ratio — yet counts are equal.Equal counts with unequal masses is exactly what Avogadro's concept of the mole guarantees.
 substance    moles   molecules            mass

 HNO₃         1       6.022×10²³           63 g

1What does a balanced chemical equation represent? (equal atoms on both sides)Meaning of balanced equationIntro to using balanced chemical equations🟡 SimilarcEqual atoms on both sides
Concept A chemical equation is "balanced" precisely when it obeys the law of conservation of mass: atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction, only rearranged. So the count of each type of atom must be identical on the reactant and product sides. This atom-by-atom equality is what the balancing coefficients guarantee, and it is what makes the equation usable for mole calculations.
Key relation Σ atoms of each element (reactants) = Σ atoms of each element (products). Mass is conserved because atoms are conserved; matching every element's atom count on both sides is the operational test for "balanced."
Solution
1. State the principle: conservation of mass ⇒ same atoms in, same atoms out.
2. Therefore a balanced equation has equal numbers of atoms of every element on both sides — option (c).
3. Check with N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: N = 2 = 2, H = 6 = 6 ✓ — balanced.Both elements match, confirming the definition.
4. Reject distractors: equal molecules or equal coefficients are not required — only equal atoms.
 element   reactant atoms   product atoms   equal?
 N         2                2               ✓
 H         6                6               ✓
 ⇒ balanced: equal atoms of each element both sides
⚡ JEE Trick "Balanced" = equal atoms of each element, never equal moles or equal molecules. Trap: options mentioning "equal number of molecules" or "equal mass of each substance" are wrong — only the total atom count per element is conserved.
Answer: (c) Equal atoms on both sides.
2What are stoichiometric coefficients? (numbers before formulae in a balanced equation)Stoichiometric coefficientsBalancing equations & using stoichiometric coefficients🟡 SimilarcNumbers before chemical formulas in balanced equations
Concept The whole numbers written in front of each formula in a balanced equation are the stoichiometric coefficients. They state how many moles (or molecules) of each species take part, and it is these numbers — not the subscripts inside a formula — that give the reacting proportions. Distinguishing a coefficient (outside, multiplies the whole formula) from a subscript (inside, counts atoms in one formula) is the key idea.
Key relation a A + b B → c C + d D: a, b, c, d are the stoichiometric coefficients. A coefficient multiplies its entire formula, so it fixes the mole amount of that whole species in the reaction. Subscripts (e.g. the 2 in H₂) instead count atoms within one molecule.
Solution
1. Identify the position: coefficients sit before the formula (e.g. the 3 in 3H₂), subscripts sit inside it (the 2 in H₂).
2. Their role: coefficients give the mole ratio of reactants and products — option (c).
3. In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ the coefficients are 1, 3, 2.These say 1 mol N₂ reacts with 3 mol H₂ to give 2 mol NH₃.
 3 H₂
 │  └ subscript 2  → atoms inside one molecule (2 H per H₂)
