1The figure shows a smooth circular path of radius R in the vertical plane which subtends an angle (π/2) at O. A block of mass m is taken from position A to B under the action of a constant horizontal force F. (a) Find the work done by this force. (b) In part (a), if the block is being pulled by a force F which is always tangential to the surface, find the work done by the force F between A and B — (Level-7)Work: constant vs tangential forceWork done by constant and variable force — Class 11 / JEE🟡 Similar(a) W = FR  (b) W = πFR/2
Concept Two forces of the same magnitude F act over the same quarter-circle path, yet they do different work — that is the whole point of this question. A force of constant direction only cares about the straight-line displacement between the endpoints, because ∫F·ds = F·∫ds = F·s can be pulled out of the integral. A force that is always tangential is parallel to ds at every instant, so its work is F times the arc length, which is longer than the chord. Neither calculation needs the mass, gravity or the normal force.
Formula Constant force: W = F⃗·s⃗ = F sx where sx (m) is the displacement component along F and F is in N, W in J. Tangential force: W = ∫F ds = F × (arc length) = F(Rθ) with R in m and θ in rad. N × m = J in both cases; θ in radians is dimensionless, so F·Rθ is still N·m = J. Geometry from the figure: O is the centre, A lies a distance R directly below O, B lies a distance R directly right of O, and the arc AB subtends 90° = π/2 rad at O.
Solution
1. Fix the geometry. Put O at the origin. Then A = (0, −R) and B = (R, 0), and the smooth arc joins them through the fourth quadrant.The dotted lines in the figure mark the right angle at O — that is what tells you A is vertically below O and B horizontally right of O.
2. Displacement A → B: s⃗ = B − A = (R − 0) î + (0 − (−R)) ĵ = R î + R ĵ. Its horizontal component is R and its vertical component is R.
3. Part (a). F⃗ = F î (constant, horizontal, as drawn at A). Then W = F⃗·s⃗ = (F î)·(R î + R ĵ) = F R (J).Only the horizontal part of the displacement counts; the vertical rise R contributes nothing because F has no vertical component.
4. Note what did not matter: the path shape, the smoothness, mg and N. For a constant force, work depends only on the straight-line displacement — so any path from A to B gives the same FR.
5. Part (b). Now F is always tangential: at every point F⃗ ∥ ds⃗, so F⃗·ds⃗ = F ds with no cosine loss anywhere.
6. Integrate along the arc: W = ∫F ds = F ∫ds = F × (arc length AB).
7. Arc length of a 90° arc of radius R: sarc = Rθ = R(π/2) = πR/2 (m).
8. Hence W = πFR/2 ≈ 1.571 FR (J), which is larger than FR by the factor π/2 = 1.571.Sanity: the tangential force never wastes any of itself on a perpendicular component, and it travels the longer curved distance — both effects push its work above FR.

      R │  ╲                │   arc AB = R(π/2)
        │   ╲___            │
        ↓        ╲──╲       │
        A ──────────→ F     │   chord components: Δx = R, Δy = R

⚡ JEE Trick Decide first whether the force has a fixed direction or a fixed relation to the path. Fixed direction ⇒ pull F out of the integral and use the displacement (chord). Trap: writing πFR/2 for part (a) because "the path is an arc" — the path length is irrelevant for a constant force. Answer: (a) W = FR  (b) W = πFR/2.
2A small block of mass 20 kg rests on a bigger block of mass 30 kg, which lies on a smooth horizontal plane. Initially the whole system is at rest. The coefficient of friction between the blocks is 0.5. A horizontal force F = 50 N is applied on the lower block. Is the work done by frictional force on the upper block converted to heat or mechanical energy or both? — (Level-7)Friction work, heat vs KEA small block of mass 20 kg rests on a bigger block of mass 30 kg, which lies on a smooth hori...🟢 Exact−40 J ⚠
Concept The decisive question is do the two blocks slip on each other? Heat is generated by friction only when there is relative sliding at the contact, because the heat equals f × (relative displacement). If the blocks move together, the relative displacement is zero, so friction generates no heat at all — every joule it does on the upper block becomes that block's kinetic energy. Check the "move together" assumption first; everything else follows.
Formula Test for common motion: a = F/(m₁+m₂) (m s⁻²), required friction on the top block freq = m₁a (N), limiting friction fmax = μ m₁ g (N). Heat produced: Q = f × srel (J). Data: m₁ = 20 kg (top), m₂ = 30 kg (bottom), μ = 0.5 between them, floor smooth, F = 50 N on the bottom block, g = 10 m s⁻². N × m = J, and kg × m s⁻² = N — every term is consistent.
Solution
1. Assume they move together and treat the pair as one 50 kg body on a smooth floor: a = 50 N ÷ 50 kg = 1 m s⁻².
2. The only horizontal force on the top block is friction from the bottom block, so it must supply freq = m₁a = 20 × 1 = 20 N forward.
3. Available static friction: fmax = μ m₁ g = 0.5 × 20 × 10 = 100 N. Since 20 N ≪ 100 N, the assumption holds — no slipping.This is the whole crux. Had F been large enough to need more than 100 N, the blocks would slide and heat would appear.
4. Relative displacement at the contact srel = 0 ⇒ heat Q = f × srel = 0 J.
5. Therefore the work friction does on the upper block, W = +20 N × s, goes entirely into the upper block's kinetic energy — it is pure mechanical energy, no heat.Cross-check with the work–energy theorem on the top block alone: after distance s, ½(20)v² = 20s, and v² = 2(1)s from kinematics ⇒ 10(2s) = 20s ✓.
6. Where the key's −40 J comes from. Friction is an action–reaction pair: the top block pushes back on the bottom block with 20 N. In the first 2 s the system covers s = ½at² = ½(1)(2)² = 2 m, so the work done by friction on the lower block is W = −20 N × 2 m = −40 J, exactly the printed key value.
7. Note the bookkeeping: +40 J on the top block and −40 J on the bottom block sum to zero. That zero total is the signature of a non-dissipative (non-slipping) friction pair — friction here merely transfers energy from one block to the other.

Trap: seeing "μ = 0.5" and "friction" and immediately writing Q = μmgs — you must first test 20 N against the 100 N limit. Second trap: the total friction work on a non-slipping pair is zero, so friction here is internal energy transfer, not loss.

3The potential energy function of a particle in a region of space is given as U = (2x² + 3y³ + 2z) joule. Here x, y and z are in metres. Find the force acting on the particle at point P(1 m, 2 m, 3 m) — (Level-7)Force from a 3-D potentialThe potential energy function (in J) of a particle in a region of space is given as U = (2x^2 + 3y^3 + 2z)🟢 ExactF⃗ = −4î − 36ĵ − 2k̂ (N)
Concept For any conservative field the force is minus the gradient of the potential energy: each Cartesian component of F⃗ comes from a partial derivative of U with respect to that coordinate only, treating the other two as constants. So a term like 3y³ contributes nothing to Fx or Fz. The negative sign encodes the physical statement that a body is always pushed downhill in potential energy.
Formula F⃗ = −∇U = −(∂U/∂x)î − (∂U/∂y)ĵ − (∂U/∂z)k̂, with U in J, x,y,z in m, hence F in J m⁻¹ = N. J/m = (N·m)/m = N, so the gradient of an energy is automatically a force — the dimensions take care of themselves. Here U = 2x² + 3y³ + 2z (J), evaluated at P(1, 2, 3) m.
Solution
1. ∂U/∂x: differentiate 2x² and treat 3y³ and 2z as constants ⇒ ∂U/∂x = 4x (N).
2. ∂U/∂y: differentiate 3y³ ⇒ ∂U/∂y = 9y² (N).
3. ∂U/∂z: differentiate 2z ⇒ ∂U/∂z = 2 (N) — a constant, independent of position.
4. Assemble the force field: F⃗ = −4x î − 9y² ĵ − 2 k̂ (N).
5. Substitute P(1, 2, 3): Fx = −4(1) = −4 N; Fy = −9(2)² = −9 × 4 = −36 N; Fz = −2 N.Note z = 3 never gets used — Fz is uniform because U is linear in z, exactly like uniform gravity where U = mgz gives F = −mg.
6. Hence F⃗ = −4î − 36ĵ − 2k̂ N, of magnitude |F⃗| = √(16 + 1296 + 4) = √1316 ≈ 36.3 N, pointing back toward the origin in all three directions.
   U(x,y,z) = 2x² + 3y³ + 2z    (J)

   ∂U/∂x = 4x     → F_x = −4x     at x=1 → −4  N
   ∂U/∂y = 9y²    → F_y = −9y²    at y=2 → −36 N
   ∂U/∂z = 2      → F_z = −2      any z  → −2  N

   F⃗(1,2,3) = −4 î − 36 ĵ − 2 k̂  N     |F⃗| = √1316 ≈ 36.3 N
⚡ JEE Trick Write the general field first (−4x, −9y², −2) and substitute the point only at the end — you then get the whole force field for free, which the follow-up parts of such questions usually ask for. Trap: forgetting the minus sign and quoting +4î + 36ĵ + 2k̂. Second trap: differentiating 3y³ to 3y² instead of 9y² — the coefficient rides along.
Answer: F⃗ = −4î − 36ĵ − 2k̂ N (magnitude ≈ 36.3 N).
4An automobile of mass m accelerates, starting from rest, while the engine supplies constant power P. Show that: (a) the velocity is given as a function of time by v = (2Pt/m)1/2; (b) the acceleration of the car is not constant but is given as a function of time by a = (P/2m)1/2 t−1/2; (c) the position is given as a function of time by s = (8P/9m)1/2 t3/2 — (Level-7)Constant-power kinematicsAn automobile of mass m accelerates, starting from rest, while the engine supplies constant power P🟢 Exactnot in key
Concept "Constant power" is not constant force. Since P = Fv, holding P fixed makes F fall as the car speeds up, so the acceleration decays. The clean route is energy, not force: constant power means the kinetic energy grows linearly with time, which gives v(t) in one line. Differentiate that for a(t), integrate it for s(t). Trying to start from F = ma leads to a messier differential equation.
Formula P = dW/dt = d(½mv²)/dt (W = J s⁻¹); integrate to get ½mv² = Pt (J) since the car starts from rest. Then a = dv/dt (m s⁻²) and s = ∫v dt (m). Check: J = W·s ⇒ ½mv² = Pt has kg·m²s⁻² on the left and W·s = J on the right ✓.
Solution
1. Part (a). Power is the rate of doing work, and on a car starting from rest all of that work becomes kinetic energy: W(t) = Pt (J).
2. Work–energy theorem: ½mv² − 0 = Pt ⇒ v² = 2Pt/m ⇒ v = (2Pt/m)1/2 (m s⁻¹). ✓ part (a).Units: (W·s/kg)^½ = (J/kg)^½ = (m²s⁻²)^½ = m s⁻¹ ✓.
3. Part (b). Differentiate: a = dv/dt = (2P/m)1/2 · d(t1/2)/dt = (2P/m)1/2 · ½ t−1/2.
4. Fold the ½ inside the square root: ½(2P/m)1/2 = (¼ · 2P/m)1/2 = (P/2m)1/2.
5. Hence a = (P/2m)1/2 t−1/2 (m s⁻²). ✓ part (b). It is clearly not constant: a → ∞ as t → 0 (the engine can push very hard when v is tiny) and a → 0 as t → ∞.
6. Part (c). Integrate v from rest: s = ∫₀t(2P/m)1/2 t′1/2 dt′ = (2P/m)1/2 · (2/3) t3/2.
7. Fold the 2/3 inside the root: (2/3)(2P/m)1/2 = ((4/9)(2P/m))1/2 = (8P/9m)1/2.
8. Hence s = (8P/9m)1/2 t3/2 (m). ✓ part (c). Cross-check by differentiating: ds/dt = (3/2)(8P/9m)1/2t1/2 = (9/4 · 8P/9m)1/2t1/2 = (2P/m)1/2t1/2 = v ✓.

5A block of mass m is taken from A to B under the action of a constant horizontal force F. Work done by this force is (the figure shows O with a 90° right angle, R vertically down from O to A, B horizontally right of O, and a circular arc from A to B; F is horizontal at the block) — (a) FR  (b) πFR/2  (c) FR/√2  (d) FR/4 — (Level-7)Work of a constant forceA smooth circular path of radius R which is a quarter of a circle — block taken from A to B under a constant force F, work done🟢 ExactaFR
Concept This is the MCQ twin of Q1(a) with exactly the same figure. A constant force is path-independent by construction — its work is F⃗·s⃗ where s⃗ is the straight-line displacement, no matter how curly the actual route. All four distractors are built from the geometry of the arc (πR/2), the chord (R√2) and a quarter (R/4), and every one of them is a trap for someone who confuses path length with displacement.
Formula W = F⃗·s⃗ = F s cos θ, F in N, s in m, W in J. For a constant force this is exact for any path. The integral ∫F⃗·ds⃗ has F⃗ constant, so it factors out: F⃗·∫ds⃗ = F⃗·s⃗net. Geometry: O is the arc centre; OA = R points vertically down, OB = R points horizontally right; F⃗ is horizontal.
Solution
1. Coordinates with O at the origin: A = (0, −R), B = (R, 0).
2. Displacement s⃗ = B − A = R î + R ĵ (m). Magnitude |s⃗| = R√2 at 45° above the horizontal.
3. The applied force is F⃗ = F î (horizontal, pointing from A toward the right, as drawn).
4. Work: W = F⃗·s⃗ = F î·(R î + R ĵ) = F R (1) + F R (0) = FR (J).Equivalently W = F |s⃗| cos45° = F(R√2)(1/√2) = FR — the two routes must agree, and they do.
5. Test the distractors: (b) πFR/2 is F × arc length, which is only correct if F stays tangential — it does not, it is fixed horizontal. (c) FR/√2 = F|s⃗|/2, a double-counted cosine. (d) FR/4 has no geometric meaning here.
6. So the answer is (a) FR.

6A block of mass 50 kg is projected horizontally on a rough horizontal floor. The coefficient of friction between the block and the floor is 0.1. The block strikes a light spring of stiffness k = 100 N/m with a velocity 2 m/s. The maximum compression of the spring is — (a) 1 m  (b) 2 m  (c) 3 m  (d) 4 m — (Level-7)Spring + friction energy balanceA block of mass 50 kg is projected horizontally on a rough horizontal floor. Concept At maximum compression the block is momentarily at rest, so all of its kinetic energy at the moment of contact has been shared between the spring's stored energy and the heat dumped by friction. Because friction acts over the same distance x that the spring is compressed, the energy equation becomes a quadratic in x — this is the signature of "spring + friction" problems, and you must keep both terms.
Formula ½mv² = ½kx² + μmg x — all terms in J, with m in kg, v in m s⁻¹, k in N m⁻¹, x in m, μ dimensionless. ½kx²: (N m⁻¹)(m²) = N·m = J ✓; μmgx: (kg)(m s⁻²)(m) = N·m = J ✓. Data: m = 50 kg, v = 2 m s⁻¹, μ = 0.1, k = 100 N m⁻¹, g = 10 m s⁻² (the book's numbers only come out clean with g = 10).
Solution
1. Kinetic energy at the instant of contact: ½mv² = ½ × 50 × 2² = 100 J.
2. Spring energy at compression x: ½kx² = ½ × 100 × x² = 50x² (J).
3. Friction force on the floor: f = μmg = 0.1 × 50 × 10 = 50 N; heat over distance x: f·x = 50x (J).The spring is "light", so its own weight adds nothing to the normal force; N = mg throughout.
4. Energy balance at maximum compression (v = 0): 100 = 50x² + 50x.
5. Divide by 50: 2 = x² + x ⇒ x² + x − 2 = 0.
6. Factorise: (x + 2)(x − 1) = 0 ⇒ x = −2 m or x = +1 m. Discard the negative root (a compression cannot be negative).
7. So the maximum compression is x = 1 m — option (a).Sanity check: with no friction, ½kx² = 100 J would give x = √2 ≈ 1.41 m. Friction must make it smaller, and 1 m < 1.41 m ✓.

7The potential energy U of a particle of mass m = 1 kg moving in the xy-plane is given by U = 2x + 5y − xy. The cross term −xy is what makes this interesting: it couples the axes, so ∂U/∂x contains y and ∂U/∂y contains x. Miss that and you get option (a).

Solution
1. Partial with respect to x (hold y fixed): ∂U/∂x = 2 − y.
2. Partial with respect to y (hold x fixed): ∂U/∂y = 5 − x.
3. Substitute y = −2 into step 1: ∂U/∂x = 2 − (−2) = 4 ⇒ Fx = −4 N.The double negative is the classic slip here: 2 − (−2) = 4, not 0.
4. Substitute x = 2 into step 2: ∂U/∂y = 5 − 2 = 3 ⇒ Fy = −3 N.
5. Magnitude: |F⃗| = √((−4)² + (−3)²) = √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5 N — a 3-4-5 triangle, the giveaway that the numbers were engineered.
6. With m = 1 kg, a = 5 N ÷ 1 kg = 5 m s⁻² — option (d), directed along (−4, −3)/5, i.e. into the third quadrant.

19The work done on a particle of mass m by a force K[x/(x²+y²)3/2 î + y/(x²+y²)3/2 ĵ] (K being a constant of appropriate dimensions), when the particle is taken from the point (a, 0) to the point (0, a) along a circular path of radius a about the origin in the x–y plane, is — (a) 2Kπ/a  (b) Kπ/a  (c) Kπ/(2a)  (d) 0 (JEE Advanced 2013, Level-7) — (PYQ)Central force on a circular arcJEE Advanced 2013 — work done by K[x/(x²+y²)^{3/2} î + …]🟢 Exactd0
Concept Rewrite the force and everything becomes obvious: xî + yĵ = r r̂ and (x²+y²)3/2 = r³, so F⃗ = (K/r²) r̂ — a plain inverse-square central force. On a circular arc centred at the origin, the displacement is purely tangential while the force is purely radial, so F⃗·dr⃗ = 0 at every single point. The work is zero without any integration at all.
Formula Identity: xî + yĵ = r r̂, (x²+y²)3/2 = r³ ⇒ F⃗ = (K/r²) r̂. On a circle of fixed radius, dr⃗ = r dθ θ̂ and r̂·θ̂ = 0. Equivalently, potential U(r) = K/r and W = −ΔU = K(1/ri − 1/rf) (J). K/r must be an energy, so K is J·m ✓, and K/r² is then J m⁻² × m … = N ✓.
Solution
1. Simplify the force: F⃗ = K(xî + yĵ)/(x²+y²)3/2 = K(r r̂)/r³ = (K/r²) r̂ — inverse-square and radial.
2. The path is a circular arc of radius a about the origin, from (a, 0) to (0, a) — a quarter circle in the first quadrant.
3. On that arc r is constant, so every displacement element is tangential: dr⃗ = a dθ θ̂.
4. Dot product: F⃗·dr⃗ = (K/a²)(a dθ)(r̂·θ̂) = 0, since r̂ ⊥ θ̂ everywhere.
5. Integrating zero gives zero: W = 0 — option (d).
6. Second route (potential): the force is central, hence conservative, with U(r) = K/r. Start and end are both at r = a ⇒ ΔU = K/a − K/a = 0 ⇒ W = −ΔU = 0 J ✓. The circular path was not even needed — any path from (a,0) to (0,a) gives zero.Two independent arguments landing on the same answer is the strongest kind of check.

Concept The single relation dK/dt = γt determines everything.
