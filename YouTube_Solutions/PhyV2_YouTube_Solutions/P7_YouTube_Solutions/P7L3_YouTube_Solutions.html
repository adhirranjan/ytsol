At angle of repose α, which of the following is true? (a) Limiting friction = 0 (b) Driving force = 0 (c) Limiting friction = Driving force (d) Frictional force > Driving force
P7L3VSuggQ1Angle of repose
🟡 SimilarAngle Of Friction &…
cLimiting friction = Driving force
Concept The angle of repose α is the inclination at which a block placed on the plane is just on the verge of sliding down. On the verge means the block is still in equilibrium, but static friction has reached its maximum (limiting) value.
Formula On an incline θ: N = mg cosθ, driving force F = mg sinθ, limiting friction f_L = μ mg cosθ. Both f_L and F change with θ — f_L falls (cosθ ↓) while F rises (sinθ ↑), so they must cross at exactly one angle.
Solution
1. At θ = α the block is in equilibrium along the plane, so ΣFalong = 0 → f = F.Equilibrium is what "at rest" means; the friction that acts equals the driving force.
2. At θ = α it is also on the point of slipping, so the friction acting is the limiting value: f = f_L.Combining the two: f_L = F, i.e. option (c).
3. Substituting: μ mg cosα = mg sinα → tanα = μ → α = tan⁻¹μ.
⚡ JEE Trick Angle of repose = angle of friction, both equal tan⁻¹μ. The classic trap is (b): "driving force = 0" is wrong — mg sinα is at its largest sustainable value there, not zero. (a) is wrong for the same reason.
Answer: (c) Limiting friction = Driving force, i.e. tanα = μ

Find the acceleration of the block; initially the block is at rest. A 10 kg block sits on a rough horizontal floor (μ = 0.5) and a 40 N force is applied at 37° above the horizontal, pulling it. Take g = 10 m/s².
P7L3VSuggQ2Force at an angle
🟡 SimilarFind Acceleration of…
Zero (a = 0)
Concept A pull tilted upward lifts part of the weight off the floor, so it reduces N and therefore reduces limiting friction. You must first test whether the block moves at all before writing F = ma.
Formula N = mg − F sinθ, f_L = μN; block moves only if F cosθ > f_L. sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8.
Solution
Given m = 10 kg, μ = 0.5, F = 40 N at 37°, at rest initially, g = 10 m/s²
Find the acceleration of the block
1. Vertical: N = mg − F sin37° = (10)(10) − (40)(0.6) = 100 − 24 = 76 N.The 24 N upward component is carried by the rope, not the floor.
2. Limiting friction: f_L = μN = 0.5 × 76 = 38 N.
3. Horizontal driving force: F cos37° = 40 × 0.8 = 32 N.
4. 32 N < 38 N, so the block cannot break loose. Static friction adjusts itself to 32 N and the net force is zero.Static friction is a "whatever it takes, up to μN" force — never assume it equals μN.
5. Therefore a = 0 m/s².
⚡ JEE Trick Always compute f_L after correcting N for the vertical component of the applied force. The trap answer is a = (32 − 50)/10 using N = mg = 100 N, which wrongly suggests the block never moves for a different reason — or a = (32 − 38)/10 = −0.6, a negative acceleration that is physically impossible for a body starting from rest.
Answer: a = 0 (the block stays at rest)

Find the frictional force on the block. A block of mass m = 10 kg is held against a vertical wall by a horizontal force F = 2 kN pressing it into the wall; μ = 0.1 between block and wall. (g = 10 m/s²)
P7L3VSuggQ3Friction on a vertical wall
🟡 SimilarSolving Friction Pro…
100 N (upward)
Concept Against a vertical wall the normal reaction is horizontal and equals the pressing force; the weight is what friction has to hold up. Friction is static here, so it equals the weight — not μN — provided μN is big enough.
Formula N = F, f_L = μN; if mg ≤ f_L then f = mg (upward).
Solution
Given m = 10 kg, F = 2 kN pressing into wall, μ = 0.1, g = 10 m/s²
Find the frictional force on the block
1. N = F = 2 kN = 2000 N.The wall pushes back exactly as hard as it is pushed — the block has no horizontal acceleration.
2. f_L = μN = 0.1 × 2000 = 200 N.
3. Weight to be supported: mg = 10 × 10 = 100 N.
4. 100 N < 200 N, so the block does not slide; static friction takes the value needed for vertical equilibrium: f = mg = 100 N, directed upward.
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is answering 200 N (= μN). μN is only the ceiling. Static friction on a wall always equals the weight until the weight exceeds μN — after which the block slides and friction becomes kinetic.
Answer: f = 100 N, acting vertically upward

Find the frictional force if the block is at rest on the given curved surface. A block M = 10 kg rests on the inside of a parabolic surface x² = 2y at the point (1, (1/2)); μ = 5/4. (g = 10 m/s²)
P7L3VSuggQ4Friction on a curved surface
🟡 SimilarBlock sliding off a…
100/√2 N ≈ 70.7 N
Concept A curved surface is just an incline whose angle changes from point to point. The local incline angle θ satisfies tanθ = dy/dx at that point. Once θ is known the problem is an ordinary "block at rest on an incline" problem.
Formula tanθ = dy/dx; at rest f = mg sinθ provided mg sinθ ≤ μ mg cosθ.
Solution
Given M = 10 kg inside parabola x² = 2y at (1, 1/2), μ = 5/4, g = 10 m/s²
Find the frictional force if the block is at rest
1. From x² = 2y, y = x²/2 → dy/dx = x. At x = 1: dy/dx = 1 → tanθ = 1, θ = 45°.The tangent line to the parabola at (1, (1/2)) makes 45° with the horizontal — that is the effective incline.
2. Check that the block can stay: μ = 1.25 and tanθ = 1, so tanθ < μ → it is below the angle of repose, so rest is possible.
3. At rest, friction balances the down-slope component: f = Mg sin45° = (10)(10)(1/√2) = 100/√2 N ≈ 70.7 N, directed up the slope.
4. Sanity check: f_L = μMg cos45° = 1.25 × 100/√2 = 125/√2 ≈ 88.4 N > 70.7 N. ✓
⚡ JEE Trick For any curve, "local angle" = arctan(dy/dx). The trap is plugging in μ and writing f = μN = 88.4 N; but the block is at rest, so friction is static and equals mg sinθ, which is smaller.
Answer: f = 100/√2 N ≈ 70.7 N up the surface

m₁ = 10 kg rests on a 37° rough incline (μ = 0.5) and is joined by a string over a pulley at the top to a hanging mass m₂. (i) Find the minimum and maximum values of m₂ for which both blocks remain unmoved. (ii) Find the value of m₂ for which the friction force between m₁ and the incline is zero. (g = 10 m/s²)
P7L3VSuggQ5Incline–pulley, range of m₂
🟡 SimilarTwo Blocks Connected…
(i) 2 kg & 10 kg (ii) 6 kg
Concept Static friction can point either way. If m₂ is small, m₁ tends to slide down and friction acts up; if m₂ is large, m₁ tends to be dragged up and friction acts down. The two extreme cases give the two limits.
Formula T = m₂g, N = m₁g cos37° , f_L = μN, equilibrium: T ± f = m₁g sin37°. sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8.
Solution
Given m₁ = 10 kg on 37° incline, μ = 0.5, hanging mass m₂, g = 10 m/s²
Find (i) min and max m₂ for rest; (ii) m₂ giving zero friction
1. Down-slope pull on m₁: m₁g sin37° = 10 × 10 × 0.6 = 60 N.
2. N = m₁g cos37° = 10 × 10 × 0.8 = 80 N → f_L = 0.5 × 80 = 40 N.
3. Minimum m₂: friction fully up the slope. T + 40 = 60 → T = 20 N → m₂(min) = 20/10 = 2 kg.Below 2 kg the string cannot hold m₁; it slides down.
4. Maximum m₂: friction fully down the slope. T = 60 + 40 = 100 N → m₂(max) = 100/10 = 10 kg.Above 10 kg the hanging mass drags m₁ up.
5. Zero friction: the string alone must balance the slope component, T = 60 N → m₂ = 6 kg.6 kg sits exactly midway between 2 and 10 — the centre of the "no-motion band".
⚡ JEE Trick The band of equilibrium is always centred on the "friction = 0" value and is ±f_L/g wide in mass: 6 ± 40/10 = 6 ± 4 → 2 kg to 10 kg. Get the centre and half-width and both answers fall out at once.
Answer: (i) m₂(min) = 2 kg, m₂(max) = 10 kg (ii) m₂ = 6 kg

A body starting from rest slides over a plane inclined at 60° to the horizontal. After a displacement of 40 cm the body has velocity 2 m/s. What is the coefficient of friction? (g = 9.8 m/s²)
P7L3VBoardQ1μ from kinematics on an incline
🟡 SimilarHow to Solve Coeffic…
μ ≈ 0.712
Concept Two-stage problem: kinematics gives the acceleration, then Newton's second law along the incline converts that acceleration into μ.
Formula v² = u² + 2as and a = g(sinθ − μ cosθ). The block is sliding, so friction is kinetic and acts up the slope, opposing motion.
Solution
Given θ = 60°, start from rest, s = 40 cm, v = 2 m/s, g = 9.8 m/s²
Find the coefficient of friction
1. u = 0, v = 2 m/s, s = 0.40 m → a = v²/(2s) = 4/(2 × 0.40) = 5 m/s².
2. a = g(sin60° − μ cos60°) → 5 = 9.8(0.8660 − 0.5μ).
3. 5/9.8 = 0.5102 → 0.8660 − 0.5μ = 0.5102 → 0.5μ = 0.3558.
4. μ = 0.7116 ≈ 0.712 (dimensionless).μ < tan60° = 1.73, so the block does indeed slide — consistent.
⚡ JEE Trick Rearrange once and reuse: μ = (g sinθ − a)/(g cosθ). Watch the units — 40 cm must become 0.40 m, otherwise a comes out 100× too small.
Answer: μ ≈ 0.712

A uniform ladder of length 6 m and mass 25 kg leans with its upper end against a smooth vertical wall and its lower end on rough ground. The bottom of the ladder is 4 m from the wall. Calculate the frictional force between the ladder and the ground. (g = 10 m/s²)
P7L3VBoardQ2Ladder equilibrium (torque)
🟡 SimilarLadder leaning again…
118.8 N ⚠
Concept A smooth wall can only push horizontally (no friction there). So the only horizontal force at the base is friction, and it must exactly balance the wall's push. Take torques about the foot to find that push.
Formula Horizontal: f = N_wall. Torque about the foot: N_wall · h = W · (b/2), where h is the wall-contact height and b the base distance.
Solution
Given L = 6 m, m = 25 kg, wall distance 4 m, smooth wall, g = 10 m/s²
Find the frictional force between ladder and ground
1. Height reached on the wall: h = √(6² − 4²) = √(36 − 16) = √20 = 4.472 m.
2. Weight W = 25 × 10 = 250 N, acting at the ladder's midpoint, i.e. horizontally 4/2 = 2 m from the foot.
3. Torques about the foot: N_wall × 4.472 = 250 × 2 = 500 → N_wall = 500/4.472 = 111.8 N.Taking the foot as pivot kills both unknown ground reactions in one stroke.
4. Horizontal equilibrium: f = N_wall = 111.8 N.The wall is smooth, so it contributes no vertical force and no other horizontal force exists.
⚡ JEE Trick For a uniform ladder against a smooth wall, f = W b /(2h) = (W/2)·cot(angle with ground). Here (250/2) × (4/4.472) = 111.8 N in one line. Key defect: the ICAD key prints 118.8 N — the digits 1 and 8 are transposed.
Answer: f = 111.8 N (printed key: 118.8 N — see ⚠)

Which factor decreases when the angle of inclination θ increases? (a) Driving force (b) Normal reaction (c) Coefficient of friction (d) Weight of the block
P7L3V1Q1Incline: how N, F vary with θ
🟡 SimilarFriction on Inclined…
bNormal reaction
Concept On an incline the weight splits into mg sinθ (down the slope, the driving force) and mg cosθ (into the slope, balanced by N). As θ grows, sinθ grows and cosθ shrinks.
Key relation N = mg cosθ ↓, F = mg sinθ ↑, f_L = μ mg cosθ ↓, μ and mg are constants.
Solution
1. (a) Driving force mg sinθ increases with θ — not the answer.
2. (b) Normal reaction mg cosθ decreases as θ goes 0° → 90° (cos falls from 1 to 0). ✓
3. (c) μ is a property of the two surfaces only — it does not depend on θ at all.This is the most commonly mis-picked option.
4. (d) Weight mg is fixed by mass and gravity; tilting the plane cannot change it.
⚡ JEE Trick Only two things vary with θ: sinθ up, cosθ down. Anything containing cosθ (N, f_L) falls; anything containing sinθ (driving force) rises. μ and mg never move.
Answer: (b) Normal reaction

Two cars of unequal masses use similar tyres. If they are moving at the same initial speed, the minimum stopping distance (a) is smaller for the heavier car (b) is smaller for the lighter car (c) is same for both cars (d) depends on the volume of the car
P7L3V1Q4Stopping distance vs mass
🟢 ExactTwo cars of unequal…
cis same for both cars
Concept The braking force is friction, which is proportional to mass; the resistance to deceleration is also proportional to mass. The two m's cancel, so the deceleration — and hence the stopping distance — is mass-independent.
Formula μmg = ma → a = μg, then s = v²/(2a) = v²/(2μg).
Solution
1. Maximum retarding force without skidding = μN = μmg.
2. Newton's second law: μmg = ma → a = μg — mass cancels.Exactly the same cancellation that makes all bodies fall at the same rate.
3. s = v²/(2a) = v²/(2μg): depends only on v and μ, both of which are stated to be the same ("similar tyres", "same initial speed").
4. Therefore the stopping distance is the same for both cars ✓ option (c). Volume is irrelevant, killing (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever the only horizontal force is friction from the ground, mass always cancels. Same reason: max speed on a curve, angle of repose, and time to slide down an incline are all mass-independent.
Answer: (c) The stopping distance is the same for both cars

In order to stop a car in the shortest distance on a horizontal road, one should (a) apply the brakes very hard so that the wheels stop rotating (b) apply the brakes hard enough to just prevent slipping (c) pump the brakes (press and release) (d) shut the engine off and not apply brakes
P7L3V1Q5Static vs kinetic friction in braking
🟢 ExactHC Verma — In order…
bapply the brakes hard enough to just prevent slipping
Concept A rolling tyre contacts the road with static friction; a locked, skidding tyre uses kinetic friction. Since μs > μk, rolling-without-slipping gives the larger retarding force and therefore the shorter stop.
Key relation s = v²/(2μg); larger μ ⟹ smaller s, and μ_s > μ_k.
Solution
1. Option (a) locks the wheels → skidding → kinetic friction μk, the smaller coefficient → longer distance. Also steering is lost.
2. Option (b) keeps the tyres on the verge of slipping → static friction at its maximum μsmg → shortest distance. ✓This is exactly what ABS does electronically — it releases just before lock-up.
3. Option (c) pumping means the friction is at its maximum only part of the time → average retardation is lower.
4. Option (d) leaves only rolling resistance and air drag — by far the longest distance.
⚡ JEE Trick "Just prevent slipping" is code for "use μs, the bigger one". Remember μs ≥ μk always — it is why ABS exists and why the answer is never "lock the wheels".
Answer: (b) Apply the brakes hard enough to just prevent slipping

The angle between the resultant contact force and the normal force exerted by a body on the other is called the angle of friction. If λ is the angle of friction and μ the coefficient of static friction, then λ ≤ tan⁻¹(nμ), where n = ?
P7L3V2Q5Angle of friction, definition
🟢 ExactHC Verma Friction Ch…
n = 1
Concept The contact force R is the vector sum of N (perpendicular) and f (along the surface). Its tilt from the normal is λ, with tanλ = f/N. Since static friction can be anything from 0 up to μN, λ ranges from 0 up to tan⁻¹μ.
Key relation tanλ = f/N, and f ≤ μN ⟹ tanλ ≤ μ ⟹ λ ≤ tan⁻¹(μ).

2. f: for θ ≤ α it equals mg sinθ, a rising sine; at θ = α it switches to μkmg cosθ, which then falls as a cosine. So f rises, peaks near α, then decays. Graph (b) ✓There is also a small downward jump at α because μk < μs.
3. F = √(N² + f²): for θ ≤ α this is √(mg²cos²θ + mg²sin²θ) = mg, constant; beyond α it becomes mg cosθ√(1 + μk²), a falling cosine. Graph (c) ✓
4. Graph (d) keeps F = mg all the way to π/2, which contradicts step 3 — it is wrong. ✗
⚡ JEE Trick The beautiful bit: while the block is static, the contact force must exactly balance the weight, so F = mg regardless of θ — a flat line. The instant it slips, F drops below mg. Spotting that flat segment is the whole question.
