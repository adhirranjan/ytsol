1What is the primary requirement for calculating COM of a body with removed section? (a) Moment of inertia (b) Location and area of the removed section (c) Time duration (d) WeightCavity method essentialsCentre of Mass Complete Lecture | Cavity Problems in Centre of Mass | Remaining Portion | #JEE #NEET🟡 SimilarbLocation and area of the removed section
Concept A body with a hole is handled by superposition: the full body equals the remaining body plus the removed piece, so the remaining body equals the full body minus a piece of negative area sitting exactly where the hole is. To run that subtraction you need only two facts about the hole — how much area (mass) it carries and where its own centroid sits. Moment of inertia is a second-moment quantity and never enters a first-moment (COM) calculation; weight and time are irrelevant because for a uniform lamina density cancels top and bottom of the ratio.
Formula xCOM = (A1x1 − A2x2) / (A1 − A2). Uniform thickness and density make m ∝ A, so areas may replace masses everywhere — the ρt factor cancels.
Solution
1. Write the whole body as the sum of what stays and what goes: A1x1 = (A1−A2)xrem + A2x2, hence xrem = (A1x1 − A2x2)/(A1−A2).This identity is the whole chapter — every cavity problem is just this line with numbers.
2. Read off the inputs the formula demands: A1, x1 (known from the full shape's symmetry) and A2, x2 — the area of the removed section and the location of its centroid. Nothing else appears.
3. Test option (a): moment of inertia is Σmiri2, a second moment. It changes when the hole moves but is never needed to find where the COM sits — reject.Students confuse “removed portion” problems in rotation (where I is needed) with COM problems; the two chapters share a figure, not a formula.
4. Test option (d): weight W = mg. Since g is common to every element it cancels in the ratio, exactly as ρ and t do — so weight adds no information beyond area. Reject.
5. Test option (c): the configuration is static; no rate or time appears anywhere in the first-moment definition. Reject.
6. Only option (b) supplies both missing quantities — A2 and (x2,y2) — so (b) is the answer.Note the phrase “location and area”: one without the other is not enough, which is why (c) “subtracting areas only” style options fail elsewhere in this set.
  full body  =  remaining part  +  removed piece
  A1 * x1    =  (A1-A2)*x_rem   +  A2 * x2
  ------------------------------------------
  need:  A2  (how big is the hole)
         x2  (where is the hole's own centre)
  do NOT need: I, W, t, rho
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the word “removed”, which fires the rotational-motion reflex “I = Ifull − Ihole” and drags option (a) in. Decode the rest: (c) time is dimensional nonsense here, (d) weight is proportional to mass so it cancels and adds nothing new. Whenever an option offers a pair (size + position) in a first-moment question, it is almost always the intended one.
Answer: (b) Location and area of the removed section.
2A body has two segments with areas A1 = 4 m2, A2 = 6 m2, and x-coordinates x1 = 2 m, x2 = 5 m. Find xCOM. (a) 3.8 m (b) 4.2 m (c) 3.0 m (d) 5.0 mArea-weighted COMCENTRE OF MASS OF TWO PARTICLES SYSTEM🟡 Similara3.8 m
Concept Two joined segments — nothing is removed — so this is a plain weighted average, and both areas enter with a plus sign. The COM must land strictly between x = 2 m and x = 5 m, and because the heavier segment (6 m2) sits at x = 5 m the answer must be pulled past the midpoint 3.5 m toward 5 m. The naive mistake is to average the coordinates, (2+5)/2 = 3.5 m, which throws away the fact that the two pieces are not equally large.
Formula xCOM = (A1x1 + A2x2) / (A1 + A2). For a lamina of uniform thickness and density, m = ρtA, so ρt cancels and area is a legitimate stand-in for mass.
Solution
1. Both segments are present, so use the sum form: xCOM = (4×2 + 6×5)/(4 + 6) = (8 + 30)/10 = 38/10 = 3.8 m.Plus signs, not minus — the “−” version of this formula is only for a cavity, and using it here would give (8−30)/(4−6) = 11 m, far outside the body.
2. Sanity-check the bracketing: 2 m < 3.8 m < 5 m, so the COM lies inside the span of the two segments as it must for two positive masses.
3. Check the bias direction: the fraction of total area on the right is 6/10 = 0.6, so the COM should sit 60% of the way along the 3 m gap from x1: 2 + 0.6×3 = 3.8 m — the same number by a different route.This “fraction of the gap” shortcut is the fastest way to do any two-body COM mentally.
4. Kill option (c): 3.0 m would need the left segment to be the heavier one; it is the lighter one, so 3.0 m is on the wrong side of even the midpoint.
5. Kill option (b): 4.2 m = 2 + 0.733×3 corresponds to the split 6:4 read backwards, i.e. area 6 assigned to x = 2 and area 4 to x = 5 — the classic reciprocal slip.Weights go with their own coordinate; the lever-arm intuition (heavier mass sits closer to COM) is what makes students swap them.
6. Kill option (d): 5.0 m is the coordinate of segment 2 alone — it would require A1 = 0. Only (a) survives.

  ----+------+----+-----
   1  |  4   | 2  |   8
   2  |  6   | 5  |  30
  ----+------+----+-----
  sum |  10  |    |  38   ->  x_cm = 38/10 = 3.8 m

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the word “segments” in a chapter about removed portions — it tempts you into the minus formula. Read the verb: nothing is removed here, so both terms are positive. Decode the distractors: (c) 3.0 m is below even the unweighted midpoint 3.5 m, (b) 4.2 m is the 4↔6 swap, and (d) 5.0 m ignores segment 1 entirely.

35 particles of equal mass are fixed at the vertices of a regular hexagon of side 2 m. Find the distance from the centre of the hexagon to the centre of mass of the system.Symmetry with one mass missingShift of Centre of Mass When a portion of mass is Removed JEE and NEET Concept in 5 Minutes🟡 Similar0.4 m
Concept Do not set up six coordinates and add sines and cosines. Use the cavity idea in its point-mass form: six equal masses at the vertices of a regular hexagon are perfectly symmetric, so their position vectors measured from the centre cancel exactly. Taking one away is the same as adding a negative mass at that vertex, so the whole unbalanced first moment is just the one missing vector. The obvious alternative — averaging five awkward vertex coordinates — gives the same answer but wastes five minutes and invites sign errors.
Formula rCOM = Σmiri / Σmi, with the six-vertex identity Σ6 ri = 0. The six vertices are equally spaced by 60°, so their unit vectors form a closed regular polygon and sum to zero.
Solution
1. Fix the geometry: for a regular hexagon the circumradius equals the side, so R = a = 2 m — every vertex is 2 m from the centre O.A regular hexagon is six equilateral triangles glued at O; that is why R = a, a fact worth memorising.
2. Take O as origin. Full six-particle system: rCOM = m(Σ6ri)/(6m) = m(0)/(6m) = 0, i.e. the COM of all six sits exactly at O.
3. Remove the particle at vertex 6: the surviving sum is Σ5ri = Σ6ri − r6 = 0 − r6 = −r6, a single vector of magnitude R = 2 m.Subtracting a vector from zero is where the “negative mass” picture pays off — no trigonometry is ever written down.
4. Divide by the surviving mass: d = |−m r6| / (5m) = R/5 = 2/5 = 0.4 m, directed from O away from the empty vertex.The direction matters in follow-up parts: the COM always runs away from the hole, never toward it.
5. Cross-check by brute force on coordinates. With vertices at 2(cos60k°, sin60k°) and the one at 0° missing, Σx = 2(cos60°+cos120°+cos180°+cos240°+cos300°) = 2(0.5−0.5−1−0.5+0.5) = −2, and Σy = 0 by up–down symmetry.Σy vanishing confirms the COM lies on the diameter through the empty vertex.
6. Hence xCOM = −2/5 = −0.4 m, so the distance from the centre is 0.4 m — matching step 4 and confirming the key.
  regular hexagon: R = a = 2 m
  all 6 masses  ->  sum of r_i = 0   (COM at O)
  drop one      ->  sum of r_i = -r_6 ,  |r_6| = 2 m
  d = |sum| / (number left) = 2 / 5 = 0.4 m
  direction: from O, opposite the empty vertex
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is reading “side 2 m” and hunting for a circumradius with a √3 in it — that is the apothem (a√3/2 = √3 m), not the vertex distance. In a hexagon R = a exactly. The second trap is dividing by 6 instead of 5: only five particles remain, and 2/6 = 0.33 m is the classic wrong answer. General result worth carrying: remove one of n symmetric equal masses and the COM shifts by R/(n−1).
Answer: 0.4 m from the centre, on the side opposite the vacant vertex.
4A circular plate of uniform thickness has a diameter of 60 cm. A circular portion of diameter 30 cm is removed from one edge of the plate as shown in fig. Find the position of the centre of mass of the remaining portion.Disc with edge cavityShift of Centre of mass of a disc when portion of it is removed from a Edge JEE and NEET Concept🟡 Similar5 cm from C, opposite the cavity
Concept Treat the holed plate as the full disc plus a disc of negative area placed exactly over the hole. Everything happens on the line joining the two centres, so this is a one-dimensional calculation and yCOM = 0 by reflection symmetry about that line. The single geometric fact that must be extracted from the figure is where the small disc's own centre sits: because the cut-out touches the rim, its centre is at R − r from C, not at R and not at r. Getting that offset wrong is the only real difficulty in the problem.
Formula xCOM = (A1x1 − A2x2) / (A1 − A2). Uniform thickness makes m ∝ A, so π and the density cancel and only the area ratio survives.
Solution
1. Convert diameters to radii: R = 60/2 = 30 cm for the plate, r = 30/2 = 15 cm for the removed disc.The question gives diameters; using 60 and 30 as radii is the single most common error and inflates every later number.
2. Locate the cavity centre. Its rim touches the plate's rim, so its centre lies at x2 = R − r = 30 − 15 = 15 cm from C, and the full plate has x1 = 0 with C as origin.
3. Areas: A1 = πR2 = 900π cm2, A2 = πr2 = 225π cm2, so the hole is exactly one quarter of the plate.Halving the diameter quarters the area — a useful check that keeps the arithmetic honest.
4. Substitute: xCOM = (900π×0 − 225π×15)/(900π − 225π) = −3375π/675π = −5 cm.The minus sign is the physics: the COM moves to the side away from the missing material, which the figure shows as the left of C.
5. Repeat with the closed form to confirm: d = r2(R−r)/(R2−r2) = r2/(R+r) = 225/(30+15) = 225/45 = 5 cm.Because R2−r2 factorises as (R+r)(R−r), the (R−r) cancels — memorise d = r2/(R+r) for every edge-cavity disc.
6. Check the y-coordinate: both centres lie on the same horizontal line, so yCOM = (900π×0 − 225π×0)/675π = 0. The COM is 5 cm from C along CO produced backwards, still well inside the remaining metal.
  R = 30 cm,  r = 15 cm,  hole centre at R - r = 15 cm
  A1 = 900*pi     x1 = 0
  A2 = 225*pi     x2 = +15
  x_cm = (0 - 225*15) / (900 - 225) = -3375/675 = -5 cm
  shortcut: d = r^2/(R+r) = 225/45 = 5 cm
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit side by side. First, diameter-for-radius: using R = 60, r = 30 gives 10 cm, exactly double. Second, putting the cavity centre at R = 30 cm instead of R − r = 15 cm, which gives 10 cm as well — so a doubled answer never tells you which mistake you made. Lock in d = r2/(R+r) and both traps disappear; here it is 152/45 = 5 cm in one line.
Answer: 5 cm from the centre C, on the side away from the removed portion (i.e. 5 cm to the left of C).

So part (ii) is not a second calculation — it is part (i) re-read from a shifted zero. Everything lies on one line, so the vector formula collapses to a single weighted average, and the answer is forced to lie strictly between 3 m and 9 m, closer to the heavier 6 kg mass. The obvious wrong move is to average the distances, (3+9)/2 = 6 m, which ignores the unequal masses.
Formula xCOM = (m1x1 + m2x2) / (m1 + m2). Both masses are physically present, so both terms carry a plus sign — the minus form belongs only to removed portions.

1A uniform square lamina is placed such that its center lies at the origin. A smaller square piece is removed from its top-right corner. After removing the piece, how does the position of the centre of mass of the remaining lamina change? (a) It shifts towards the top-right direction (b) It shifts towards the bottom-left direction (c) It remains at the origin (d) It shifts only along the x-axisDirection of COM shiftFig shows a uniform square plate from which one or more of the four identical squares at the corners🟡 SimilarbIt shifts towards the bottom-left direction
Concept Removing material is equivalent to superposing a piece of negative mass at the hole's location, and a negative mass repels the centre of mass instead of attracting it. The full lamina's COM sits at the origin by symmetry; taking mass out of the top-right quadrant destroys that symmetry, so the surviving mass is now biased toward the bottom-left and the COM must follow it there. The tempting alternative — “the COM moves toward where the change happened” — is exactly backwards, and it is the reason option (a) is offered first.
Formula xCOM = (A1x1 − A2x2) / (A1 − A2) and the same in y. The minus sign in the numerator is what converts “hole at positive x” into “COM at negative x”.
Solution
1. Set the square of side L centred at O, so A1 = L2 at (x1, y1) = (0, 0). Any piece cut from the top-right corner has its own centroid at some (x2, y2) with x2 > 0 and y2 > 0.You do not need the piece's size — only the signs of its centroid coordinates decide the direction.
2. Apply the cavity formula in x: xCOM = (L2×0 − A2x2)/(L2 − A2) = −A2x2/(L2−A2) < 0, since A2 < L2 makes the denominator positive.Checking that the denominator stays positive is the step that guarantees the sign of the whole expression.
3. Repeat in y by the identical algebra: yCOM = −A2y2/(L2−A2) < 0. Both coordinates turn negative, so the COM moves into the third quadrant — down and to the left.
4. Kill option (d): it claims the shift is purely along x. That would need y2 = 0, i.e. a piece removed from the middle of the right edge, not from a corner.A corner cut always has two non-zero centroid coordinates, so it always produces a diagonal shift.
5. Kill option (c): the COM stays at O only if the removal preserves the symmetry (for example four identical corner squares removed together). One corner alone breaks both mirror symmetries.
6. Kill option (a): a shift toward the top-right would require the numerator +A2x2, i.e. adding the small square rather than removing it. Only option (b) survives.
   +-----------+ . . .
   |           |  X  |   X = piece removed (top-right)
   |     O     |. . .|
   |   *       |         O = old COM (origin)
   |           |         * = new COM, down-left of O
   +-----------+
   x_cm = -A2*x2/(L^2-A2) < 0 ,  y_cm < 0  ->  3rd quadrant
⚡ JEE Trick Name the trap: “mass removed here, so the COM goes here”. The rule is the opposite — the COM always runs away from a cavity and toward added mass. Decode the rest: (c) needs symmetry that a single corner cut destroys, (d) needs the cut centred on an axis, and (a) is the sign error itself. For any diagonal corner cut, answer “along the diagonal, away from the corner” without writing a single number.
Answer: (b) It shifts towards the bottom-left direction.
2A man of mass m is suspended in air by holding the rope of a balloon of mass M. As the man climbs up the rope, the balloon (a) Moves upward (b) Moves downward (c) Remains stationary (d) Cannot sayCOM fixed, internal motionCENTRE OF MASS A man of mass m is suspended in air by holding the rope of a balloon🟢 ExactbMoves downward
Concept The man+rope+balloon form one system that is already floating in equilibrium: buoyancy up exactly balances total weight down, so the net external force is zero. The muscular force the man exerts on the rope and the rope's reaction on him are internal, and internal forces can never move the system's centre of mass. So the COM must stay put, and if one part of the system rises the other must sink to keep the weighted average fixed. The intuitive alternative — “he pulls himself up, so the balloon is dragged up too” — would move the COM upward with no external agent, which is impossible.
Formula Fext = 0 ⇒ yCOM = (M yB + m ym)/(M + m) = constant. Zero external force means zero COM acceleration, and starting from rest that means the COM never moves at all.
Solution
1. Check the external ledger before the climb: buoyant force B acts up on the balloon, weight (M+m)g acts down, and the system hangs at rest, so B = (M+m)g and Fext = 0.Climbing does not change the balloon's volume, so B is unchanged during the motion — the ledger stays balanced throughout.
2. Impose the constraint by differencing the COM equation: MΔyB + mΔym = (M+m)ΔyCOM = 0, so ΔyB = −(m/M)Δym.The minus sign is the entire answer: the two displacements must have opposite signs.
3. The man climbs, so Δym > 0, which forces ΔyB = −(m/M)Δym < 0 — the balloon descends.
4. Size the effect: if the man rises h relative to the ground, the balloon falls (m/M)h, so a heavy balloon barely moves while a light one drops noticeably.Ground displacements, not displacements along the rope, are what the COM equation constrains — mixing the two is the usual algebra slip.
5. Cross-check with Newton's third law: to accelerate himself upward the man pulls the rope downward, and that downward force on the balloon is exactly what pushes it down. The two views agree.
6. Kill the remaining options: (a) needs an unbalanced upward external force that does not exist, (c) needs m/M = 0, and (d) is unnecessary because the COM argument is decisive. Hence (b).
    (B)  balloon  M   -- moves DOWN by (m/M)h
     |
    rope                COM stays fixed
     |
    man  m            -- climbs UP by h

    M*(-dB) + m*(+h) = 0   ->   dB = (m/M) h  downward
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is thinking a self-generated pull can move the whole system; it cannot, because action and reaction cancel inside the system. Whenever a question says “X climbs / walks / crawls on Y” with no external horizontal or net vertical force, write yCOM = constant first and read the direction off the minus sign. Decode the options: (a) is the internal-force fallacy, (c) would need an infinitely heavy balloon, and (d) is bait for anyone who did not spot that Fext = 0.
Answer: (b) Moves downward.

4In the formula for the center of mass along the x-axis, what does the numerator represent? Options: (a) Total mass (b) Area-weighted sum of x-coordinates (c) Moment of inertia (d) VolumeMeaning of the first momentCENTRE OF MASS in One Shot: All Concepts & PYQs Covered | JEE Main & Advanced🟡 SimilarbArea-weighted sum of x-coordinates
Concept The centre-of-mass formula is a weighted average, and every weighted average has the same anatomy: a numerator that adds up (weight × value) and a denominator that adds up the weights alone. For the laminae of this lecture the weight is area, so the numerator is ΣAixi — the first moment of area about the y-axis — while the total area ΣAi is the denominator. The trap is that “total mass” is a true statement about the denominator, and options are written so that a half-remembered formula lands on the wrong half of the fraction.
Formula xCOM = (A1x1 + A2x2 + … + Anxn) / (A1 + A2 + … + An). For a uniform lamina mi = ρt Ai, so ρt cancels between numerator and denominator and areas act as the weights.
Solution
1. Split the formula: numerator = ΣAixi, denominator = ΣAi. Only the numerator carries any x, so it alone can encode where the material sits.If the numerator had no position information the formula could not return a position at all — a one-second way to eliminate (a) and (d).
2. Check dimensions: xCOM must be a length, and [ΣAixi]/[ΣAi] = L3/L2 = L. The numerator therefore has dimensions L3, matching “area × distance”.
3. Test (a): total mass (or total area) is the denominator, not the numerator, and has no x in it — reject.This is the intended near-miss: the phrase is correct physics attached to the wrong position in the fraction.
4. Test (c): moment of inertia is Σmixi2, a second moment with dimensions ML2. The COM numerator is a first moment, linear in x — reject.
5. Test (d): volume has dimensions L3, which coincidentally matches step 2, but volume contains no information about position and would be identical for a body shifted anywhere along x — reject.Dimensional agreement alone never proves an identification; the physical content must match too.
6. Only (b) describes ΣAixi: each x-coordinate weighted by its own segment's area, then summed.

  x_cm = --------------
          sum(A_i)          <- denominator: total area, [L^2]

10As shown in figure, when a spherical cavity (centred at O) of radius 1 is cut out of a uniform sphere of radius R (centred at C), the centre of mass of remaining (shaded) part of sphere is at G, i.e. on the surface of the cavity. R can be determined by the equation (a) (R2+R+1)(2−R) = 1 (b) (R2+R−1)(2−R) = 1 (c) (R2−R−1)(2−R) = 1 (d) (R2−R+1)(2−R) = 1 [Figure: the cavity touches the sphere internally on the right, and G lies on the cavity's surface on the side away from O.]Sphere with cavity, JEE MainAs shown in fig. when a spherical cavity (centred at O) of radius 1 is cut out of a uniform sphere🟢 Exacta(R2+R+1)(2−R) = 1
Concept Two separate facts have to be turned into equations and then set equal. First, the cavity touches the sphere internally, which fixes the centre separation CO = R − 1. Second, “G is on the surface of the cavity” is a geometric condition, not a definition: the computed centre of mass must land exactly one cavity-radius from O. Equating the two expressions for xG gives a cubic that the options have already factorised for you. This is a volume problem, so masses go as radius cubed — importing the R2 habit from disc problems is the expensive mistake here.
Formula xG = −r3d/(R3 − r3) with r = 1 and d = CO. Uniform sphere ⇒ mass ∝ volume ∝ radius3; the cavity is a negative mass at its own centre, and C is taken as origin so the full sphere contributes nothing.
Solution
1. Put C at the origin with O on the +x axis. Internal tangency on the right gives d = CO = R − 1.The cavity's far edge is then (R−1)+1 = R, exactly the sphere's surface — matching the figure.
2. Centre of mass of the remaining solid: xG = (R3·0 − 13·d)/(R3 − 1) = −(R−1)/(R3−1).Negative x, i.e. G lies on the opposite side of C from the cavity — exactly where the figure marks it.
3. The cavity's surface, measured on the −x side, is the point x = d − 1 = (R−1) − 1 = R − 2.O is at x = d and the cavity has radius 1, so its left-hand surface point is d − 1.
4. Impose “G on the cavity's surface”: −(R−1)/(R3−1) = R − 2, i.e. (R−1)/(R3−1) = 2 − R.Both sides are positive only if R < 2, which is the physically admissible branch — and it is why every option carries the factor (2−R).
5. Factorise R3−1 = (R−1)(R2+R+1) and cancel the common (R−1) (legal since R ≠ 1): 1/(R2+R+1) = 2 − R, so (R2+R+1)(2−R) = 1 — option (a).Recognising a3−b3 is the entire trick; without it you are left staring at an unfactorable cubic.
6. Numerical check: R = 1.83 gives (3.349+1.83+1)(0.17) = 1.05 and R = 1.84 gives 1.00 — a root near R ≈ 1.84, comfortably in (1, 2), so the configuration is physically realisable. Key prints (a); working gives (a). Agreement.

⚡ JEE Trick The moment you see R3−1 in a denominator with an (R−1) upstairs, cancel — that single step converts the problem from a cubic into the printed option. The distractors are all built on plausible factorisation slips: (d) uses R2−R+1, the factor of R3+1 rather than R3−1; (b) and (c) drop or flip a sign inside the quadratic. Note also that the shift −(R−1)/(R3−1) is tiny (about −0.16 here) because the cavity is a small fraction of the sphere — if your R comes out above 2 or below 1, you have used the sum of radii instead of the difference for CO.
Answer: (a) (R2+R+1)(2−R) = 1.
