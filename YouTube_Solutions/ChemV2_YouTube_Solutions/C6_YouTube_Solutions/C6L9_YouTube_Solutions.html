1 (i)
State whether the following are true or false.
The amount of heat evolved or absorbed when one gram of the substance is dissolved in excess of water is called heat of solution.
C6L9VRecapQ1iDefinition of enthalpy of solution
🟡 Similar6 Enthalpy of hydrat…
True
Concept The enthalpy of solution is the heat change that accompanies dissolving a substance in so much solvent that adding more solvent produces no further heat effect ("infinite dilution"). The physical content of the statement — heat evolved or absorbed, dissolution in excess of water, and the name "heat of solution" — is exactly right; only the amount is quoted loosely.
Key relation X(s) + aq → X(aq); ΔHsol measured per mole of X. Enthalpy is extensive, so a heat of solution is meaningless until you say how much substance dissolved.
Solution
1. Read the three parts of the claim separately: (a) heat may be evolved or absorbed — correct, KOH warms water while KNO3 cools it; (b) the solvent must be in excess — correct, that is the infinite-dilution condition; (c) the amount is "one gram".
2. Parts (a) and (b) are the defining features, so the statement is marked True.
3. On part (c), the chapter's own theory (p. 250) says one mole of substance: KCl + aq → KClaq, ΔH = 4.4 kcal is a per-mole figure.
4. Strictly, then, "one gram" should read "one mole" — per-gram values are called specific heats of solution and are the molar value divided by M.
5. Every worked problem in this lecture (BaCl2, CuSO4, CaCl2, NaCl) quotes ΔHsol in kJ mol−1, confirming the molar convention.
6. Answer as keyed: True, with the amount understood to be one mole.
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a thermochemical quantity is named — solution, neutralisation, formation, combustion, hydration — the silent unit is "per mole" (per equivalent for neutralisation). If a question hands you grams, convert to moles before you touch the enthalpy.
Answer: True — heat of solution is the heat change on dissolving in excess solvent (per mole, as the chapter's own theory states).
1 (ii)
Whenever an acid is neutralised by a base, the net reaction is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l); ΔH = −13.7 kcal
C6L9VRecapQ1iiNet ionic reaction of neutralisation
🟡 SimilarEnthalpy of Neutrali…
True
Concept A strong acid and a strong base are both fully ionised in dilute solution. Na+ and Cl− stay hydrated and untouched on both sides — they are spectators. The only chemical change is H+ combining with OH− to make water, so every strong acid–strong base neutralisation has the same net ionic equation and therefore the same enthalpy.
Key relation H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l); ΔH = −13.7 kcal = −57.1 kJ This is just the enthalpy of formation of liquid water from its aqueous ions.
Solution
1. Write HCl + NaOH in full ionic form: H+ + Cl− + Na+ + OH− → Na+ + Cl− + H2O.
2. Cancel the spectators Na+ and Cl−; what survives is H+ + OH− → H2O.
3. Repeat with HCl + KOH, HNO3 + NaOH, HClO4 + KOH — the same net equation appears every time, so ΔH is the same −13.70 kcal.
4. The book's own table confirms it: HCl + NaOH and HCl + KOH both give −13.70 kcal.
5. Caveat worth knowing: this holds for strong acid with strong base. With a weak partner some of the released heat is spent ionising it, so the apparent value falls (12.3 kcal for NH4OH, 13.4 for CH3COOH).
6. Read as the book intends it — the standard strong/strong case — the statement is True.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the pair −13.7 kcal = −57.1 kJ per equivalent. Papers switch units mid-question, and the two numbers are the same physics: 13.7 × 4.184 = 57.3.
Answer: True — the net reaction is H+ + OH− → H2O with ΔH = −13.7 kcal (strong acid + strong base).
1 (iii)
The value −13.7 kcal is constant for all neutralization reaction between strong acid and strong base.
C6L9VRecapQ1iiiWhy ΔHneut is constant for SA–SB
🟡 SimilarHeat of neutralizati…
True
Concept Constancy follows directly from the previous statement. Because every strong acid–strong base pair reduces to the identical net reaction, the measured heat cannot depend on which acid or which base was used. This is one of the cleanest experimental confirmations that ions in dilute solution behave independently of one another.
Key relation ΔHneut(SA + SB) = ΔHf[H2O(l)] from aqueous ions = −13.7 kcal eq−1 A single number, not a family of numbers.
Solution
1. Strong acids (HCl, HNO3, HClO4, H2SO4) are ≈100% ionised in dilute solution, so no energy is needed to liberate H+.
2. Strong bases (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2) are likewise fully ionised, so OH− is already free.
3. Therefore the only bond-making step is H+ + OH− → H2O for every such pair.
4. Cations and anions merely exchange partners; their hydration shells are unchanged, so they contribute no enthalpy.
5. Experiment agrees: HCl/NaOH, HCl/KOH, HNO3/NaOH all give −13.70 ± 0.05 kcal per equivalent.
6. Hence the statement is True. (Note "per equivalent": 1 mol H2SO4 releases 2 × 13.7 kcal because it supplies two equivalents.)
⚡ JEE Trick Constant "per equivalent", not per mole. For a dibasic acid or a diacidic base, first count equivalents, then multiply by 13.7 kcal (or 57.1 kJ). Half the marks lost on this topic come from forgetting the factor of 2.
Answer: True — all strong acid + strong base pairs share one net reaction, so ΔHneut is the constant −13.7 kcal per equivalent.

1 (v)
Bond enthalpies of diatomic molecules are equal to the bond enthalpies of dissociation.
C6L9VRecapQ1vBond enthalpy vs bond dissociation enthalpy
🟡 SimilarWhat is the Differen…
True
Concept For a molecule with only one bond there is nothing to average. Breaking H—H gives two H atoms in one step, so the "bond enthalpy" and the "bond dissociation enthalpy" describe the very same process and carry the same magnitude. The distinction between the two words only becomes meaningful for polyatomic molecules.
Key relation A–B(g) → A(g) + B(g); ΔH = bond dissociation enthalpy One bond, one step, one number — no averaging is possible.
Solution
1. Take H2: H2(g) → 2H(g), ΔH = +435.14 kJ mol−1. That single figure is both the bond enthalpy and the bond dissociation enthalpy of H—H.
2. Contrast H2O, which has two O—H bonds broken in sequence: +501.87 then +423.38 kJ mol−1.
3. Those two stepwise dissociation enthalpies differ because the fragment left behind (•OH vs a bare O atom) is different, so only their average, 462.6, is quoted as the O—H bond enthalpy.
4. A diatomic offers no second step, so no such discrepancy can arise.
5. Sign convention: bond dissociation is endothermic (+), bond formation exothermic (−); equal in magnitude, opposite in sign.
6. The statement is therefore True for diatomic molecules.
⚡ JEE Trick If a question quotes one number for a diatomic, use it directly. If it quotes several numbers for a polyatomic, they are stepwise values and you must average them before using them as "the" bond energy.
Answer: True — for a diatomic molecule bond enthalpy and bond dissociation enthalpy are the same quantity.
1 (vi)
Bond formation is always exothermic
C6L9VRecapQ1viSign of bond-formation enthalpy
🟡 SimilarWhat is Bond Enthalp…
True
Concept Two free atoms sit at high potential energy. As they approach, attraction between the nucleus of each and the electrons of the other lowers the potential energy until a minimum is reached at the equilibrium bond length. That drop in energy has to leave the system as heat, so forming a bond is necessarily exothermic. Breaking the bond is the uphill reverse and is always endothermic.
Key relation H•(g) + H•(g) → H2(g); ΔH = −435.14 kJ mol−1 The negative sign is not a convention — it is the depth of the potential-energy well.
Solution
1. Sketch potential energy against internuclear distance: it falls from zero at infinite separation to a minimum, then rises steeply as the nuclei repel.
2. Energy released on going from infinity to the minimum equals the well depth, which is the bond enthalpy.
3. Since the products (the molecule) sit lower than the reactants (free atoms), ΔH for bond formation is negative — exothermic.
4. The book states it directly: bond enthalpies are exoergic (ΔH = −ve), bond dissociation enthalpies endoergic (ΔH = +ve).
5. No counter-example exists: if a "bond" cost energy to form, the atoms would simply fly apart and the molecule would not be stable.
6. Hence the statement is True.
⚡ JEE Trick Use this as a sign check on every bond-energy sum. If your calculated "bond formation" enthalpy comes out positive, you have added the products instead of subtracting them.
Answer: True — bond formation lowers the potential energy of the atoms, so it is always exothermic.
1 (vii)
Heat of reaction = Σ Bond energies of product − Σ Bond energies of reactants.
C6L9VRecapQ1viiCorrect direction of the bond-energy formula
🟡 SimilarBond Energy Calculat…
False
Concept A reaction proceeds conceptually in two stages: every bond in the reactants is broken (costing energy) and every bond in the products is formed (releasing energy). The net enthalpy is therefore energy spent minus energy regained — reactants first, products subtracted. The statement has the two terms the wrong way round, which flips the sign of every answer.
Key relation ΔrH = ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products) Note this is the opposite order to the ΔHf formula, which is products minus reactants — the classic trap.
Solution
1. Break all reactant bonds: this absorbs ΣBE(reactants), a positive contribution.
2. Assemble the products from the free atoms: this releases ΣBE(products), a negative contribution.
3. Add them: ΔrH = +ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products).
4. The statement writes products minus reactants, which is the negative of the correct expression.
5. Test it on H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl with BE = 435, 243, 431: correct gives (435 + 243) − 862 = −184 kJ, exothermic as observed. The reversed version would predict +184 kJ, which is wrong.
6. The statement is therefore False.
⚡ JEE Trick Two formulae, opposite orders — keep them apart: bond energies = reactants − products; formation enthalpies = products − reactants. Remember it as "bonds must break first".
Answer: False — heat of reaction = ΣBE(reactants) − ΣBE(products), not the other way round.

🟡 SimilarEnthalpies of phase…

Concept Each phase change carries its own name, fixed by which two phases are involved and in which direction. Here liquid water becomes gaseous water at constant temperature and pressure, and the enthalpy needed to do that is the enthalpy (heat) of vaporisation.

What is phase transformation?
C6L9VBoardQ1Phase transformation defined
🟡 SimilarEnthalpy Change duri…
—
Concept A phase transformation is a change of a substance from one physical state (or allotropic form) to another at constant T and P, with no change in chemical composition. Only the arrangement and freedom of the particles differ, and the enthalpy change measures the energy needed to rearrange the intermolecular forces.
Key relation solid ⇆ liquid ⇆ gas, each step at a fixed T and P with its own ΔH Temperature stays constant throughout a phase change; all supplied heat goes into potential energy, not kinetic energy.
Solution
1. Definition. Phase transformation is the conversion of a substance from one phase to another at the characteristic temperature for that change, the chemical identity remaining unaltered.
2. The changes. Fusion (s → l) at the melting point, vaporisation (l → g) at the boiling point, sublimation (s → g) below the melting point, and their reverses — freezing, condensation, deposition.
3. Allotropic transition counts too: Cdiamond → Camorphous, ΔH = −1.9 kJ, at the transition temperature.
4. Energetics. Changes that increase disorder absorb heat (ΔH +ve); their reverses release the same amount. H2O(l) → H2O(g) needs +40.6 kJ mol−1, condensation returns −40.6.
5. Constant temperature. While ice melts at 0°C the thermometer does not move: the heat is latent heat, spent breaking attractions, not raising kinetic energy.
6. Why it matters. ΔH is a state function, so these enthalpies add freely in Hess cycles — that is how ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap arises.
⚡ JEE Trick Give the definition, one labelled example with its ΔH, and the sign rule. Quoting H2O(l) → H2O(g), ΔH = +40.6 kJ mol−1 earns the example mark alone.
Answer: A change of a substance between physical states or allotropic forms at constant T and P without change in chemical identity, each step carrying its own enthalpy (ΔHfus, ΔHvap, ΔHsub, ΔHtrans).

Define and explain the following:
(A) Enthalpy of vaporisation.
(B) Enthalpy of sublimation.
C6L9VBoardQ2ΔHvap and ΔHsub defined
🟡 SimilarHeat of Vaporisation…
—
Concept Both quantities are molar enthalpies of phase change, defined for exactly one mole and at the temperature where the two phases coexist. Vaporisation takes a liquid to its vapour at the boiling point; sublimation takes a solid straight to its vapour below the melting point, skipping the liquid entirely.
Key relation H2O(l) → H2O(g), ΔHvap = +40.6 kJ  |  I2(s) → I2(g), ΔHsub = +62.4 kJ Both are endothermic: attractions are being broken, not made.
Solution
1. (A) Enthalpy of vaporisation. The enthalpy change when one mole of a liquid is completely converted into vapour at its boiling point and constant pressure.
2. It measures the strength of intermolecular forces in the liquid: water needs +40.6 kJ mol−1 because of hydrogen bonding, whereas helium needs only +0.092 kJ mol−1 and NaCl a huge +170 kJ mol−1.
3. Condensation, the reverse, has the same magnitude with the opposite sign.
4. (B) Enthalpy of sublimation. The enthalpy change when one mole of a solid is converted directly into vapour at a temperature below its melting point, e.g. I2(s) → I2(g), ΔH = +62.4 kJ.
5. Because enthalpy is a state function, the direct route and the two-step route must cost the same: ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap.
6. So ΔHsub is the largest of the three — it pays for melting and boiling. Camphor, naphthalene, dry ice and NH4Cl are the standard examples.
⚡ JEE Trick Large ΔHvap means strong intermolecular forces, which means a high boiling point. The chapter's table is a ready-made ranking: He < O2 < ethanol < water < MgCl2 < NaCl.
Answer: (A) ΔHvap = heat needed to convert one mole of liquid to vapour at its boiling point (water, +40.6 kJ mol−1); (B) ΔHsub = heat needed to convert one mole of solid directly to vapour below its melting point (I2, +62.4 kJ mol−1), and ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap.

Define and explain enthalpy of freezing.
C6L9VBoardQ3Enthalpy of freezing defined
🟡 SimilarEnthalpy Of Phase Ch…
—
Concept Freezing is the reverse of fusion. When a liquid solidifies, its particles lose the freedom to move past one another and settle into a fixed lattice, so intermolecular attractions are made rather than broken. Energy is therefore released and the enthalpy of freezing is negative, equal in magnitude to the enthalpy of fusion.
Key relation ΔHfreezing = −ΔHfusion Reversing any process reverses the sign of its enthalpy change, because H is a state function.
Solution
1. Definition. The enthalpy of freezing (heat of solidification) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a liquid is completely converted into solid at its freezing point and constant pressure.
2. Sign. It is exothermic, ΔH negative, because ordered bonding in the lattice releases the energy that melting had to supply.
3. Example. H2O(l) → H2O(s) at 273 K, ΔH = −6.01 kJ mol−1, whereas fusion H2O(s) → H2O(l) has ΔH = +6.01 kJ mol−1.
4. Constant temperature. Throughout freezing the temperature stays at the freezing point; the heat removed is latent heat, not a temperature drop.
5. Freezing point = melting point. The two phases are in equilibrium at the same temperature, so one number serves both directions.
6. Everyday use. Orchards are sprayed with water before a frost: as the water freezes it releases 6 kJ mol−1, keeping the fruit near 0°C instead of colder.
⚡ JEE Trick Any "reverse process" question is a one-line answer: flip the sign, keep the number. Examiners often quote ΔHfus and ask for freezing (or ΔHvap and ask for condensation) purely to test the sign.

d(A)→(p, r), (B)→(p), (C)→(q), (D)→(p, r, s)
Concept The trap in this match is the assumption that each reaction has exactly one label. A reaction can be a formation and a combustion at the same time, provided it makes one mole of a compound from its elements while also burning something in oxygen. Only one option allows the multiple entries the chemistry demands.
Key relation formation = 1 mol of compound from elements in standard states; combustion = complete or partial burning in O2 The two definitions overlap whenever an oxide is made directly from its elements.
Solution
1. (A) C(s) + (1/2)O2(g) → CO(g): one mole of CO from the elements → formation (r); and carbon is burning in oxygen → combustion (p). So (A) → (p, r).
2. (B) CO(g) + (1/2)O2(g) → CO2(g): CO is burning → combustion (p). But CO is a compound, not an element, so this is not a formation reaction. (B) → (p) only.
3. (C) NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O: acid + base → salt + water, a neutralisation (q).
4. (D) H2(g) + (1/2)O2(g) → H2O(l): formation of water from its elements (r); hydrogen burning (p); and it is the cell reaction of the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell (s). So (D) → (p, r, s).
5. Collect: (A) → (p, r), (B) → (p), (C) → (q), (D) → (p, r, s) — option (d).
6. Options (a), (b) and (c) each force a strict one-to-one pairing and so miss the overlaps; (a) even calls (B) a neutralisation, which is plainly wrong.
⚡ JEE Trick In a match-the-column question, scan the options first. If exactly one of them assigns multiple entries to a row, the examiner is almost certainly testing an overlap — and that option is usually the answer.
Answer: (d) (A)→(p, r), (B)→(p), (C)→(q), (D)→(p, r, s).

The lattice energy of solid KCl is 181 kcal mol−1 and the enthalpy of solution of KCl in H2O is 1.0 kcal mol−1. If the hydration enthalpies of K⊕ and Cl⊖ ions are in the ratio of 2 : 1 then the enthalpy hydration of K⊕ is −20x K cal mol−1. Find the value of x.
C6L9V6Q7Splitting a hydration enthalpy between two ions
🟢 ExactThe lattice energy o…
x = 6
Concept Dissolving an ionic solid is the sum of two terms: the endothermic break-up of the lattice and the exothermic hydration of the resulting ions. Given the lattice energy and the observed heat of solution, the total hydration enthalpy follows immediately; the stated 2 : 1 ratio then splits it between the two ions.
Key relation ΔHsol = Ulattice(dissociation) + ΔHhyd(total) U is quoted here as the positive 181 kcal needed to pull the lattice apart.
Solution
Given lattice energy = 181 kcal mol-1, ΔHsol = 1.0 kcal mol-1, hydration enthalpy ratio K+ : Cl- = 2 : 1
Find value of x in ΔHhyd(K+) = -20x kcal/mol
1. Lattice break-up: KCl(s) → K+(g) + Cl−(g), ΔH = +181 kcal mol−1.
2. Overall dissolution: KCl(s) + aq → K+(aq) + Cl−(aq), ΔHsol = +1.0 kcal mol−1.
3. Substitute: 1.0 = 181 + ΔHhyd, so ΔHhyd(total) = 1.0 − 181 = −180 kcal mol−1.
4. Split in the ratio K+ : Cl− = 2 : 1, so K+ takes two of the three parts: ΔHhyd(K+) = (2/3)(−180) = −120 kcal mol−1 and Cl− takes −60.
5. The question writes ΔHhyd(K+) = −20x, so −20x = −120 and x = 6.
6. Consistency check: −120 + (−60) = −180, and 181 − 180 = +1.0 kcal, the given (slightly endothermic) heat of solution — which is why KCl cools water gently.
⚡ JEE Trick Check the sign convention of the lattice energy first. If it is quoted as negative (formation of the lattice), you must subtract it instead of adding. Here "lattice energy = 181 kcal" is the dissociation value, so it enters with a plus.
Answer: x = 6 — ΔHhyd(K+) = −120 kcal mol−1 out of a total −180 kcal mol−1.

27.49 g of O<sub>2</sub>
Concept Carbon has burnt to a mixture of CO and CO2, and the split is unknown. Two equations pin it down: the carbon balance (all 1 mol is accounted for) and the heat balance (the two products contribute different enthalpies). Solve for the split, then convert to the oxygen consumed.
Key relation a + b = 1 and 95a + 24b = 75 a = mol CO2, b = mol CO; heats are taken as magnitudes since the question quotes heat liberated.
Solution
Given m(C) = 12.0 g, q = 75.0 kcal liberated, ΔHf(CO2) = -95 kcal mol-1, ΔHf(CO) = -24 kcal mol-1
Find mass of oxygen that reacted (g)
1. 12.0 g of carbon is exactly 1 mol, and none remains, so a + b = 1 where a = mol of CO2 and b = mol of CO.
2. Heat balance: 95a + 24b = 75 kcal, using |ΔHf| = 95 for CO2 and 24 for CO.
3. Substitute b = 1 − a: 95a + 24(1 − a) = 75, so 71a = 51 and a = 0.7183 mol CO2, b = 0.2817 mol CO.
4. Oxygen used: C + O2 → CO2 takes 1 mol O2 per mol; C + (1/2)O2 → CO takes (1/2) mol O2 per mol.
5. n(O2) = 0.7183 + (1/2)(0.2817) = 0.7183 + 0.1408 = 0.8592 mol.
6. Mass = 0.8592 × 32 = 27.49 g.
⚡ JEE Trick Any "mixture of CO and CO2" problem is two linear equations: one atom balance and one energy (or mass) balance. Set them up before touching a calculator — the algebra is always this short.
Answer: 27.49 g of oxygen reacted (0.8592 mol, from 0.7183 mol CO2 and 0.2817 mol CO).

The heat of combustion of acetylene is 312 kcal. If heat of formation of CO2 and H2O are 94.38 and 68.38 kcal respectively. Calculate C≡C bond energy. Given that heat of atomisation of C and H are 150.0 and 51.5 kcal respectively and C—H bond energy is 93.64 kcal. (in kcal)
C6L9V6Q9C≡C bond energy from combustion + atomisation
🟢 ExactThe heat of combust…
160.86 kcal
Concept Two cycles in sequence. First convert the heat of combustion of acetylene into its heat of formation using the formation enthalpies of CO2 and H2O. Then feed that formation enthalpy into an atomisation cycle to extract the C≡C bond energy.
Key relation ΔfH = 2ΔHat(C) + 2ΔHat(H) − [BE(C≡C) + 2BE(C—H)] Heats of atomisation are quoted per mole of atoms: 150.0 for C, 51.5 for H.
Solution
Given ΔHc(C2H2) = 312 kcal, ΔHf(CO2) = 94.38 kcal, ΔHf(H2O) = 68.38 kcal, atomisation C = 150.0 kcal, H = 51.5 kcal, C-H = 93.64 kcal
Find C≡C bond energy (kcal)
1. Combustion: C2H2(g) + 5/2 O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + H2O(l), ΔH = −312 kcal.
2. Apply ΔcH = ΣΔfH(products) − ΔfH(C2H2): −312 = [2(−94.38) + (−68.38)] − ΔfH.
3. −312 = −257.14 − ΔfH, so ΔfH(C2H2) = −257.14 + 312 = +54.86 kcal mol−1 — acetylene is an endothermic compound, as expected.
4. Atomisation cycle for 2C(s) + H2(g) → C2H2(g): energy in = 2(150.0) + 2(51.5) = 300 + 103 = 403 kcal.
5. Energy out = BE(C≡C) + 2(93.64) = BE + 187.28 kcal. Hess: 54.86 = 403 − BE − 187.28 = 215.72 − BE.
6. BE(C≡C) = 215.72 − 54.86 = 160.86 kcal mol−1.
⚡ JEE Trick Read "heat of atomisation of H = 51.5 kcal" as per mole of H atoms, i.e. (1/2)H2 → H. Doubling it gives the H—H bond energy, 103 kcal. Mistaking it for the whole H2 dissociation halves the hydrogen term and wrecks the answer.
Answer: BE(C≡C) = 160.86 kcal mol−1, via ΔfH(C2H2) = +54.86 kcal mol−1.

Heat of solution of NaCl is 3.9 kJ mol−1. If the lattice energy of NaCl is 787 kJ mol−1, calculate the hydration energies of ions of the salt.
C6L9V6Q10Hydration energy from lattice energy and ΔHsol
🟡 SimilarCalculate heat of so…
−783.1 kJ mol<sup>−1</sup> (total for Na<sup>+</sup> + Cl<sup>−</sup>)
Concept The same two-term cycle as the KCl question. Sodium chloride dissolves with a small endothermic heat because the enormous lattice energy is almost exactly cancelled by the enormous hydration energy — the two differ by less than 0.5%, which is why table salt dissolves so readily yet cools the water only slightly.
Key relation ΔHsol = Ulattice(dissociation) + ΔHhyd Rearranged: ΔHhyd = ΔHsol − U.
Solution
Given ΔHsol(NaCl) = 3.9 kJ mol-1, lattice energy = 787 kJ mol-1
Find hydration energies of the ions of NaCl
1. Lattice break-up: NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g), ΔH = +787 kJ mol−1.
2. Hydration: Na+(g) + Cl−(g) + aq → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq), ΔHhyd = ?
3. Sum of the two steps is the overall dissolution, ΔHsol = +3.9 kJ mol−1.
4. So 3.9 = 787 + ΔHhyd, giving ΔHhyd = 3.9 − 787 = −783.1 kJ mol−1 for the ion pair.
5. Sign check: hydration is exothermic and its magnitude must be just under the lattice energy, otherwise the salt would not dissolve endothermically. 783.1 < 787 by 3.9 — exactly the observed heat of solution.
6. This near-cancellation is the general rule for soluble 1:1 salts: |U| and |ΔHhyd| are both large and nearly equal, so ΔHsol is a small difference of two big numbers.
⚡ JEE Trick A tiny ΔHsol is the signature of near-cancellation. Never round the lattice or hydration energies mid-calculation: rounding 787 to 790 would turn a +3.9 kJ answer into +0.9 kJ, an error of 75%.
Answer: ΔHhydration = −783.1 kJ mol−1 for Na+ and Cl− together.
