1According to VSEPR theory, what is the molecular shape of SO2? (Level-1)
(a) Linear   (b) Bent   (c) Trigonal planar   (d) T-shaped		Shape of SO₂VSEPR geometry of SO₂ — why sulphur dioxide comes out bent🟢 ExactbBent
Concept VSEPR asks one question only: how many electron-pair clouds (bond pairs + lone pairs) surround the central atom? That total — the steric number Q — fixes the electronic geometry, and then the lone pairs are stripped off to leave the shape you can actually see. In SO2 the sulphur is surrounded by two σ-bonded oxygens plus one lone pair, so Q = 3 (trigonal-planar parent) but only two of those three directions carry an atom. The invisible lone pair is exactly what turns a would-be linear O–S–O into a V.
Formula Q = σ-bond pairs + lone pairs on the central atom, and for monovalent attachments Q = ½ (V + M − c+ + c−). Only σ-linked atoms are counted; a double bond points in the same single direction in space as a single bond, so each S=O contributes exactly one domain. Shape from (bp, lp): (2,0) linear · (3,0) trigonal planar · (2,1) bent · (4,0) tetrahedral · (3,1) pyramidal · (2,2) bent.
Solution
1. Central atom S: valence electrons V = 6 (group 16, 3s²3p⁴).
2. S forms two σ bonds to O, using 2 × 2 = 4 of its valence electrons.Each S=O is one σ + one π; the π cloud sits on top of the σ and occupies no separate direction, so it never adds to the steric count.
3. Electrons left on S = 6 − 4 = 2 ⇒ 1 lone pair.
4. Steric number Q = 2 bond pairs + 1 lone pair = 3 ⇒ hybridisation sp², electronic geometry trigonal planar, ideal angle 120°.
5. Delete the lone-pair vertex: AB2L ⇒ shape = bent (V-shaped), with the angle squeezed slightly from 120° to about 119.5° by lp–bp repulsion.
6. Eliminate the distractors: (a) linear needs Q = 2 with no lone pair (CO2, BeCl2); (c) trigonal planar needs 3 bp and zero lp (BF3, SO3); (d) T-shaped needs 3 bp + 2 lp on a Q = 5 framework (ClF3).

(a) BeF2   (b) BCl3   (c) NH3   (d) ClF3		T-shaped speciesClF₃ molecular geometry, bond angles and electron geometry🟢 ExactdClF3
Concept A T-shape can only come out of a trigonal-bipyramidal parent (Q = 5) in which two of the three equatorial sites are lone pairs. What survives is one equatorial bond plus two axial bonds — three atoms at roughly 90°, 90° and 180°, which is the letter T (the module calls it an "arrow"). So the hunt is for a molecule of the AB3L2 type: three bond pairs and two lone pairs. Only a central atom that can expand its octet to 10 electrons manages this, which rules out every second-period centre and points straight at a heavier halogen such as Cl in ClF3.
Formula Q = ½ (V + M) for a neutral molecule with monovalent substituents. For ClF₃: V = 7, M = 3 ⇒ Q = (7 + 3)/2 = 5 ⇒ sp³d, trigonal-bipyramidal parent; lone pairs = Q − bp = 5 − 3 = 2. AB3L2 ⇒ T-shaped.
Solution
1. Run Q = ½(V + M) on every option:
  • BeF2: V(Be) = 2, M = 2 ⇒ Q = 2, lp = 0 ⇒ linear, 180°.
  • BCl3: V(B) = 3, M = 3 ⇒ Q = 3, lp = 0 ⇒ trigonal planar, 120°.
  • NH3: V(N) = 5, M = 3 ⇒ Q = 4, lp = 1 ⇒ trigonal pyramidal, 107°.
  • ClF3: V(Cl) = 7, M = 3 ⇒ Q = 5, lp = 2 ⇒ T-shaped. ✓
2. Now place the two lone pairs of ClF3. They go equatorial, because an equatorial lone pair meets only 2 bond pairs at 90° while an axial one meets 3.Counting 90° lp–bp contacts is the single rule that settles every trigonal-bipyramidal arrangement — 90° repulsions dominate because they are the closest approach.

Concept Hybridisation is just the steric number written in orbital language: Q = 2 → sp, 3 → sp², 4 → sp³, 5 → sp³d, 6 → sp³d². So the entire problem collapses to counting electron pairs around the central iodine. The one place candidates lose the mark is the charge: a positive charge means the ion has lost an electron, so subtract it from the central atom's valence count; a negative charge means an electron was gained, so add it. One sign error moves the answer two rows down the table.

1. Central atom I (group 17): V = 7.
2. Two chlorines attached by single bonds: M = 2.

4Which of the following does not show octahedral geometry? (Level-4)
(a) SF6   (b) IF5   (c) SiF62−   (d) SF4		Octahedral familyWhich of the following does not show octahedral geometry?🟢 ExactdSF4
Concept "Octahedral geometry" in VSEPR language means the central atom carries six electron pairs, Q = 6, i.e. sp³d² hybridisation. Those six pairs always point at the corners of an octahedron; whether all six are bonds (SF6, octahedral shape), five are bonds (IF5, square-pyramidal shape) or four are bonds (XeF4, square-planar shape), the underlying geometry stays octahedral. So the odd one out has to be a species whose steric number is not 6 in the first place.
Formula Q = ½ (V + M + c−); Q = 6 ⇒ sp³d² ⇒ octahedral electronic geometry. Q = 5 gives sp³d and a trigonal-bipyramidal parent instead — a completely different framework, carrying 90° and 120° angles rather than 90° only.
Solution
1. SF6: V(S) = 6, M = 6 ⇒ Q = (6 + 6)/2 = 6, lp = 0 ⇒ octahedral geometry and octahedral shape.
2. IF5: V(I) = 7, M = 5 ⇒ Q = (7 + 5)/2 = 6, lp = 1 ⇒ octahedral geometry, square-pyramidal shape.It still "shows octahedral geometry" — the lone pair merely occupies the sixth vertex of the same octahedron.
3. SiF62−: V(Si) = 4, add 2 for the 2− charge ⇒ 6; M = 6 ⇒ Q = (6 + 6)/2 = 6, lp = 0 ⇒ octahedral.
4. SF4: V(S) = 6, M = 4 ⇒ Q = (6 + 4)/2 = 5, lp = 1 ⇒ trigonal-bipyramidal geometry and a see-saw shape. Q = 5 ≠ 6, so this is the exception.
5. Hence (d) SF4 is the only species not built on an octahedron.

5Molecule showing angle greater than observed in tetrahedral structure is: (Level-5)
(a) NH3   (b) Cl2O   (c) H2O   (d) OF2		Bond angle vs 109°28′Bond-angle comparison of OCl₂, H₂O and OF₂🟢 ExactbCl2O
Concept All four molecules have Q = 4, so their reference angle is the tetrahedral 109°28′. Two effects then push the true angle away from it in opposite directions. Lone pairs squeeze bond pairs together (angle ↓). But by VSEPR rule 3, raising the electronegativity of the surrounding atom drags the shared pair away from the central atom and also lowers the angle, while a large, weakly electronegative substituent keeps the pair close to the centre and physically crowds its neighbour, raising the angle. In Cl2O the chlorines are big and less electronegative than oxygen, so crowding wins and the angle opens past 109°28′.
Key relation EN of surrounding atom ↑ ⇒ bond angle ↓ (central atom fixed, lone pair present). A more electronegative substituent pulls the bond pair towards itself, so the two bond pairs sit further from the central atom, repel each other less, and the angle closes. Working the other way: size of surrounding atom ↑ ⇒ steric repulsion ↑ ⇒ angle ↑.
Solution
1. Fix the reference value: the regular tetrahedral angle is 109°28′ ≈ 109.5°.
2. NH3: Q = 4, 3 bp + 1 lp. A single lone pair compresses the angle to 107° — below the reference.
3. H2O: Q = 4, 2 bp + 2 lp. Two lone pairs squeeze harder, giving 104.5° — below.
4. OF2: Q = 4, 2 bp + 2 lp, and F is the most electronegative element of all, so it drags both bond pairs outward; the angle falls further, to about 103° — below.
5. Cl2O: Q = 4, 2 bp + 2 lp, but Cl (EN 3.0) is less electronegative than O (EN 3.5), so the bond pairs stay close in to oxygen and repel strongly; on top of that the two chlorine atoms are bulky and bump into each other. The angle opens to about 110.9° — greater than 109°28′. ✓
6. Therefore the required molecule is (b) Cl2O; every other option lies below the tetrahedral value.

1Answer in one sentence: Indicate the factors on which stability of an ionic compound is measured.Stability of ionic solidsFactor affecting the stability of ionic compound🟢 Exact—
Concept An ionic crystal is held together by the electrostatic attraction of oppositely charged ions packed in a lattice. The single number that measures how strongly it is held is the lattice enthalpy (lattice energy) — the energy released when one mole of the solid is built from its gaseous ions, or equivalently the energy needed to tear it apart. A larger lattice enthalpy means a more stable ionic compound, and lattice enthalpy itself is controlled by just two structural quantities: the charges on the ions and the distance between them (i.e. their sizes).
Key relation U ∝ (q+ × q−) ⁄ (r+ + r−) q are the ionic charges and (r⁺ + r⁻) is the inter-ionic distance; the relation is just Coulomb's law applied to a lattice. Doubling the charge on both ions quadruples U, whereas halving the inter-ionic distance only doubles it — so charge dominates. Supporting factors: the ease of forming the ions themselves — low ionisation enthalpy of the metal, high electron gain enthalpy of the non-metal — and, in solution, a high hydration enthalpy.
Solution
1. Name the governing quantity: the stability of an ionic compound is measured by its lattice enthalpy.
2. Charge on the ions — the higher the product of the charges, the stronger the attraction and the more stable the solid.MgO (2+/2−) has a lattice enthalpy near 3800 kJ mol⁻¹ against about 788 kJ mol⁻¹ for NaCl (1+/1−) — nearly five times larger, which is why MgO melts above 2800 °C.
3. Size of the ions — smaller ions sit closer, so (r⁺ + r⁻) falls and U rises.Down group 1 the halide lattice enthalpies fall steadily: LiCl > NaCl > KCl > RbCl > CsCl, purely because the cation gets bigger.
4. Ease of ion formation — a low ionisation enthalpy for the metal and a highly negative electron gain enthalpy for the non-metal make the overall formation exothermic.
5. Packing and lattice type — the Madelung constant of the arrangement, and the requirement that the compound be formed from a metal and a non-metal of sufficiently different electronegativity so that transfer, not sharing, occurs.
6. Write the one-sentence answer demanded by the paper: the stability of an ionic compound is measured by its lattice enthalpy, which depends on the charges of the ions and the distance (sizes) between them, together with the ionisation enthalpy of the metal and the electron gain enthalpy of the non-metal.

2Give the total number of electrons around sulphur (S) in the SF6 compound.Expanded octet in SF₆Lewis structure of SF₆ — counting the electrons around sulphur🟢 Exact—
Concept Sulphur sits in period 3, so it has empty 3d orbitals lying close in energy to 3s and 3p. That lets it break the octet rule and accommodate more than eight electrons in its valence shell — an expanded octet. In SF6 the sulphur promotes all six of its valence electrons into six singly-occupied orbitals (one 3s, three 3p, two 3d), hybridises them into sp³d², and forms six identical S–F σ bonds. Each bond puts two electrons around the sulphur, so the total is 6 × 2 = 12.
Key relation electrons around the central atom = 2 × (number of σ bonds) + 2 × (number of lone pairs). For SF₆ there are 6 σ bonds and 0 lone pairs ⇒ 2 × 6 + 0 = 12 electrons — a "duodectet" rather than an octet. Ground state S: 3s²3p⁴ ⇒ excited state 3s¹3p³3d² ⇒ six unpaired electrons ⇒ six bonds.
Solution
1. Ground-state sulphur is 1s²2s²2p⁶ 3s²3p⁴ — six valence electrons, but only two of them unpaired.
2. On excitation, one 3s electron and one 3p electron are promoted into two empty 3d orbitals: 3s¹ 3p³ 3d², giving six unpaired electrons.The promotion costs energy, but forming six strong S–F bonds releases far more, so the process is worth it.
3. These six orbitals mix into six equivalent sp³d² hybrid orbitals pointing at the corners of an octahedron.
4. Each hybrid overlaps with a half-filled 2p orbital of one fluorine, making six σ bonds; there is no lone pair left on sulphur.
5. Count the electrons that now belong to the sulphur valence shell: 6 bonds × 2 electrons per bond = 12 electrons (i.e. 6 electron pairs).
6. Cross-check with the whole-molecule electron count: total valence electrons = 6 (S) + 6 × 7 (F) = 48; 12 of them are in the six S–F bonds and the other 36 are the 18 lone pairs on the six fluorines (3 each). Consistent.

3Covalent bond is directional in nature. Justify.Directional characterWhy covalent bonds are directional while ionic bonds are not🟢 Exact—
Concept A covalent bond is formed by the overlap of two atomic orbitals, and atomic orbitals — apart from s — have a definite shape and a definite orientation in space (px, py, pz point along three mutually perpendicular axes). Overlap is only effective when the two orbitals approach along the axis in which they have maximum electron density. That requirement forces the bond to lie in a specific direction, which is why covalent compounds have fixed bond angles and definite shapes at all. An ionic bond, by contrast, comes from the electric field of a point-like ion, which is spherically symmetric and therefore has no preferred direction.
Key relation bond strength ∝ extent of orbital overlap Overlap is maximum along the orbital axis and falls off rapidly as the approach direction tilts away; the molecule therefore settles into the geometry that maximises overlap, giving a fixed direction to every bond. Compare: ionic field ∝ q/r² in every direction (non-directional).
Solution
1. State the origin: a covalent bond is formed by overlap of half-filled atomic orbitals of the two combining atoms.
2. Atomic orbitals other than s are oriented: the three p orbitals lie along the mutually perpendicular x, y and z axes, and d orbitals have their own fixed lobes.
3. Overlap — and hence bond strength — is maximum only along the direction of maximum electron density of those orbitals, so the bond forms in that direction and nowhere else.This is the principle of maximum overlap: sideways or oblique approach gives weaker overlap and a weaker bond, so the molecule avoids it.
4. As a consequence the molecule acquires a definite shape and definite bond angles — H2O is bent at 104.5°, NH3 pyramidal at 107°, CH4 tetrahedral at 109°28′ — none of which would exist if the bonds could point anywhere.
5. A further consequence: covalent compounds can show isomerism and optical activity, both of which require a fixed spatial arrangement of bonds.
6. Contrast with the ionic case to complete the justification: an ion's electric field is the same in every direction, so a cation attracts anions from all sides equally. There is no "one bond" pointing one way — hence ionic bonds are non-directional and ionic solids form extended lattices rather than discrete molecules.

4What is Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory (VSEPR)?Statement of VSEPRVSEPR theory — basic introduction and statement🟢 Exact—
Concept VSEPR is a predictive model for molecular shape. Its whole content is one physical idea: the electron pairs in the valence shell of the central atom are all negatively charged, so they repel one another and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Once you know how many pairs there are, the geometry is fixed by pure geometry — 2 pairs go 180° apart, 3 pairs 120° apart, 4 pairs to the corners of a tetrahedron, and so on. The theory was proposed by Sidgwick and Powell and refined by Gillespie (1957) to predict bond angles more exactly.
Statement "The shape of a molecule is determined by the total number of electron pairs (bond pairs + lone pairs) in the valence shell of the central atom, which arrange themselves so that the repulsion between them is minimum." Repulsion order: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair — a lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so its cloud is fatter and closer in, and it pushes harder.
Solution
1. Give the name and origin: Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory, put forward by Sidgwick and Powell and developed by Gillespie in 1957 to predict and explain molecular shapes and bond angles.
2. State the central postulate: the shape depends on the total number of electron pairs — bond pairs and lone pairs — around the central atom.
3. State the driving principle: these pairs, being alike in charge, repel one another and take up positions of minimum repulsion, i.e. maximum separation.
4. Give the repulsion order, since it is what makes the theory quantitative: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp.This is why NH₃ (one lone pair) closes to 107° and H₂O (two lone pairs) to 104.5°, both below the ideal 109°28′.

5Explain the rules of Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory (VSEPR).Rules of VSEPRVSEPR theory, its rules and the shapes of molecules (full JEE lecture)🟢 Exact—
Concept The bare statement of VSEPR ("pairs repel, so they spread out") only predicts the ideal geometries. The five rules Gillespie added are the corrections that turn ideal angles into real ones: they rank the repulsions, allow for multiple bonds needing more room, and describe how electronegativity and back bonding shift the angles further. Every "why is this angle smaller/larger" question in the chapter is answered by one of these five rules.
Key relations Rule 1: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp. Rule 2: double–double > double–single > single–single. Rule 3: EN of surrounding atom ↑ ⇒ angle ↓. Rule 4: EN of central atom ↑ ⇒ angle ↑. Rule 5: back bonding transfers a lone pair from a filled shell into a vacant orbital of the adjacent atom. Rules 3 and 4 both work by moving the bond pair closer to, or further from, the central atom — the closer the pair, the harder it pushes its neighbour and the wider the angle.
Solution
1. Rule 1 — order of repulsions. Electron pairs minimise repulsion, and the strength runs lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair (here "bond pair" means a single bond). The shape therefore depends on the repulsions between bond pairs and lone pairs.A lone pair is attracted by only one nucleus, so its cloud spreads wider and closer to the central atom and pushes hardest — this is why every lone pair shrinks the bond angle.
2. Rule 2 — multiple bonds need more space. A double bond is fatter than a single bond, and the repulsion order in relation to bonds is double–double > double–single > single–single.In COCl₂ the C=O pushes the two C–Cl bonds together, so Cl–C–Cl falls to about 111° while O=C–Cl opens to about 124.5°.
3. Rule 3 — electronegativity of the surrounding atom. Keeping the central atom (with a lone pair) the same, if the electronegativity of the surrounding atom increases, the bond angle decreases, provided no other factor such as size or back bonding interferes.The shared pair is dragged away from the central atom, so the two bond pairs repel each other less: NH₃ 107° > NF₃ 102.5°, and H₂O 104.5° > OF₂ 103°.
4. Rule 4 — electronegativity of the central atom. Keeping the surrounding atoms the same, if the electronegativity of the central atom (carrying the lone pair) increases, the bond angle increases.A more electronegative centre holds the bond pairs closer to itself, so they crowd one another more: NH₃ 107° > PH₃ 93.6° > AsH₃ 91.8° > SbH₃ 91.3°.
5. Rule 5 — back bonding. Sometimes a lone pair is transferred from the filled shell of one atom into an unfilled orbital of the adjacent bonded atom; this phenomenon is called back bonding.In BF₃ the fluorine lone pairs feed into the empty 2p of boron (2pπ–2pπ), shortening and strengthening the B–F bonds; in (SiH₃)₃N back bonding into silicon's d orbitals flattens the nitrogen to trigonal planar instead of pyramidal.
