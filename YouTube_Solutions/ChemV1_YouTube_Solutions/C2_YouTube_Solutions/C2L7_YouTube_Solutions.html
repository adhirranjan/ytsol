1Which series of the hydrogen spectrum lies in the UV region? (Lyman)Spectral series / regionsThe Lyman series of hydrogen spectrum lies in the region🟢 ExactcLyman
Concept The hydrogen line spectrum is split into series, and each series is fixed by the level the electron falls to, the lower level n₁. Because energy released grows as n₁ gets smaller, the series that ends on the lowest level (n₁ = 1) carries the most energetic photons — highest frequency, shortest wavelength — which places it in the ultraviolet. That series is the Lyman series; Balmer (n₁ = 2) is visible, and Paschen/Brackett/Pfund (n₁ = 3, 4, 5) lie in the infrared.
Formula 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), with R = Rydberg constant, n₁ = final level, n₂ = initial level (n₂ > n₁). A smaller n₁ makes 1/n₁² large, so 1/λ is large and λ is short — pushing the series toward the high-energy UV end.
Solution
1. Sort the named series by their final level n₁: Lyman → 1, Balmer → 2, Paschen → 3, Brackett → 4, Pfund → 5.n₁ alone decides the energy band of the whole series.
2. The UV region needs the shortest wavelengths, i.e. the largest 1/λ, i.e. the smallest n₁.
3. The smallest possible n₁ is 1, which is the Lyman series — option (c).
 series     n₁   region
 Lyman       1   ultraviolet   <-- answer
 Balmer      2   visible
 Paschen     3   infrared
 Brackett    4   infrared
 Pfund       5   infrared
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the region ladder "Ly-UV, Bal-Visible, everything-else-IR" keyed to n₁ = 1, 2, ≥3. Any question naming a series and asking its region is instant. Trap: the region depends only on n₁ (where it lands), never on n₂ (where it starts) — don't be misled by a large starting level.
Answer: (c) Lyman.
2Wavelength of first line of Lyman series (2→1) in terms of R [4/(3R)]Rydberg formulawatch?v=v8zOEE7UiGY🟢 Exactb4/(3R)
Concept The first line of any spectral series is the transition from the level just above the series base, so for Lyman (base n₁ = 1) it is 2 → 1. "In terms of R" means the answer stays symbolic: evaluate the Rydberg bracket as an exact fraction and invert it — no value of R is ever substituted. The first line is also the longest-wavelength line of its series, because the 2 → 1 jump is the smallest energy gap that still lands on n = 1.
Formula 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) with n₁ = 1, n₂ = 2. For hydrogen Z = 1, so no Z² factor is needed; the bracket alone fixes λ as a multiple of 1/R.
Solution
1. Identify the transition: first Lyman line = n₂ = 2 → n₁ = 1.The k-th line of a series starts k levels above the base level.
2. Evaluate the bracket: 1/1² − 1/2² = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.
3. Insert into Rydberg: 1/λ = R × 3/4 = 3R/4.
4. Invert for wavelength: λ = 1/(3R/4) = 4/(3R) — option (b).Flipping the fraction flips numerator and denominator; forgetting to invert is the classic slip here.
5. Numeric feel: λ = 4/(3 × 1.097 × 10⁷) = 1.216 × 10⁻⁷ m ≈ 121.6 nm — the famous Lyman-α line, deep UV as every Lyman member must be. ✓

 λ               invert                 4/(3R)   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the fraction pair: first Lyman line λ = 4/3R, Lyman limit λ = 1/R — most "in terms of R" MCQs are one of these. Trap: options usually include 3R/4 (the un-inverted 1/λ) and 4/R (bracket botched); always finish by inverting and sanity-check that λ > 1/R is impossible for Lyman — 4/(3R) ≈ 1.33/R is the longest Lyman line, consistent.
Answer: (b) λ = 4/(3R).
3Electron in H at n=5 de-excites to ground — distinct spectral lines? [10]Line countingwatch?v=uiWPfMWFMbA🟢 Exactb10
Concept When an electron sits at n = 5 and de-excites to the ground state by every possible route, a large sample of atoms emits one photon for every ordered pair of levels (higher → lower) between 5 and 1. Counting "distinct spectral lines" is therefore pure combinatorics — choose any 2 levels out of the 5 involved; the higher one is the start, the lower the end. Energy values never enter the count.
Formula N = n(n − 1)/2 for de-excitation from level n down to the ground state. This is ⁵C₂: each unordered pair of levels gives exactly one line because the direction (high → low) is forced in emission.
Solution
1. Levels involved: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 — five levels in total, since the electron may cascade through any intermediate level.A single atom takes one path, but the question asks over all atoms/paths.
2. Apply the formula: N = n(n − 1)/2 = 5 × 4/2 = 10.
3. Cross-check by series: Lyman (→1): 4 lines; Balmer (→2): 3; Paschen (→3): 2; Brackett (→4): 1. Total 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10. ✓
4. So 10 distinct lines appear — option (b).Note this is the count of lines in the spectrum of the sample, not the photons from one atom.
 ends on level   lines from {5,4,3,2}        count
 n=1 (Lyman)     5->1 4->1 3->1 2->1        4
 n=2 (Balmer)    5->2 4->2 3->2             3
 n=3 (Paschen)   5->3 4->3                  2
 n=4 (Brackett)  5->4                       1
 total           = 4+3+2+1                  10   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Falling to ground: lines = n(n−1)/2 in one step — for n = 5 that is 10, for n = 4 it is 6. Trap: if the fall stops at some n₁ > 1, the formula becomes (n₂−n₁)(n₂−n₁+1)/2 — using n(n−1)/2 there overcounts. Second trap: "a single atom emits 10 photons" is false; 10 is the count across many atoms taking all routes.
Answer: (b) 10 distinct spectral lines.
4Why does the 2nd Balmer line have shorter λ than the 1st Paschen line?Series comparisonA-BOHR🟡 SimilaraBalmer transitions end at a lower final level (n=2) than Paschen (n=3), so the energy gaps are larger → shorter λ.
Concept Wavelength is set by the energy gap of the jump: bigger gap → more energetic photon → shorter λ. A Balmer line ends on n₁ = 2, a Paschen line on n₁ = 3. Because level energies crowd together as n rises, transitions that finish on the lower level 2 span a wider energy gap than those finishing on level 3. So even the "2nd" Balmer line out-energises the "1st" Paschen line — the deciding factor is where each series lands, not its position within the series.
Formula 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). The 2nd Balmer line is n₂ = 4 → n₁ = 2; the 1st Paschen line is n₂ = 4 → n₁ = 3. Larger 1/λ means shorter λ, so we just compare the two bracketed values.
Solution
1. Identify the transitions: "2nd Balmer" counts 3→2 (1st), 4→2 (2nd); "1st Paschen" is 4→3.The n-th line of a series starts from the level n positions above its base.
2. Balmer 4→2: 1/λ = R(1/2² − 1/4²) = R(1/4 − 1/16) = R(4/16 − 1/16) = R(3/16) = 0.1875 R.
3. Paschen 4→3: 1/λ = R(1/3² − 1/4²) = R(1/9 − 1/16) = R(16/144 − 9/144) = R(7/144) = 0.0486 R.
4. Compare: 0.1875 R > 0.0486 R, so the Balmer line has the larger 1/λ and therefore the shorter wavelength.Its energy gap is about 3.9× bigger, purely because it ends on level 2, not level 3.
 line            transition   1/λ (×R)        verdict
 2nd Balmer      4 → 2        3/16 = 0.1875   larger 1/λ → shorter λ
 1st Paschen     4 → 3        7/144 = 0.0486  smaller 1/λ → longer λ
⚡ JEE Trick To rank any two H lines, just compute 1/n₁² − 1/n₂² and pick the bigger — no R, no calculator. The final level n₁ dominates: a series ending lower (smaller n₁) always beats one ending higher, whatever the line number. Trap: "2nd vs 1st" tempts you to think the 1st line wins on λ, but the series base (n₁) outweighs the line index.
Answer: (a) Balmer ends at the lower level n = 2 vs Paschen's n = 3, giving a larger energy gap and thus shorter λ.
5Li²⁺ (Z=3): exact λ (as fraction of 1/R) of transition 3→1Hydrogen-like Rydbergwatch?v=ySUl2qZSSpc🟡 Similara1/(8R)
Concept Li²⁺ is a hydrogen-like ion — one electron orbiting a nucleus of charge Z = 3. The Rydberg formula still applies, but every energy (and hence 1/λ) is scaled up by Z² because the stronger nuclear pull deepens all the levels. So the calculation is the ordinary hydrogen one with a single extra factor of Z² = 9 inserted; the wavelength comes out 9× shorter than the corresponding H line.
Formula 1/λ = R·Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), Z = 3, transition n₂ = 3 → n₁ = 1. Z² multiplies the whole bracket because the electron's binding energy scales as Z² in a one-electron ion. Rearranged, λ = 1 / [R·Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)].
Solution
1. Evaluate the bracket for 3→1: 1/1² − 1/3² = 1 − 1/9 = 9/9 − 1/9 = 8/9.
2. Insert Z² = 3² = 9: 1/λ = R × 9 × (8/9).
3. The 9 and the 1/9 cancel: 9 × 8/9 = 8, so 1/λ = 8R.The Z² = 9 exactly cancels the 9 in the denominator of 8/9 — a neat coincidence for a 3→1 jump in Li²⁺.
4. Invert to get the wavelength: λ = 1 ÷ (8R) = 1/(8R) — option (a).
 factor          value        running 1/λ
 bracket 1−1/9    8/9          8/9 · R
 × Z² (=9)        ×9           8R
 λ = 1/(8R)                    ← answer
⚡ JEE Trick For any hydrogen-like ion just staple a Z² onto the H result: 1/λ(ion) = Z² × 1/λ(H, same jump), so λ shrinks by Z². Here Z² = 9 cancels the 9 from (1 − 1/9), collapsing the whole thing to 8R in one line. Trap: use Z of the ion (Li²⁺ → Z = 3), not the group number or the charge 2+.
Answer: (a) 1/(8R).

1State Heisenberg's uncertainty principleUncertainty principleA-HEISEN🟡 Similar—
Concept Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states a hard limit built into nature: for a very small particle such as an electron, you cannot simultaneously know both its exact position and its exact momentum. The act of pinning down one blurs the other. This is not a defect of our instruments but a fundamental property of matter that behaves as a wave, and it is precisely what dooms Bohr's picture of an electron on a sharply-defined orbit.
Key relation Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π, where Δx = uncertainty in position (m), Δp = uncertainty in momentum (kg m s⁻¹), and h = Planck's constant = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s. Their product can never fall below h/4π, so shrinking Δx toward zero forces Δp to blow up, and vice versa. Equivalently Δx · Δv ≥ h/(4πm) in terms of velocity.
Solution
1. State it in words: it is impossible to measure exactly the position and the momentum (or velocity) of a microscopic particle at the same instant.
2. Write the quantitative bound: Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π — the product of the two uncertainties has a fixed non-zero floor.The tinier you make one uncertainty, the larger the other must grow to keep the product ≥ h/4π.
3. Note why it matters only for small bodies: h/4π ≈ 5.3×10⁻³⁵ is negligible for everyday objects (huge m makes Δx·Δv immeasurably small) but significant for an electron.
4. Consequence: a well-defined Bohr orbit (exact radius and exact velocity together) violates this bound, so we speak of orbitals/probability instead.
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the exact constant on the right: h/4π (not h, not h/2π) is the standard exam form; some books write ħ/2 with ħ = h/2π — identical value. Trap: the principle pairs position with momentum (or velocity), and separately energy with time — do not mix it up with position–energy.
Key point: Heisenberg's uncertainty principle — the position and momentum of a microscopic particle cannot both be known exactly at once, with Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π; the smaller one uncertainty, the larger the other.
2What are the drawbacks of Rutherford's atomic model?Rutherford modelWrite the drawbacks of Rutherford's model of atom? How did Bohr remove them?🟢 Exact—
Concept Rutherford pictured the atom as a tiny dense nucleus with electrons circling it like planets. The fatal problem is that a circling electron is a charge that is constantly accelerating (its direction keeps changing), and classical electromagnetism says any accelerating charge must radiate energy. Losing energy continuously, the electron cannot stay in a stable orbit — the whole model predicts an atom that self-destructs, which contradicts the plainly stable, discrete-spectrum atoms we observe.
Key relation accelerating charge ⇒ continuous EM radiation ⇒ energy loss ⇒ spiral collapse. By Maxwell's electrodynamics the radiated power is non-zero for any centripetal acceleration, so an orbiting electron can never be in equilibrium. Estimates give a collapse time of only ~10⁻⁸ s.
Solution
1. Stability failure: an orbiting electron accelerates centripetally, so it must radiate, lose energy, spiral inward and crash into the nucleus in about 10⁻⁸ s.A real atom is stable indefinitely, so the model is wrong on its most basic prediction.
2. Spectrum failure: as it spirals it would emit all frequencies, giving a continuous spectrum; but hydrogen actually shows a discrete line spectrum.
3. No electronic structure: the model says nothing about the arrangement or energies of electrons — it cannot explain why definite lines appear at all.
 prediction of Rutherford model     observed reality
 electron spirals in, atom collapses   atoms are stable
 continuous emission spectrum          sharp discrete lines
 no fixed energy levels                definite quantised levels
⚡ JEE Trick The one-line killer to quote is "accelerating charge radiates ⇒ electron spirals into the nucleus ⇒ atom is unstable," plus "cannot explain the discrete line spectrum." Those two points earn the marks. Trap: don't say the nucleus is unstable — it is the electron's orbit that classical theory cannot sustain.
Key point: Rutherford's model cannot explain atomic stability (an orbiting, accelerating electron would radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus) nor the observed discrete line spectrum, and it gives no information about electron energy levels.
3On what basis did Bohr overcome the drawbacks of Rutherford's model?Bohr vs RutherfordWrite the drawbacks of Rutherford's atomic model. How did Bohr remove them? Show that different orbits🟢 Exact—
Concept Bohr rescued Rutherford's nuclear atom by grafting quantum postulates onto it. Instead of allowing every orbit, he asserted that electrons may occupy only certain allowed "stationary" orbits in which — by decree, contradicting classical physics — they do not radiate. Energy is exchanged only when an electron jumps between these fixed levels, and each jump emits or absorbs one photon of a definite energy. This simultaneously fixes the stability problem and explains why the spectrum is a set of sharp lines.
Key relation quantisation of angular momentum mvr = n·h/2π (n = 1, 2, 3 …) and the transition rule ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hν. Only orbits meeting the mvr condition are allowed, so energy is quantised; a photon appears only on a level-to-level jump, giving discrete frequencies.
Solution
1. Stationary states: Bohr postulated fixed orbits in which the electron has constant energy and does not radiate, directly overturning the classical "accelerating charge radiates" objection.If it does not radiate, it does not lose energy, so it never spirals in — the atom is stable.
2. Quantised angular momentum: allowed orbits satisfy mvr = nh/2π, selecting a discrete set of radii and energies rather than a continuum.
3. Quantum jumps: radiation is emitted/absorbed only when an electron moves between two allowed levels, with ΔE = hν — so only certain photon energies (frequencies) are possible.
4. Result: these definite ΔE values produce the observed discrete line spectrum, curing Rutherford's second failure as well.
 Rutherford drawback          Bohr's fixing postulate
 electron radiates, collapses    stationary non-radiating orbits
 allowed any orbit               mvr = nh/2π (quantised)
 continuous spectrum             ΔE = hν on jumps → sharp lines
⚡ JEE Trick Answer with the three keywords "stationary orbits, quantised angular momentum (mvr = nh/2π), ΔE = hν jumps" — each maps directly onto a Rutherford flaw. Trap: Bohr did not derive non-radiation from classical physics — it is a postulate he imposed; say "postulated," not "proved."
Key point: Bohr postulated quantised, non-radiating stationary orbits (angular momentum mvr = nh/2π) with energy exchanged only in jumps (ΔE = hν) — giving a stable atom and a discrete line spectrum, the two things Rutherford's model could not explain.
4Which H transition has same λ as Balmer n=4→2 of He⁺?Hydrogen-like ionswatch?v=aXlmv56GRp4🟢 Exact—
Concept He⁺ is hydrogen-like with Z = 2, so every 1/λ carries an extra factor Z² = 4. Two transitions in different one-electron species share the same wavelength when their products Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) are equal. Because 4 is a perfect square it can be absorbed into the quantum numbers: dividing both levels of the He⁺ transition by 2 gives an H transition with an identical bracket value — this is the standard "same λ" mapping.
Formula 1/λ = R·Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²); equal λ ⇔ Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) is equal for both species. R is the same constant for both (ignoring the tiny reduced-mass correction), so only the Z²-bracket product matters.
Solution
1. He⁺, 4 → 2: 1/λ = R × 4 × (1/2² − 1/4²) = 4R(1/4 − 1/16) = 4R × 3/16 = 3R/4.
2. For hydrogen (Z = 1) demand the same value: 1/n₁² − 1/n₂² = 3/4.
3. Solve by inspection: n₁ = 1, n₂ = 2 gives 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. ✓No other integer pair works: n₁ ≥ 2 caps the bracket at 1/4 < 3/4.
4. Shortcut check: divide He⁺ levels by Z = 2: (4/2) → (2/2) = 2 → 1 — the same result instantly.
5. So the H transition 2 → 1 (first Lyman line, λ ≈ 121.6 nm) matches the He⁺ 4 → 2 line.Both give 1/λ = 3R/4, i.e. λ = 4/(3R) ≈ 121.6 nm in each species.

1What happens when atomic H is excited by electricity at low pressure? (sharp lines)Line spectrumA-HSPEC🟡 SimilarbSpectrum of sharp lines is emitted
Concept When atomic hydrogen at low pressure is energised by an electric discharge, its electrons are kicked up to higher levels and then fall back, emitting light. Because the electron may only sit in discrete energy levels, the photons it releases can only carry certain fixed energies — so the light, spread by a prism, shows up as a set of sharp, separate lines at definite wavelengths, not a smooth rainbow. Low pressure keeps the atoms far apart so the lines stay clean and distinct.
Formula ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hν = hc/λ. Only differences between allowed levels are possible, so only a discrete set of ν (and λ) is emitted — that is what a line spectrum is.
Solution
1. The discharge excites electrons from the ground level to higher allowed levels.Energy is absorbed only in the exact amounts that match level gaps.
2. Excited electrons fall back to lower levels, each drop releasing a photon of energy ΔE = hν.
3. Since the levels — and hence the ΔE values — are discrete, only specific frequencies appear: an emission line spectrum of sharp lines, option (b).
4. Rule out the distractors: it is not a continuous spectrum (that needs a continuum of levels) and not "no emission" (excited atoms must relax).
⚡ JEE Trick "Discrete levels ⇒ discrete lines" is the whole story — a line spectrum is the fingerprint of quantised energy. Trap: hot dense sources (like a filament) give a continuous spectrum; a line spectrum specifically needs low-pressure, excited atoms.
Answer: (b) A spectrum of sharp lines is emitted.

Concept The wavelengths of every line in the hydrogen spectrum are predicted by a single empirical equation discovered by Rydberg (generalising Balmer's visible-line formula). It expresses the wave number (1/λ) of a line purely in terms of the two levels involved and one universal constant, R. It is the master tool for this entire lesson — series identification, wavelengths, wave numbers and series limits all come out of it.
Formula 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), where λ = wavelength, 1/λ = wave number (m⁻¹ or cm⁻¹), R = Rydberg constant ≈ 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, n₁ = lower level, n₂ = upper level. Only the two integers n₁, n₂ and the constant R are needed — nothing about mass or charge for plain hydrogen.
Solution
1. The question asks which named formula gives hydrogen-spectrum wavelengths — the standard answer is the Rydberg formula (b).
2. Recall its form: 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), covering all series by choice of n₁.
3. Rule out distractors: Planck's E = hν gives photon energy, not λ directly from levels; Bohr's radius/energy formulas describe orbits, not the line-wavelength relation named here.
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the master equation 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) — it answers "which formula," plus every wavelength/wave-number/series-limit numerical in this chapter. Trap: it returns 1/λ (wave number) first; remember to invert for λ, and keep n₁ < n₂.

3Name given to the last line of a series? (Limiting line)Series limitThe limiting line of any spectral series in the hydrogen spectrum is the line when n2 in the Rydberg formula🟢 ExactbLimiting line
Concept Inside any one series the lines crowd closer and closer together as the upper level n₂ climbs, converging on a single shortest-wavelength boundary. This boundary — reached when the electron comes from n₂ = ∞ (a free electron falling into the level n₁) — is the last, highest-energy line of the series. It is called the series limit or limiting line, and its wavelength is the shortest the series can produce.
Formula series limit: set n₂ → ∞ so 1/n₂² → 0, giving 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 0) = R/n₁². With the second term gone, the wave number is maximal, so λ is minimal — the convergence point of the series.
Solution
1. As n₂ increases the term 1/n₂² shrinks, so 1/λ grows and successive lines pack closer.The energy gaps between consecutive upper levels get tiny near the top.
2. The extreme case is n₂ = ∞, where 1/λ = R/n₁² is the largest value the series can reach.
3. This final converging line is named the limiting line (series limit) — option (b).
4. Distinguish from the "α line," which is the first (longest-λ) line, not the last.
⚡ JEE Trick "Last line / series limit" ⇒ plug n₂ = ∞, so 1/λ = R/n₁² — the fastest way to any series-limit numerical. Trap: don't confuse the limiting line (last, shortest λ, n₂ = ∞) with the α / first line (n₂ = n₁+1, longest λ) — they sit at opposite ends of the series.
Answer: (b) Limiting line.
4Number of lines when an electron jumps n=6 to n=3?Line countingA-LINES🟡 Similard6
Concept When an excited electron cascades down between two levels, it can take many different downward paths, and each distinct downward step produces one spectral line. Counting the lines is therefore just counting how many pairs of levels lie between the start and the end (inclusive). Here the electron does not return all the way to the ground state — it moves within the band from n = 6 down to n = 3 — so only the levels 3, 4, 5, 6 participate.
Formula for a drop from n₂ down to n₁, number of lines = (n₂ − n₁)(n₂ − n₁ + 1)/2. This counts every pair of the (n₂ − n₁ + 1) involved levels — the number of distinct emissive transitions. (When the fall is to the ground state n₁ = 1 it reduces to the familiar N(N−1)/2 with N = n₂.)
Solution
1. Identify the band: upper n₂ = 6, lower n₁ = 3, so the levels available are 3, 4, 5, 6.
2. Compute the level difference: n₂ − n₁ = 6 − 3 = 3.
3. Apply the formula: lines = 3 × (3 + 1) / 2 = 3 × 4 / 2 = 12 / 2 = 6.
4. Cross-check by listing the transitions:Each arrow is one line; counting them gives 6, confirming the formula.
