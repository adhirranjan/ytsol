1What happens to power when a heavy weight is held stationary for a long time? (a) It increases (b) It becomes infinite (c) No mechanical power is delivered (d) Maximum power is produced — (Level-1)Power needs displacementInstantaneous Power Class 11 — average vs instantaneous power🟡 SimilarcNo mechanical power is delivered
Concept Power is the rate of doing work, and mechanical work requires a displacement along the force. Holding a weight stationary means the muscle exerts a force but the point of application never moves, so W = 0 for every second that passes and hence P = 0 for every second. The physiological tiredness you feel is chemical energy burned inside the muscle fibres (they twitch and relax microscopically) — that is not mechanical work on the weight.
Formula Pavg = W / t — W in joules (J), t in seconds (s), P in watts (W = J/s). Also P = F·v cosθ — F in N, v in m/s. N × m/s = J/s = W ✓, so a zero velocity forces a zero power no matter how huge F is.
Solution
1. Identify the displacement of the load: it is held stationary, so d = 0 m and v = 0 m/s."Stationary" is the entire physics content of the question — everything else follows from it.
2. Work done on the load: W = F d cosθ = F × 0 = 0 J, no matter how long you hold it.
3. Average power: Pavg = W/t = 0/t = 0 W for every finite t.
4. Instantaneous power: P = F·v = F × 0 = 0 W as well — so both definitions agree.
5. Therefore no mechanical power is delivered — option (c).Note it does not "become infinite": that would need W/t with t → 0, but here it is the numerator that is zero, not the denominator.
   held stationary:
        ┌─────┐
        │  M  │   F(muscle) ↑ = Mg ↓        v = 0
        └─────┘
   ───────────────────────  time t grows, height h stays fixed

   W = F·d = F × 0 = 0 J      for ANY t
   P = W/t = 0/t = 0 W
   P = F·v = F × 0 = 0 W      (same conclusion)
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question says "held", "stationary", "kept at rest", or "carried horizontally at constant height", the mechanical work — and therefore the power — is zero; no calculation is needed. Trap: option (b) "It becomes infinite" tempts because P = W/t looks like it blows up for small t, but the numerator W here is identically zero, so P = 0/t = 0 for every t. Companion trap in the exam: "a man carries a bag horizontally" — gravity is ⊥ to the displacement, so again zero work.
Answer: (c) No mechanical power is delivered.
2What is the power of an engine which can lift 20 metric ton of coal per hour from a mine 20 metre deep? — (Level-2)Power = (dm/dt)·ghPower calculation — water/mass lifting pump numericals🟡 Similar1.11 × 10³ W (≈ 1111 W ≈ 1.5 hp)
Concept The engine raises coal continuously, so instead of one lump of work you have a steady rate of work. The natural quantity is the mass flow rate dm/dt (kg/s): each kilogram that comes up gains mgh of gravitational potential energy, so the power is simply (dm/dt)·gh. The coal is only lifted — the problem says nothing about ejecting it at speed — so there is no kinetic-energy term.
Formula P = (dm/dt) g h — dm/dt in kg/s, g in m/s², h in m, P in W. (kg/s)(m/s²)(m) = kg·m²/s³ = J/s = W ✓. The general pump formula is P = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2); here the delivery speed v is not asked for, so put v = 0.
Solution
1. Convert the load: 20 metric ton = 20 × 1000 kg = 20 000 kg, raised in one hour = 3600 s.
2. Mass flow rate: dm/dt = 20000/3600 = 5.556 kg/s.Converting to per-second first keeps every later unit in SI, so the answer lands in watts automatically.
3. Height lifted: h = 20 m (the depth of the mine — the coal must be raised the full depth).
4. Substitute with g = 10 m/s² (the value the book uses in its worked Example-5 of this very lecture):
  P = 5.556 × 10 × 20
5. P = 1111.1 W = 1.11 × 10³ W.With g = 9.8 m/s² you would get 1088.9 W ≈ 1.09 × 10³ W; the key's 1.11 × 10³ W confirms g = 10 m/s² is intended.
6. In horsepower (1 hp = 746 W): 1111.1/746 ≈ 1.5 hp — the same figure the book quotes in Example-5.
                 ground level
   ══════════════╤═════════════
                 │            ↑  20 000 kg per 3600 s
          20 m   │            │   (dm/dt = 5.556 kg/s)
                 │            │
   ══════════════╧═══ coal ═══╧═  mine floor

   P = (dm/dt)·g·h
     = (20000/3600) × 10 × 20
     = 5.556 × 200

⚡ JEE Trick For any "lift X kg per hour through h metres" question, collapse it to one line: P = (X·g·h)/3600 watts. Do the per-second conversion first — students who compute mgh = 20000 × 10 × 20 = 4 × 10⁶ J and then forget to divide by 3600 report 4 MW, a factor 3600 too big. Second trap: adding a ½v² term. Only add it when the question explicitly says the material is ejected/delivered with a speed.
Answer: P = 1.11 × 10³ W (1111 W, about 1.5 horsepower).
3A one kilowatt motor pumps out water from a well 10 metre deep. Calculate the quantity of water pumped out per second. (g = 9.8 m/s²) — (Level-3)Inverting P = (dm/dt)ghWater pump physics problem — power, work & energy🟡 Similar10.204 kg per second
Concept This is the previous problem run backwards: the power is given and the mass flow rate is the unknown. Because P = (dm/dt)gh is a single linear relation, no calculus or energy bookkeeping is needed — just divide. The phrase "one kilowatt motor" means the motor's useful mechanical output is 1000 W; no efficiency is mentioned, so treat the pump as 100 % efficient.
Formula P = (dm/dt) g h ⇒ dm/dt = P / (g h) — P in W, g in m/s², h in m, dm/dt in kg/s. W / [(m/s²)(m)] = (J/s)/(m²/s²) = (kg·m²/s³)(s²/m²) = kg/s ✓ — a mass flow rate, exactly what is asked.
Solution
1. List what is given: P = 1 kW = 1000 W, h = 10 m, g = 9.8 m/s² (stated in the question).
2. Each kilogram raised through 10 m needs energy gh = 9.8 × 10 = 98 J/kg.Thinking of gh as "joules needed per kilogram" makes the division below obvious.
3. The motor supplies 1000 J every second, so the number of kilograms it can raise each second is
  dm/dt = 1000 J/s ÷ 98 J/kg
4. dm/dt = 10.204 kg/s.Since 1 litre of water = 1 kg, this is also 10.204 litres per second — about 612 litres a minute.
5. Cross-check by putting the answer back: 10.204 × 9.8 × 10 = 1000.0 W ✓.
        ┌──────────┐
        │ MOTOR    │  P = 1000 W  (= 1000 J every second)
        │  1 kW    │
        └────┬─────┘
             │  water raised at dm/dt kg/s
   ══════════╪═════════ ground
             │  ↑
      10 m   │  │   energy needed per kg  =  g h = 9.8 × 10 = 98 J/kg
             │  │
   ══════════╧══╧═════ water surface

   dm/dt = P /(g h) = 1000 / 98 = 10.204 kg/s
⚡ JEE Trick Read gh as "joules per kilogram" and the whole family of pump problems becomes one division: (watts) ÷ (J per kg) = kg per second. Trap: multiplying instead of dividing gives 1000 × 98 = 98 000, which is neither a mass nor a mass rate — a quick unit check kills it. Second trap: the question says "per second", so do not convert to per-minute or per-hour.
Answer: 10.204 kg of water per second (≈ 10.2 litres/s).
4An elevator of total mass (elevator + passenger) 1800 kg is moving up with a constant speed of 2 ms⁻¹. A frictional force of 4000 N opposes its motion. Determine the minimum power delivered by the motor to the elevator. Take g = 10 ms⁻². — (Level-4)Constant-speed lift, P = FvElevator 1800 kg moving up at 2 m/s with 4000 N friction — minimum power🟢 Exact59.78 Hp (= 44 000 W = 44 kW)
Concept "Constant speed" is the key phrase: the acceleration is zero, so the motor's upward pull must exactly balance everything pulling down — the weight and the 4000 N friction (friction opposes the motion, and the motion is upward, so friction acts downward). Once the force is fixed, power at constant speed is the trivial product P = Fv. "Minimum" power just means we ignore any extra losses inside the motor.
Formula Equilibrium: F = mg + f — m in kg, g in m/s², f in N, F in N. Power: P = F v — v in m/s, P in W. N × m/s = J/s = W ✓. Conversion: 1 hp = 746 W (imperial) or 736 W (metric).
Solution
1. Since the speed is constant, a = 0, so Newton's second law reduces to a force balance.If a were non-zero we would need F = m(g+a) + f and the power would change with time — constant speed is what makes P a single number.
2. Weight of the loaded elevator: mg = 1800 × 10 = 18 000 N (down).
3. Friction opposes the upward motion, so it acts downward: f = 4000 N.
4. Motor force needed: F = 18000 + 4000 = 22 000 N (up).
5. Power: P = F v = 22000 × 2 = 44 000 W = 44 kW.
6. In horsepower: 44000 / 736 = 59.78 Hp, which is the printed key value.The book has used the metric horsepower, 1 hp = 736 W, here. With the imperial 1 hp = 746 W you would get 58.98 ≈ 59 Hp (the NCERT figure). The physics — 44 kW — is identical either way; only the hp convention differs.
           F (motor)  ↑ 22 000 N
              ┌──────────────┐
              │   ELEVATOR   │   v = 2 m/s  (constant, so a = 0)
              │  + PASSENGER │
              │   1800 kg    │
              └──────────────┘
        friction f ↓ 4000 N     weight mg ↓ 18 000 N

   a = 0  ⇒  F = mg + f = 18000 + 4000 = 22 000 N
   P = F·v = 22000 × 2 = 44 000 W = 44 kW
   in hp  : 44000 / 736 = 59.78 Hp    (metric hp, as in the key)
            44000 / 746 = 58.98 Hp    (imperial hp)
⚡ JEE Trick At constant speed the motor never has to supply a ma term — you only add up the resisting forces and multiply by v. Learn the sign rule: friction always points against the velocity, so for an ascending lift it adds to the weight; for a descending lift it would subtract. Trap: computing only mgv = 36 kW and forgetting the 4000 N friction — that is the single commonest slip on this NCERT-style problem, and it costs 8 kW.
Answer: P = 44 000 W = 44 kW = 59.78 Hp (using the metric hp, 1 hp = 736 W).
5A pump on the ground floor of a building can pump up water to fill a tank of volume 30 m³ in 15 minute. If the tank is 40 m above the ground and the efficiency of the pump is 30%, how much electric power is consumed by the pump? — (Level-5)Pump power with efficiencyEngine pumps water through a height — power with efficiency🟡 Similar4.44 × 10⁴ W (≈ 44.4 kW)
Concept Two layers here. First the useful (output) power — the rate at which gravitational PE is being handed to the water. Then the efficiency step: only 30 % of the electrical power drawn from the mains ends up as that useful power, so the electrical input is the output divided by 0.30 (not multiplied). Volume must be turned into mass through the density of water, 1000 kg/m³.
Formula Mass from volume: m = ρV — ρ = 1000 kg/m³, V in m³, m in kg. Useful power: Pout = mgh/t — J/s = W. Efficiency: η = Pout/Pin ⇒ Pin = Pout/η. η is a pure ratio (dimensionless), so Pin stays in watts ✓.
Solution
1. Mass of water: m = ρV = 1000 × 30 = 30 000 kg.
2. Time: 15 minute = 15 × 60 = 900 s.
3. Useful work done against gravity: W = mgh = 30000 × 10 × 40 = 1.2 × 10⁷ J (using g = 10 m/s²).
4. Useful (output) power: Pout = W/t = 1.2 × 10⁷ / 900 = 13 333.3 W.
5. Apply the efficiency — the mains must supply more than the water receives:
  Pin = Pout/η = 13333.3 / 0.30Dividing by a number smaller than 1 increases the answer, which is the physically correct direction — 70 % of the electricity is wasted as heat and noise.
6. Pin = 44 444 W ≈ 4.44 × 10⁴ W.With g = 9.8 m/s² the answer would be 4.36 × 10⁴ W; the key's 4.44 × 10⁴ W confirms g = 10 m/s².

                 │░░░░░ water ░░░│  (m = ρV = 30 000 kg)
                 └───────┬───────┘
                         │
                 h = 40 m│

   ══════ ground ════════╧════  ┌───────┐

   P_out = mgh/t = (30000)(10)(40)/900 = 13 333 W

Trap: 13333 × 0.30 = 4000 W, which would mean the pump gives out more than it takes in — impossible, spot it instantly. 1A body is moved along a straight line by a machine delivering a constant power. The distance, moved by the body in time t, is proportional to (a) √t (b) t3/4 (c) t3/2 (d) t² — (Level-1)Constant-power kinematicsBody moved by a machine delivering constant power — distance ∝ ?🟢 Exactct3/2
Concept With constant power the force is not constant — as the body speeds up, F = P/v must shrink. So the SUVAT formulas are illegal here. The clean route is energy: constant power means the kinetic energy grows linearly with time, which immediately fixes v(t), and one integration then gives x(t). This "P constant ⇒ v ∝ √t ⇒ x ∝ t3/2" chain is one of the most-repeated results in JEE.
Formula W = P t (P in W, t in s, W in J) and the work–energy theorem W = ΔKE = ½mv² (m in kg, v in m/s). Then v = dx/dt and x = ∫v dt (m). ½mv² has units kg·m²/s² = J and Pt has W·s = J ✓, so the two may legitimately be equated.
Solution
1. Constant power acting for a time t delivers work W = P t.Only because P is constant can we write W = Pt instead of ∫P dt.
2. The body starts from rest, so all of that work becomes kinetic energy: ½ m v² = P t.
3. Solve for v: v = √(2Pt/m) = √(2P/m) · t1/2 ⇒ v ∝ t1/2 (m/s).
4. Integrate the velocity to get distance: x = ∫₀ᵗ √(2P/m) t′1/2 dt′ = √(2P/m) · (2/3) t3/2.∫t1/2dt = (2/3)t3/2 — the exponent rises by 1, which is exactly why ½ becomes 3/2.
5. All the front factors are constants, so x ∝ t3/2 — option (c).
6. Sanity check: for a constant force we would get x ∝ t²; constant power is weaker at large t because the force fades, so the exponent must be smaller than 2 — and 3/2 < 2 ✓.
   constant POWER  (not constant force)

   W = P t          ┐
   W = ½ m v²       ┘ ⇒  v = √(2P/m) · t^(1/2)

   x = ∫ v dt = √(2P/m) · (2/3) t^(3/2)

   ⇒  v ∝ t^(1/2)      a ∝ t^(-1/2)      x ∝ t^(3/2)
   (compare: constant FORCE gives v ∝ t , x ∝ t² )
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the constant-power triple: v ∝ t1/2, x ∝ t3/2, a ∝ t−1/2. Also worth storing: at constant power v ∝ x1/3. Trap: option (b) t3/4 is a printed-book variant of this same stem and is the commonest wrong pick — do not pattern-match, do the two-line derivation. Option (d) t² is the constant-force result.
Answer: (c) distance ∝ t3/2.
2A body starts from rest and acquires a velocity V in time T. The instantaneous power delivered to the body in time t is proportional to: (a) (V/T)t (b) (V²/T)t² (c) (V²/T²)t (d) (V²/T²)t² — (Level-1)Instantaneous power, uniform accelerationInstantaneous power — worked examples (Class 11)🟡 Similarc(V²/T²) t
Concept "Starts from rest and acquires V in time T" is code for uniform acceleration a = V/T. Uniform acceleration means a constant force, and a constant force with a linearly growing speed gives a power that grows linearly with time. So this is the mirror image of Q1: there the power was constant and the force faded; here the force is constant and the power grows.
Formula a = V/T (m/s²), F = ma (N), v = at (m/s), P = F v (W). P = (mV/T)(Vt/T) = mV²t/T² — units kg·(m²/s²)·s/s² = kg·m²/s³ = W ✓.
Solution
1. Uniform acceleration from rest to V in time T: a = (V − 0)/T = V/T m/s².
2. Constant driving force: F = m a = m V / T N — constant, because a is constant.
3. Speed at any earlier instant t: v = a t = (V/T) t m/s.Valid only for t ≤ T, i.e. while the uniform acceleration lasts — which is why the question says "in time t".
4. Instantaneous power: P = F v = (mV/T)·(Vt/T)
5. P = m V² t / T². The mass m is a fixed constant of the body, so P ∝ (V²/T²) t — option (c).
6. Consistency check at t = T: P = mV²/T, while the average power over the whole trip is ΔKE/T = (½mV²)/T = half of that — exactly right for a quantity rising linearly from zero ✓.
   uniform acceleration      a = V/T   (constant)

   F = m a  = m V / T        (constant, horizontal line)
   v = a t  = (V/T) t        (straight line through origin)
   ------------------------------------------------------
   P = F v  = m V² t / T²    ⇒  P ∝ t     (straight line)

        P │            ╱
          │         ╱          slope = m V²/T²
          │      ╱
          └────────────── t
                       T
   avg power over 0→T = ½ m V²/T = ½ × (final power) ✓
⚡ JEE Trick "From rest, uniform acceleration" ⇒ P ∝ t, always — a one-line answer. And whenever P rises linearly from zero, the average power is exactly half the final power; that shortcut kills a whole class of MCQs without integrating. Trap: option (d) (V²/T²)t² comes from mistakenly writing v = at² instead of v = at. Option (a) (V/T)t has the dimensions of a velocity, not a power.
Answer: (c) P ∝ (V²/T²) t (exactly, P = mV²t/T²).
3When a pump lifts water from a well and delivers it with velocity v, the total power delivered is: (a) dm/dt · gh (b) ½mv² (c) dm/dt (gh + v²/2) (d) ½ gh · dm/dt — (Level-1)Pump power formulaPhysics 8 — Work, Energy and Power: pumping water🟡 Similarc(dm/dt)(gh + v²/2)
Concept A pump that delivers water at a speed v is doing two jobs at once: it raises the water through the height h (potential energy) and it launches it at speed v (kinetic energy). The total energy handed to each kilogram is therefore gh + v²/2 joules per kilogram, and multiplying by the mass flow rate dm/dt converts joules-per-kilogram into joules-per-second, i.e. watts. This is exactly the derivation printed on book page 181.
Formula In a small time dt an amount dm is delivered: dW = (dm) gh + ½ (dm) v² (J). Divide by dt: P = dW/dt = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2) — dm/dt in kg/s, gh and v²/2 both in J/kg, P in W. Both gh (m²/s²) and v²/2 (m²/s²) carry the same units, which is precisely why they may be added inside the bracket ✓.
Solution
1. Take a small slug of water of mass dm that the pump handles in time dt.
2. Energy given to it as gravitational PE (raised through the well depth h): (dm) g h J.
3. Energy given to it as KE (it leaves the outlet with speed v, having started essentially at rest): ½ (dm) v² J.
4. Total work in time dt: dW = (dm) gh + ½ (dm) v² = (dm)(gh + v²/2).
5. Power is the rate of that work: P = dW/dt = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2) — option (c).dm/dt factors out cleanly because both terms are proportional to dm.
6. Limiting checks: put v = 0 (water just lifted, not squirted) and you recover P = (dm/dt)gh — the formula used in Suggested Problems 2 and 3 ✓. Put h = 0 (a horizontal jet) and you get P = ½(dm/dt)v² — the formula used in Level-3 Q2 ✓.
                         outlet, speed v
                              ↗↗↗
   ═════════ ground ══════════╤══════
                              │  ↑
                     h        │  │  each kg gains:
                              │  │     g h        (potential)
                              │  │   + v²/2       (kinetic)
   ═════════ water ═══════════╧══╧══

   dW = (dm) g h + ½ (dm) v²
   P  = dW/dt = (dm/dt) ( g h + v²/2 )       ← option (c)

   v = 0 ⇒ P = (dm/dt) g h        h = 0 ⇒ P = ½ (dm/dt) v²
⚡ JEE Trick Store the master pump formula P = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2) and read the stem for which term survives: "just lift / raise" ⇒ keep only gh; "ejects / delivers with a speed" ⇒ keep both. Trap: option (b) ½mv² is an energy (joules), not a power — killed by units alone. Option (d) carries a stray ½ on the gh term, which would mean each kg needs only half the potential energy it really needs.
Answer: (c) P = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2).
4What does the term dm/dt represent in the context of a pump? (a) Total mass (b) Mass per unit area (c) Rate of mass flow (d) Water density — (Level-1)Meaning of dm/dtWork and power calculations for a water pump🟡 SimilarcRate of mass flow
Concept This is a definition question, but it is best answered by dimensional reading rather than by memory: any symbol of the form d(quantity)/dt means "how fast that quantity is changing", so dm/dt is "how many kilograms pass per second". In pump problems it is the one number that converts a per-kilogram energy (gh + v²/2) into a power.
Formula dm/dt = (mass delivered) / (time taken) — kg/s. The three distractors have different dimensions: total mass = kg, mass per unit area = kg/m², density = kg/m³. Only "rate of mass flow" is kg/s ✓. Useful relation: for a fluid of density ρ flowing at volume rate dV/dt, dm/dt = ρ (dV/dt).
Solution
1. Read the notation literally: the numerator dm is a small mass (kg); the denominator dt is a small time (s).
2. So the ratio has units kg/s — a mass per unit time, not per unit area or volume.Attaching units to a symbol before choosing an option eliminates three of the four distractors instantly.
3. Physically, dm/dt is the number of kilograms of water the pump moves through its outlet each second — the rate of mass flow. Option (c).
4. Check against the book's own usage: in Example-5 it writes dm/dt = 20000/3600 kg/s — a mass divided by a time ✓.
5. Practical conversion for water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³): a flow of 0.50 m³ per minute means dm/dt = 1000 × 0.50/60 = 8.33 kg/s — exactly the number needed in Level-5 Q9.
   dm  ──►  kilograms                dm      kg

5What happens to power when a heavy weight is held stationary for a long time? (a) It increases (b) It becomes infinite (c) No mechanical power is delivered (d) Maximum power is produced — (Level-1)Zero displacement ⇒ zero powerPower (average and instantaneous) — micro lesson🟡 SimilarcNo mechanical power is delivered
Concept Identical to Suggested Problem 1 — the book repeats it deliberately, because it is the statement the whole lecture opens with. Power is work per unit time, and work requires displacement along the force. A stationary load has zero displacement for all time, so both the numerator W and the instantaneous product F⃗·v⃗ vanish.
Formula Pavg = W/t and Pinst = F⃗·v⃗ = Fv cosθ — F in N, v in m/s, P in W. Both reduce to zero when v = 0, and they must agree, since Pavg is simply the time-average of Pinst ✓.
Solution

Concept The spring's power is P = (spring force) × (block speed) = kx·v. Energy conservation on a smooth floor gives v as a function of x, so P becomes a single function of x — zero at both ends (at x = x₀ the block is momentarily at rest, at x = 0 the spring force vanishes) and therefore maximum somewhere in between. Maximising x²(x₀² − x²) is a standard "product of two terms with a fixed sum" problem, and the answer is the geometric middle, x = x₀/√2.
Formula Energy conservation: ½kx₀² = ½kx² + ½mv² ⇒ v = √[(k/m)(x₀² − x²)] (m/s). Spring force F = kx (N). Power P = Fv = kx√[(k/m)(x₀² − x²)] (W). N × m/s = W ✓. Note the coefficient inside the root is k/m, not k/2m — the two ½'s cancel when solving for v.
Solution
1. Energy conservation from the fully compressed state (block at rest) to a general deflection x:
  ½ k x₀² = ½ k x² + ½ m v²
2. Solve for the speed — every term carries a ½, so all three cancel:
  m v² = k(x₀² − x²) ⇒ v = √[(k/m)(x₀² − x²)].This kills option (a), which carries k/2m inside the root — it is smaller by a factor √2 and is the deliberate trap of this question.
3. Instantaneous power of the spring: P = F v = k x √[(k/m)(x₀² − x²)]. So (a) is WRONG (wrong constant inside the root).
4. To maximise P, maximise P² = k³/m · x²(x₀² − x²). Write u = x²; the product u(x₀² − u) has two factors whose sum is the constant x₀², so it peaks when they are equal: u = x₀² − u ⇒ x² = x₀²/2.
5. Hence x = x₀/√2 — (c) is CORRECT ✓, and (d) x₀/2 is WRONG.
