Concept Every half-angle ratio can be written using cosA alone. The engine is the double-angle cosine, which comes in two "flavoured" forms — one that isolates sin², one that isolates cos². Replacing A by A/2 in those forms turns them into expressions for sin(A/2) and cos(A/2) purely in terms of cosA. The ± sign is unavoidable: knowing cosA fixes A only up to sign/quadrant, so A/2 can land in two different quadrants.
Formula cos2θ = 1 − 2sin²θ and cos2θ = 2cos²θ − 1. Put θ = A/2 (so 2θ = A) to get cosA = 1 − 2sin²(A/2) and cosA = 2cos²(A/2) − 1.
Solution
1. Start from cosA = 1 − 2sin²(A/2) and solve for sin²(A/2): 2sin²(A/2) = 1 − cosA.Move the sin² term across and cosA to the other side.
2. Divide by 2 and take the square root: sin(A/2) = ±√((1 − cosA)/2).The ± appears because squaring lost the sign; the quadrant of A/2 decides it.
3. Now use cosA = 2cos²(A/2) − 1 and solve for cos²(A/2): 2cos²(A/2) = 1 + cosA.
4. Divide by 2 and root: cos(A/2) = ±√((1 + cosA)/2).Same algebra, but the sign of cosA flips from − to +.
5. Divide step 2 by step 4 — tan = sin ÷ cos — the halves of 2 cancel: tan(A/2) = ±√((1 − cosA)/(1 + cosA)).
6. Invert step 5, since cot is the reciprocal of tan: cot(A/2) = ±√((1 + cosA)/(1 − cosA)).

4Match Column-I → Column-II: tan2A, sin2A, cos2A with their double-angle formulae.Double-angle formulae recallDouble-angle identities for sin2A, cos2A, tan2A🟡 SimilarA→r, B→p, C→q, (D)→s
Concept All three double-angle formulae are just the compound-angle formulae with B replaced by A — there is nothing new to memorise, only a substitution. cos2A is the interesting one: using sin²A + cos²A = 1 you can trade sin² for cos² (or vice versa), which spawns three equivalent forms. Each form exists for a reason — pick whichever leaves you holding the ratio you were given.
Formula Start from sin(A+B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB, cos(A+B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB, tan(A+B) = (tanA + tanB)/(1 − tanA tanB), then set B = A.
Solution
1. sin2A = sin(A+A) = sinA cosA + cosA sinA = 2 sinA cosA.The two terms are identical, so they add to twice one of them.
2. cos2A = cos(A+A) = cosA cosA − sinA sinA = cos²A − sin²A.
3. Swap cos²A = 1 − sin²A into step 2: cos2A = (1 − sin²A) − sin²A = 1 − 2sin²A.This form is what you want when the question hands you sinA.
4. Swap sin²A = 1 − cos²A into step 2 instead: cos2A = cos²A − (1 − cos²A) = 2cos²A − 1.Use this form when cosA is the given quantity.
5. tan2A = tan(A+A) = (tanA + tanA)/(1 − tanA·tanA) = 2 tanA/(1 − tan²A).
6. Divide step 1 and step 2 by cos²A to get the tan-only versions: sin2A = 2tanA/(1 + tan²A) and cos2A = (1 − tan²A)/(1 + tan²A).These are the forms used in the "t-substitution" and are worth recognising on sight.

⚡ JEE Trick Choose the cos2A form by what you are given: handed sinA ⇒ use 1 − 2sin²A; handed cosA ⇒ use 2cos²A − 1; handed tanA ⇒ use (1 − tan²A)/(1 + tan²A). Picking correctly means zero Pythagorean side-work. Trap: sin2A and tan2A look alike but the signs differ — sin2A carries 1 + tan²A, tan2A carries 1 − tan²A. Mixing them up is the single most common slip here.

Concept The whole problem turns on one hidden fact: secA − tanA and secA + tanA are reciprocals, because their product is sec²A − tan²A = 1. So being handed secA − tanA = 1/4 silently hands you secA + tanA = 4 as well. With two linear equations in secA and tanA, adding and subtracting recovers both — no quadrants, no surds, no Pythagorean square roots.
Formula sec²A − tan²A = 1, so (secA − tanA)(secA + tanA) = 1. Then cosA = 1/secA, sinA = tanA · cosA, and sin2A = 2 sinA cosA. sec² − tan² = 1 is just sin² + cos² = 1 divided through by cos²A.
Solution
1. Write the given: secA − tanA = 1/4. Call this equation (i).
2. Use the identity (secA − tanA)(secA + tanA) = sec²A − tan²A = 1.A difference of squares — exactly the Pythagorean identity in sec/tan form.
3. Substitute (i) into step 2: (1/4)(secA + tanA) = 1, so secA + tanA = 4. Call this equation (ii).This is the crucial move — the second equation comes free, without any extra information.
4. Add (i) and (ii): the tangents cancel, giving 2secA = 1/4 + 4 = 17/4.
5. So secA = 17/8, and therefore cosA = 1/secA = 8/17.
6. Subtract (i) from (ii): the secants cancel, giving 2tanA = 4 − 1/4 = 15/4.
7. So tanA = 15/8.Note the 8-15-17 Pythagorean triple emerging — a good sign the arithmetic is clean.
8. Recover sinA = tanA × cosA = (15/8) × (8/17) = 15/17.The 8s cancel neatly.
9. Apply the double-angle formula: sin2A = 2 × (15/17) × (8/17) = 2 × 120/289 = 240/289 — option (b).
