1Which of the following hybridisations corresponds to a bond angle of 120°? (a) sp (b) sp² (c) sp³ (d) sp³dHybridisation ↔ bond anglesp and sp² hybridisation — bond angle and geometry from first principles🟡 Similarbsp²
Concept Hybrid orbitals on a central atom are all equivalent and mutually repelling, so they spread out to the geometry that puts them as far apart as possible. The number of hybrid orbitals therefore fixes the angle between them, and that number is the steric number — σ bonds plus lone pairs on the central atom. Two hybrids must sit opposite each other (180°), three must sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle (360° ÷ 3 = 120°), and four must go to the corners of a tetrahedron (109°28′). The question gives the angle and asks you to run this mapping backwards.
Key relation steric number (SN) = number of σ bonds + number of lone pairs, and SN = 2 → sp (180°), SN = 3 → sp² (120°), SN = 4 → sp³ (109°28′), SN = 5 → sp³d (120° & 90°), SN = 6 → sp³d² (90°). The s-character of each hybrid is 1/SN for the sp-series: sp = 50%, sp² = 33.3%, sp³ = 25% — and bond angle rises with s-character, which is why the angles fall 180° → 120° → 109°28′.
Solution
1. Read off the target angle: 120°. This is the interior angle of an equilateral triangle, i.e. a trigonal planar arrangement.
2. A trigonal planar set has three equivalent directions, so three atomic orbitals must have been mixed: one s + two p.One s and two p orbitals give exactly three hybrids ⇒ the label is sp², matching SN = 3.
3. Confirm with real molecules: BF₃, BCl₃, SO₃ and C₂H₄'s carbons are all sp² and all show 120° angles.
4. Eliminate the other options one by one:
  • (a) sp — only two hybrids, they must be collinear ⇒ 180° (BeCl₂, CO₂, C₂H₂), not 120°.
  • (c) sp³ — four hybrids ⇒ tetrahedral 109°28′ (CH₄, NH₄⁺, SiH₄), not 120°.
  • (d) sp³d — five hybrids ⇒ trigonal bipyramidal, which has two different angles (120° equatorial and 90° axial), so it is not "the" 120° hybridisation the question is after.
 SN  hybridisation  orbitals mixed   geometry               bond angle    example
 2   sp             s + p            linear                 180°          BeCl₂, CO₂
 3   sp²            s + 2p           trigonal planar        120°          BF₃, BCl₃   <-- answer (b)
 4   sp³            s + 3p           tetrahedral            109°28′       CH₄, SiH₄
 5   sp³d           s + 3p + d       trigonal bipyramidal   120° and 90°  PCl₅
 6   sp³d²          s + 3p + 2d      octahedral             90°           SF₆
 % s-character:  sp = 50 > sp² = 33.3 > sp³ = 25   ⇒  angle 180 > 120 > 109°28′
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the ladder as a single chain — 2→180, 3→120, 4→109½, 5→120/90, 6→90 — and you can go either direction (angle → hybridisation, or hybridisation → angle) in one second. The physical driver is % s-character = 1/SN: more s-character ⇒ the orbital is fatter near the nucleus ⇒ larger angle. Trap: option (d) sp³d also contains a 120° angle in its equatorial plane, so a careless reader picks it; but sp³d is not a single-angle geometry, and the pure 120° set is sp².
Answer: (b) sp².
2The bond angle in H₂O is smaller than that in NH₃ mainly because: (a) O is more electronegative than N (b) H₂O has two lone pairs while NH₃ has one (c) H–O bonds are longer (d) NH₃ is planarLone-pair count vs bond angleWhy the bond angles of H₂O and NH₃ are 104.5° and 107° — lone-pair compression🟢 ExactbH₂O has two lone pairs while NH₃ has one
Concept A lone pair is held by one nucleus only, so its electron cloud is fat and diffuse and sits close to the central atom; a bond pair is pulled by two nuclei and is therefore squeezed into a thinner cylinder between them. The fat lone pair consequently occupies more angular space and pushes the bond pairs together. The repulsion hierarchy is lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp, so each extra lone pair takes another bite out of the bond angle. Both O in H₂O and N in NH₃ are sp³ (SN = 4), so hybridisation cannot be the discriminator — only the lone-pair count can.
Key relation SN = σ bonds + lone pairs; for NH₃, SN = 3 + 1 = 4 ⇒ sp³; for H₂O, SN = 2 + 2 = 4 ⇒ sp³. Identical hybridisation, identical ideal angle 109°28′ — so the whole difference must come from the number of lone pairs. Observed compression: 109°28′ → 107° (one lp) → 104.5° (two lp), roughly 2.5° per lone pair.
Solution
1. Compute the steric number of NH₃: N has 5 valence electrons, 3 go into N–H bonds, leaving 1 lone pair. SN = 3 + 1 = 4 ⇒ sp³, ideal 109°28′.
2. Compute the steric number of H₂O: O has 6 valence electrons, 2 go into O–H bonds, leaving 2 lone pairs. SN = 2 + 2 = 4 ⇒ sp³, ideal 109°28′.
3. Apply the repulsion order lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp: NH₃ suffers one lp–bp compression set, H₂O suffers two plus an lp–lp term.More lone pairs ⇒ more angular space stolen from the bonding region ⇒ smaller H–X–H angle.
4. Read the numbers: NH₃ = 107°, H₂O = 104.5°. The extra lone pair costs about 2.5°, exactly as option (b) says.
5. Eliminate the distractors:
  • (a) O is more electronegative than N, but higher electronegativity of the central atom pulls bond pairs closer to itself and would increase the angle — it works against the observed trend, so it cannot be the main reason.
  • (c) H–O (96 pm) is in fact shorter than N–H (101 pm), so the statement is factually wrong.
  • (d) NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal, not planar — if it were planar its angle would be 120°, larger still.

 NH₃       N           3          1          4        sp³     109°28′    107°
 H₂O       O           2          2          4        sp³     109°28′    104.5°   <-- answer (b)

⚡ JEE Trick For any sp³ centre just count the lone pairs and subtract ~2.5° each from 109°28′: 0 lp → 109.5°, 1 lp → 107°, 2 lp → 104.5°. That one line reproduces CH₄ / NH₃ / H₂O and also NH₄⁺ (109.5°) and NH₂⁻ (~104°). Trap: option (a) is a true statement about electronegativity but the wrong explanation — an assertion-style trap. Whenever two molecules share the same hybridisation, the discriminator is the lone-pair count, never the hybridisation.
Answer: (b) H₂O has two lone pairs while NH₃ has one.
3For the series NF₃, NCl₃, NBr₃, the bond angle increases in the order: (a) NBr₃ < NCl₃ < NF₃ (b) NF₃ < NBr₃ < NCl₃ (c) NF₃ < NCl₃ < NBr₃ (d) NCl₃ < NF₃ < NBr₃EN of surrounding atomsBond angle order in NCl₃, NH₃ and NF₃ explained🟡 SimilarcNF₃ < NCl₃ < NBr₃
Concept Here the central atom is fixed (always N, always sp³, always one lone pair), so hybridisation and lone-pair count are constant and cannot explain any difference. The only variable is the electronegativity of the surrounding atom. A highly electronegative substituent drags the shared bond pair away from nitrogen and towards itself; the bond pair then sits far from the central atom, its cloud no longer crowds its neighbours, bp–bp repulsion near N falls, and the lone pair — now relatively unopposed — squeezes the bonds inward. Hence higher electronegativity of the surrounding atom ⇒ smaller bond angle.
Key relation Central atom constant ⇒ bond angle ∝ 1 / (electronegativity of surrounding atom B). Pauling electronegativities: F 4.0 > Cl 3.0 > Br 2.8 > I 2.5. So going F → Cl → Br the pull weakens, the bond pairs move back towards N, bp–bp repulsion grows and the angle opens out.
Solution
1. Check that the central atom really is invariant: in NF₃, NCl₃ and NBr₃ the centre is N with 5 valence electrons, 3 σ bonds and 1 lone pair ⇒ SN = 3 + 1 = 4 ⇒ sp³, trigonal pyramidal in every case.Same SN, same hybridisation, same lone-pair count — so the electronegativity of X is the only thing left that can vary.
2. Order the halogens by electronegativity: F (4.0) > Cl (3.0) > Br (2.8).
3. Apply the inverse rule: the most electronegative substituent gives the smallest angle, so NF₃ is smallest and NBr₃ largest.
4. Write the increasing order: NF₃ < NCl₃ < NBr₃ — option (c).Experimental values back this up: NF₃ = 102°, NCl₃ ≈ 107°, NBr₃ ≈ 108°.
5. Kill the rest: (a) is the exact reverse; (b) and (d) both scramble the electronegativity sequence and would require Cl to be less electronegative than Br, which is false.

 NCl₃      N        3 + 1 = 4     sp³      3.0      ≈107°

⚡ JEE Trick Fix which atom is changing first — that decides which rule to use. Surrounding atom changing → angle ∝ 1/EN(B). Central atom changing → angle ∝ EN(A). The two rules point in opposite directions, and swapping them is the single most common mistake in this chapter. Trap: the same reasoning gives NH₃ (107°) > NF₃ (102°) even though H is far less electronegative than F — consistent, because low EN(B) means a large angle.
Answer: (c) NF₃ < NCl₃ < NBr₃.
4Which statement correctly explains why SO₂ has a larger bond angle than H₂O? (a) SO₂ has more lone pairs than H₂O (b) Double bonds in SO₂ create greater repulsion than single bonds (c) Oxygen is more electronegative in SO₂ (d) H₂O is sp² while SO₂ is sp³π-bond (double-bond) repulsionSO₂ molecular geometry, shape and bond angle — bent, sp², ≈119°🟡 SimilarbDouble bonds in SO₂ create greater repulsion than single bonds
Concept A double bond carries four electrons in the internuclear region instead of two, so its charge cloud is fatter and it repels its neighbours harder than a single bond does. The repulsion ladder therefore extends to lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp and, within bond pairs, double–double > double–single > single–single. SO₂ has two S=O double bonds and only one lone pair; H₂O has two single bonds and two lone pairs. Both effects push the same way, but the option set is asking specifically about the π contribution.
Key relation For SO₂: SN = σ bonds + lone pairs = 2 + 1 = 3 ⇒ sp², bent, ideal 120°, observed ≈119° π bonds are not counted in the steric number — only σ bonds and lone pairs set the hybridisation; the π electrons then act as extra repulsion on top. For H₂O: SN = 2 + 2 = 4 ⇒ sp³, bent, ideal 109°28′, observed 104.5°.
Solution
1. Build SO₂: S has 6 valence electrons; two go to each O as σ, two form π density, leaving one lone pair on S. σ = 2, lp = 1 ⇒ SN = 3 ⇒ sp², so the ideal angle is already 120°.
2. Build H₂O: O has 6 valence electrons; two σ bonds and two lone pairs ⇒ SN = 4 ⇒ sp³, ideal angle only 109°28′.
3. Compare: the fat double-bond clouds in SO₂ repel each other strongly and hold the O–S–O angle wide open at ≈119°, whereas in H₂O two lone pairs squeeze the angle down to 104.5°.The double bond's extra π electron density is exactly the "greater repulsion than single bonds" of option (b).
4. Eliminate the distractors:
  • (a) False — SO₂ has one lone pair, H₂O has two; SO₂ has fewer.
  • (c) Meaningless as a comparison — oxygen has the same electronegativity in both, and in SO₂ it is the surrounding atom while in H₂O it is the central atom.
  • (d) Backwards — H₂O is sp³ and SO₂ is sp²; the option has the two labels swapped.
 species  σ bonds  lone pairs  SN  hybrid  π bonds  ideal      observed
 H₂O         2         2       4    sp³       0     109°28′    104.5°
 SO₂         2         1       3    sp²       2     120°       ≈119°   <-- answer (b)
 note: π electrons are EXCLUDED from SN but ADDED to the repulsion

⚡ JEE Trick Two rules, applied in order: (i) π bonds never enter the steric number — count σ + lp only, and you get the hybridisation right every time; (ii) π bonds do enter the repulsion — a double bond widens the angle it sits next to. That pair explains COF₂ (F–C–F = 108°, O=C–F > 120°), H₂SO₄ (O=S=O > O–S–O) and this question in one stroke. Trap: option (d) reverses the hybridisations, and it "sounds" right because sp² does give a bigger angle — read which molecule is which.
Answer: (b) Double bonds in SO₂ create greater repulsion than single bonds.
5Consider PH₃, AsH₃, and NH₃. Which statement is correct? Concept Now the surrounding atom is fixed (always H) and the central atom changes down group 15. A less electronegative central atom holds the shared pairs less tightly, so the bond pairs drift outwards towards hydrogen; near the central atom the bonding clouds are then further apart, bp–bp repulsion collapses, and the lone pair pushes the bonds together. So lower electronegativity of the central atom ⇒ smaller bond angle. Equivalently (Drago's rule): from P onwards the central atom barely hybridises and uses nearly pure p orbitals, whose mutual angle is 90°.
Key relation Surrounding atom constant ⇒ bond angle ∝ electronegativity of the central atom A. Electronegativity down group 15: N 3.0 > P 2.1 > As 2.0 > Sb 1.9. Solution
1. Check the steric number for each: A has 5 valence electrons, 3 σ bonds to H, 1 lone pair ⇒ SN = 3 + 1 = 4 in every member.Nominally sp³ for all four — so lone-pair count cannot be the discriminator, and option (d) dies immediately.
2. List the observed angles: NH₃ = 107°, PH₃ = 93.8°, AsH₃ = 91.8°, SbH₃ = 91.3°. They clearly decrease, which kills (a) "increases down the group" and (c) "all the same".
3. Line the angles up against central-atom electronegativity: 3.0 → 2.1 → 2.0 → 1.9, falling in step with the angles. That is exactly the wording of option (b).
4. 1Depending on the number of lone pairs and bonded pairs of electrons represent the following molecules: AB₂E, AB₃E, AB₂E₂, AB₄E, AB₃E₂, AB₅E, AB₄E₂, where A is the central atom, B a bonded atom and E a lone pair of electrons. Examples of the above type of molecules are given in the table.VSEPR ABxEy notationMemorise the whole VSEPR chart — every ABxEy shape and its bond angle🟢 Exact—
Concept VSEPR notation compresses a molecule's whole geometry into one symbol: A is the central atom, B a bonded (surrounding) atom and E a lone pair. The subscripts on B and E add up to the steric number, which fixes the electron-pair geometry; deleting the invisible lone pairs from that skeleton then gives the molecular shape. Two molecules with the same SN but different numbers of E therefore share an electron geometry but look completely different — that is the entire content of this question.
Key relation SN = x + y for ABxEy, then SN = 2 → sp (linear) · 3 → sp² (trigonal planar) · 4 → sp³ (tetrahedral) · 5 → sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal) · 6 → sp³d² (octahedral). In a trigonal bipyramid lone pairs always take equatorial positions (only two 90° neighbours instead of three); in an octahedron two lone pairs go trans to each other (180° apart). These two placement rules generate see-saw, T-shape, linear and square planar.
Solution
1. AB₂E — SN = 2 + 1 = 3 ⇒ sp², trigonal planar electron geometry; remove one lone pair ⇒ bent / V-shaped, angle slightly under 120° (≈119°). Examples: SO₂, O₃, NO₂⁻, SnCl₂.
2. AB₃E — SN = 3 + 1 = 4 ⇒ sp³, tetrahedral electron geometry; remove one lone pair ⇒ trigonal pyramidal, ≈107°. Examples: NH₃, PCl₃, H₃O⁺.
3. AB₂E₂ — SN = 2 + 2 = 4 ⇒ sp³, tetrahedral electron geometry; remove two lone pairs ⇒ bent / angular, ≈104.5°. Examples: H₂O, OF₂, SCl₂, NH₂⁻.
4. AB₄E — SN = 4 + 1 = 5 ⇒ sp³d, trigonal bipyramidal; the lone pair takes an equatorial site ⇒ see-saw (distorted tetrahedron), ≈102° equatorial and ≈87° axial. Examples: SF₄, TeCl₄, IOF₃.
5. AB₃E₂ — SN = 3 + 2 = 5 ⇒ sp³d; both lone pairs equatorial ⇒ T-shaped, ≈87.5°. Examples: ClF₃, BrF₃.
6. AB₅E — SN = 5 + 1 = 6 ⇒ sp³d², octahedral electron geometry; one lone pair removed ⇒ square pyramidal, ≈85–90°. Examples: BrF₅, IF₅, XeOF₄.
7. AB₄E₂ — SN = 4 + 2 = 6 ⇒ sp³d²; the two lone pairs go trans (axial) ⇒ square planar, exactly 90°. Examples: XeF₄, ICl₄⁻.Placing the two lone pairs 180° apart minimises the strongest repulsion (lp–lp), which is why square planar and not see-saw-like alternatives is observed.

2Give the type of overlap by which pi (π) bond is formed.π-bond overlapπ bonds — how sideways (lateral) overlap of p orbitals forms them🟢 Exact—
Concept A covalent bond is the region of increased electron density produced when two half-filled atomic orbitals overlap. How they overlap decides the bond's name and every one of its properties. If the two orbitals approach head-on along the internuclear axis, the density piles up directly between the nuclei and the bond is a σ bond. If instead they approach side-by-side, with their axes parallel and perpendicular to the internuclear line, the density collects in two lobes above and below that line and the bond is a π bond. A π bond can only form after a σ bond has already locked the two atoms in place.
Definition π bond = sideways / lateral / parallel overlap of two unhybridised p orbitals (p–p), with the orbital axes perpendicular to the internuclear axis The overlap is small because the lobes meet at their flanks rather than nose-to-nose, so a π bond is weaker than a σ bond; and because the density lies off the axis, rotating one atom would break the parallel alignment — which is why C=C shows restricted rotation and gives cis–trans isomerism.
Solution
1. Name the overlap: a π bond is formed by sideways (lateral, parallel) overlap of two half-filled p orbitals — for example py–py or pz–pz.
2. State the orientation: the axes of the two p orbitals lie parallel to each other and perpendicular to the internuclear axis.Contrast with a σ bond, whose overlap (s–s, s–p or p–p end-on) happens along the internuclear axis.
3. Describe the resulting electron cloud: two lobes, one above and one below the plane containing the nuclei, with a nodal plane passing through the internuclear axis itself.
4. Note the ordering rule: a π bond never forms alone. The first bond between two atoms is always σ; the second and third are π. So a double bond = 1σ + 1π and a triple bond = 1σ + 2π.In N≡N the σ comes from px–px end-on overlap and the two π bonds from py–py and pz–pz sideways overlap.
5. List the consequences worth quoting: π bonds are weaker, more exposed and hence more reactive; they restrict rotation; and they are the electrons that delocalise in resonance and conjugation.

⚡ JEE Trick Answer this in three words the examiner is looking for — "sideways (lateral) overlap of p orbitals" — then earn the remaining marks with the nodal-plane picture and "1σ + 1π = double bond". The σ/π count itself is a standing JEE question: for any structure, every bond contributes exactly one σ, and every extra line contributes one π. Trap: writing "p–p overlap" alone is not enough, because p–p overlap can be end-on and give a σ bond (as in F₂) — you must say sideways.
Key point: a π bond is formed by sideways (lateral / parallel) overlap of two unhybridised p orbitals whose axes are perpendicular to the internuclear axis; the density lies above and below the internuclear axis with a nodal plane through it, making the π bond weaker than a σ bond and preventing free rotation.

1Bond angle is defined as: (a) Angle between lone pairs (b) Angle between two adjacent atoms (c) Angle between two adjacent bonds at an atom (d) None of theseDefinition of bond angleDetermining molecular geometry and bond angle using VSEPR🟡 SimilarcAngle between two adjacent bonds at an atom
Concept Bond angle is one of the three numbers that pin down a molecule's shape (the others being bond length and bond order). The definition has to be phrased carefully, because an angle needs a vertex and two arms. The vertex is the atom; the arms are the two bonds radiating from it — not the atoms at their far ends, and not the lone pairs. That is why the concept only exists for molecules of three or more atoms: a diatomic like HCl has just one bond and hence no angle at all.
Definition Bond angle = the angle between two adjacent bonds at an atom in a molecule made up of three or more atoms It is measured in degrees (and minutes), it is a property of the central atom's orbital arrangement, and it is written B–A–B with the central atom in the middle: H–O–H = 104.5°, H–N–H = 107°, Cl–Be–Cl = 180°.
Solution
1. Identify what an angle needs: a vertex plus two rays. Here the vertex is the central atom and the rays are the two bonds leaving it.
2. Match that to the options — "angle between two adjacent bonds at an atom" is a word-for-word statement of it, so (c) is correct.
3. Reject (a) "angle between lone pairs": lone pairs certainly influence the bond angle, but the angle we measure and tabulate is between bonds; lone pairs are invisible to diffraction.
4. Concept I₃⁻ is the standard AB₂E₃ case, and the whole point is that three lone pairs do not bend it — they straighten it. With a steric number of 5 the electron geometry is trigonal bipyramidal, and lone pairs always take the roomier equatorial positions. Three lone pairs fill all three equatorial sites, leaving the two iodine ligands stranded on the axial positions, which are 180° apart. So despite having more lone pairs than almost any species in the chapter, I₃⁻ has the maximum possible bond angle. Once you have 180°, options (a), (b) and (d) all follow.

Concept This is the summary question for the whole lecture: the two electronegativity rules point in opposite directions, and the paper offers each of them in both its true and its false form. The physical picture behind them is the same in both cases — where does the bonding electron density sit? A more electronegative central atom keeps the bond pairs close to itself, so they crowd each other and push apart (angle up). A more electronegative surrounding atom drags the bond pairs away from the centre, thinning the density there so the lone pair can close the angle (angle down). Statement (b) then tests whether you know that atomic size and electronegativity are inversely related.
Key relation angle ∝ EN(A) and angle ∝ 1/EN(B), with the auxiliary fact size(A) ↓ ⇒ EN(A) ↑ ⇒ angle ↑. Both rules require a lone pair on A to be visible at all; with lp = 0 the geometry is regular and neither rule bites, which is why CH₄ = CF₄ = 109°28′.
Solution
1. (a) EN(A) decreases ⇒ angle decreases. This is angle ∝ EN(A) restated. Verify on group 15: NCl₃ (N, 3.0) 107° > PCl₃ (P, 2.1) 100.3° > AsCl₃ (As, 2.0) 98.4° > SbCl₃ (Sb, 1.9) 97.2°. Falling EN(A), falling angle ⇒ TRUE ✓
2. (b) size(A) decreases ⇒ angle decreases. Size and electronegativity run opposite to each other: a smaller central atom is more electronegative, so by rule (a) the angle should increase. Check: nitrogen is the smallest group-15 centre and NCl₃ has the largest angle ⇒ the statement is backwards ⇒ FALSE ✗Equivalently by Drago: smaller centre ⇒ ns and np closer in energy ⇒ better s–p mixing ⇒ more s-character ⇒ larger angle.
3. (c) EN(B) decreases ⇒ angle increases. This is angle ∝ 1/EN(B) restated. Verify on the phosphorus trihalides: PF₃ (F 4.0) 97.8° < PCl₃ (Cl 3.0) 100.3° < PBr₃ (Br 2.8) 101° < PI₃ (I 2.5) 102°. Falling EN(B), rising angle ⇒ TRUE ✓
4. (d) EN(B) decreases ⇒ angle decreases. Direct contradiction of (c) ⇒ FALSE ✗
