1An artificial satellite moving in a circular orbit around the Earth has a total energy E0. What is its potential energy. — (Level-1)PE from total energy of satelliteAn artificial satellite moving in a circular orbit around the earth has a total energy E0. Its pote🟢 ExactU = 2E0
Concept For a circular orbit gravity alone supplies the centripetal force, which ties KE, PE and total energy together in fixed ratios: KE = −E, PE = 2E. Since a bound orbit has E0 < 0, the potential energy 2E0 is more negative than E0. The trap is to write U = E0/2 or to drop the sign and answer −2E0.
Formula K = GMm/(2r), U = −GMm/r, E = K + U = −GMm/(2r) K follows from mv²/r = GMm/r²; U is the standard point-mass potential energy with zero at infinity.
Solution
1. Centripetal condition: mv²/r = GMm/r² ⇒ mv² = GMm/r ⇒ K = (1/2)mv² = GMm/(2r).Only gravity acts, so it alone bends the path.
2. Potential energy of the satellite–Earth pair: U = −GMm/r.
3. Total energy: E0 = K + U = GMm/(2r) − GMm/r = −GMm/(2r).
4. Compare U with E0: U/E0 = (−GMm/r)/(−GMm/(2r)) = 2.The r, G, M and m all cancel, so the ratio holds for every circular orbit.
5. Hence U = 2E0; with E0 negative, U is twice as negative.
6. Consistency check: K = −E0 = |E0|, and K + U = −E0 + 2E0 = E0. ✓

 U  = -GMm/r
 E0 =  K + U = -GMm/(2r)
 U/E0 = (-GMm/r)/(-GMm/(2r)) = 2

 -----------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the circular-orbit ladder K : U : E = 1 : −2 : −1 (it is the virial theorem for a 1/r potential). Any one of the three fixes the other two instantly. Trap: answering −2E0 because "PE is negative"; E0 already carries the minus sign, so 2E0 is the negative quantity.
Answer: Potential energy U = 2E0 (twice the total energy; negative, since E0 < 0).
2The radius of a planet is R. A satellite revolves round it in a circle of radius r. The time period of revolution is T. Determine the acceleration due to gravitation of the planet at its surface. — (Level-2)surface g from satellite periodThe radius of a planet is R1 and a satellite revolves round it in a circle of radius R2 . The time🟢 Exactg = (4π²/T²)(r³/R²)
Concept The satellite's period measures the planet's GM through Kepler's third law; GM then gives surface gravity through g = GM/R². Two different radii appear: the orbit radius r (sets the period) and the planet radius R (sets surface g). The trap is to mix them up and write 4π²R/T² or 4π²r/T².
Formula GMm/r² = m(2π/T)²r ⇒ GM = 4π²r³/T² and g = GM/R² Gravity is the centripetal force in orbit; at the surface the same GM acts at distance R.
Solution
1. In orbit gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mω²r with ω = 2π/T.
2. Cancel m and rearrange: GM = ω²r³ = (2π/T)²·r³ = 4π²r³/T².The satellite's own mass drops out, so any satellite gives the same GM.
3. At the surface a body of mass m′ feels m′g = GMm′/R² ⇒ g = GM/R².
4. Substitute GM: g = GM/R² = (4π²r³/T²)/R² = 4π²r³/(T²R²).
5. Dimension check: r³/(T²R²) has m³/(s²·m²) = m/s². ✓
6. Special case r = R (grazing orbit): g = 4π²R/T², the familiar near-surface result, so the general formula reduces correctly; for r > R the factor (r/R)² makes g larger than the satellite's own centripetal acceleration 4π²r/T², as it must be.A good way to catch a misplaced power of r or R.
 orbit  : GMm/r^2 = m(2pi/T)^2 r
          GM = 4pi^2 r^3 / T^2
 surface: g = GM/R^2
 g = (4pi^2 r^3/T^2)/R^2 = 4pi^2 r^3/(T^2 R^2)
 check r = R : g = 4pi^2 R/T^2   (near-surface)
 -----------------------------------------
 g = (4pi^2/T^2)(r^3/R^2)        <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an orbit is given and a surface quantity is asked, go through GM: "period → GM → anything". Here g = GM/R² with GM = 4π²r³/T². Trap: using r in place of R in g = GM/R² gives 4π²r/T², which is the satellite's centripetal acceleration at r, not the surface gravity. Second trap: writing g = 4π²r³/T² alone, which is GM, not g; it has the wrong units (m³/s²).
Answer: g = (4π²/T²)(r³/R²).
3Two satellites have their masses in the ratio of 3 : 1. The radii of their circular orbits are in the ratio of 1 : 4. Concept The total mechanical energy of a satellite in a circular orbit is E = −GMm/(2r): it grows with the satellite's mass and shrinks in magnitude as the orbit widens. A heavier satellite on a tighter orbit is therefore much more tightly bound. Both energies are negative, so their ratio is a positive number. The trap is to square the radius ratio (as for g) or to invert it.
Formula E = −GMm/(2r) ⇒ EA/EB = (mA/mB)·(rB/rA) Same planet, so GM cancels; E ∝ m and E ∝ 1/r.
Solution
1. For a circular orbit K = GMm/(2r), U = −GMm/r, so E = K + U = −GMm/(2r).
2. Given mA : mB = 3 : 1 and rA : rB = 1 : 4.
3. Take the ratio: EA/EB = [−GMmA/(2rA)]/[−GMmB/(2rB)] = (mA/mB)(rB/rA).G, M, the factor 2 and both minus signs cancel.
4. Substitute: EA/EB = (3/1)·(4/1) = 12.
5. So EA : EB = 12 : 1; A's energy is 12 times as negative, i.e. A is 12 times more tightly bound.
6. Sanity check by binding energy: BE = GMm/(2r) obeys the same ratio, 3 × 4 = 12, so the answer does not depend on whether one speaks of E or of BE.

The radius r is (a) GMMeRe/(GMMe − 4kRet) (b) GmMeRe/(GmMe + 2kRet) (c) GmMeRe/(GmMe + 3kRet) (d) GmMeRe/(GmMe − 2kRet) — (Level-6)orbit radius from energy loss timeA satellite of mass `m` is orbiting the earth in a circular orbit of radius `r`. It starts losing🟢 ExactdGmMeRe/(GmMe − 2kRet)
Concept With a slow, steady energy loss the satellite spirals through nearly circular orbits, each with total energy E = −GmMe/(2r). Energy lost in time t is k·t, and it must equal the drop in E from radius r down to the earth's surface Re. Solving that one equation for r gives the answer. The trap is the sign in the denominator: the orbit started ABOVE the surface, so r must come out larger than Re.
Formula k·t = E(r) − E(Re) = GmMe/(2Re) − GmMe/(2r) −dE/dt = k is constant; E(r) = −GmMe/(2r) for each quasi-circular orbit.
Solution
1. Initial energy: Ei = −GmMe/(2r).
2. At the surface: Ef = −GmMe/(2Re), more negative.
3. Energy balance: Ei − Ef = kt ⇒ GmMe/(2Re) − GmMe/(2r) = kt.
4. Multiply by 2/(GmMe): 1/Re − 1/r = 2kt/(GmMe) ⇒ 1/r = 1/Re − 2kt/(GmMe).
5. Common denominator: 1/r = (GmMe − 2kRet)/(GmMeRe) ⇒ r = GmMeRe/(GmMe − 2kRet).
6. Check: t → 0 gives r → Re; larger t gives a smaller denominator, so r > Re. Matches (d).(b) and (c) give r < Re, impossible; (a) has the wrong factor 4 and a capital M.
 E(r) = -GmMe/(2r)
 kt = GmMe/(2Re) - GmMe/(2r)
 1/r = 1/Re - 2kt/(GmMe)
     = (GmMe - 2kRe t)/(GmMe Re)
 -----------------------------------------
 r = GmMe Re/(GmMe - 2kRe t)     <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Use limits to kill options: at t = 0 the satellite is already on the surface, so r = Re; and r must exceed Re for t > 0, so the denominator must SHRINK, i.e. a minus sign. That alone leaves (a) and (d); the factor 2 comes from the 1/2 in E = −GMm/(2r), picking (d).
Answer: (d) GmMeRe/(GmMe − 2kRet).

Concept The planet's mass is not given, so it must be extracted from the elliptical orbit using the known apogee speed v0. Two conservation laws (angular momentum and energy) between perigee R and apogee 2R fix GM in terms of v0. Then the circular speed at 2R is √(GM/2R). The trap is to assume v0 is the circular speed already; at apogee the craft moves SLOWER than circular speed, which is why it falls back inward.
Formula vp·R = v0·2R, (1/2)vp² − GM/R = (1/2)v0² − GM/(2R), vc = √(GM/2R) At apsides velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so L = m·v·r there.

1. Angular momentum at perigee and apogee: vp·R = v0·2R ⇒ vp = 2v0.
2. Energy per unit mass: (1/2)(2v0)² − GM/R = (1/2)v0² − GM/(2R).
3. Rearrange: 2v0² − (1/2)v0² = GM/R − GM/(2R) ⇒ (3/2)v0² = GM/(2R) ⇒ GM = 3Rv0².
4. Cross-check with vis-viva, a = 3R/2: v0² = GM(2/2R − 1/a) = GM(1/R − 2/(3R)) = GM/(3R). Same result.Vis-viva v² = GM(2/r − 1/a) is the one-line version of steps 1 to 3.
5. Circular orbit of radius 2R: vc² = GM/(2R) = 3Rv0²/(2R) = (3/2)v0².
6. New speed vc = √(3/2)·v0 ≈ 1.22v0: he must speed UP, firing the thrusters backward so the thrust acts forward.

Answer: (a) √(3/2) v0.

Concept In a freely orbiting frame gravity is cancelled exactly only at the ship's own radius. The astronaut, 200 m further out (or in) but forced to share the ship's angular speed ω, feels a small residual "tidal" pull away from the ship. The cable must supply only this difference, which is tiny, so even a 10 N cable is ample. The trap is to think the cable must hold his full weight mg' ≈ 245 N.
Formula T = m[ω²(r + l) − GM/(r + l)²] ≈ 3mω²l = 3m·(gR²/r³)·l Ship in free orbit: ω² = GM/r³; expand to first order in l/r ≪ 1.
Solution
1. (a) Guess: the astronaut and ship are both in free fall; only the 200 m offset (l/r ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻⁵) produces any force, so yes, a 10 N cable should easily keep him from drifting.
2. Orbit radius r = R + R = 2R = 12800 km = 1.28 × 10⁷ m.
3. ω² = GM/r³ = gR²/r³ = g/(8R) = 9.8/(8 × 6.4 × 10⁶) = 1.914 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻².
4. Astronaut outside at r + l: required centripetal force mω²(r + l), gravity supplies GMm/(r + l)² ≈ mω²r(1 − 2l/r). Tension = mω²(r + l) − mω²(r − 2l) = 3mω²l.On the inner side the sign flips but the magnitude is the same, 3mω²l.
5. (b) T = 3mω²l = 3 × 100 × 1.914 × 10⁻⁷ × 200 = 1.15 × 10⁻² N ≈ 0.01 N.
6. Compare: 0.0115 N vs 10 N capacity, about 870 times smaller, confirming the guess in (a). The key's 0.01 N is this value rounded to one figure.

[The orbit of mass orbiter mission, informally called Mangalyaan, was raised in five steps using this principle; before it was given the escape speed] (b) Find the impulse J that the rocket must impart to the satellite at perigee so that its maximum distance from earth's centre, during its course of motion in elliptical path, becomes 12r. For a small forward impulse ΔKE ≈ vJ, which is largest where the satellite is fastest: at perigee (the Oberth effect). For (b), the new perigee speed must be the one that makes the ellipse r → 12r, found from angular momentum plus energy conservation; J is m times the speed jump.

4. After: rp = r, ra = 12r ⇒ vp'² = 2GM·12r/[r·13r] = (24/13)GM/r ⇒ vp' = 1.3587√(GM/r).Perigee stays at r because the impulse is tangential there.
