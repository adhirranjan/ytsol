1 (i)Fill in the blank: Ostwald's dilution law states that the degree of ionization of a weak electrolyte is ________ proportional to the square root of its dilution.Ostwald dilution law statementOstwald Dilution law for Weak electrolytes- IIT JEE & NEET | Vineet Khatri | ATP STAR🟡 Similardirectly
Concept Ostwald’s dilution law applies the law of mass action to the ionisation of a weak electrolyte. Dilution V is the volume holding one mole, so V = 1/C. As the solution is diluted, α rises, and it rises as the square root of V, not linearly. The trap is to write “inversely” by thinking of concentration instead of dilution.
Key relation K = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 ⇒ α = √(K/C) = √(KV) Valid when α ≪ 1, i.e. for a weak electrolyte that is only slightly ionised.
Solution
1. For HA ⇌ H+ + A− starting at C mol/L, equilibrium holds C(1 − α), Cα and Cα.
2. Mass action: K = (Cα)(Cα)/C(1 − α) = Cα2/(1 − α).
3. For a weak electrolyte α ≪ 1, so 1 − α ≈ 1 and K ≈ Cα2. This is the step that confines the law to weak electrolytes.
4. Rearranging: α = √(K/C), and with C = 1/V this becomes α = √(KV).
5. K is constant at a fixed temperature, so α ∝ √V: the degree of ionisation is directly proportional to the square root of dilution.
6. Check: diluting 4-fold (V → 4V) gives α → √4 × α = 2α; α doubles, it does not quadruple.
7. Equivalently α ∝ 1/√C, which is the same statement written in terms of concentration.
 HA  <=>  H+  +  A-
 C(1-a)    Ca     Ca

 K = Ca^2/(1-a)  ~  Ca^2        (a << 1)
 a = sqrt(K/C) = sqrt(K V)      (V = 1/C)

 V x 4  ->  a x 2
 -----------------------------------------
 a  directly prop. to  sqrt(V)  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Read the variable carefully: α ∝ √V (dilution) but α ∝ 1/√C (concentration). Examiners swap the two words to catch students who memorised only one form. The blank here sits next to “dilution”, so the word is directly.
Answer: directly (α = √(KV)).
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: For a weak monobasic acid HA, the degree of ionization (α) is given by the square root of (Ka/C), where Ka is the ______ constant and C is the ________.Degree of ionization of acidIf α is the degree of ionization, C is the concentration of a weak electrolyte and Ka is t....🟡 Similarionization (dissociation); molar concentration
Concept In α = √(Ka/C) the two symbols are fixed by the equilibrium HA ⇌ H+ + A−. Ka is the acid’s ionisation (dissociation) constant, a property of the acid that changes only with temperature. C is the molar concentration of the acid taken, in mol/L.
Key relation Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA] = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 C enters through the equilibrium concentrations Cα and C(1 − α).
Solution
1. Write the ionisation of a weak monobasic acid: HA ⇌ H+ + A−.
2. The equilibrium constant of this ionisation is Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA], the ionisation (dissociation) constant of the acid.
3. If C mol/L of HA is dissolved, the equilibrium amounts are [HA] = C(1 − α), [H+] = [A−] = Cα. So C is the molar concentration of the acid.
4. Substituting: Ka = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 for α ≪ 1.
5. Therefore α = √(Ka/C). Larger Ka (stronger acid) or smaller C (more dilute) both raise α.
6. Example: Ka = 1.8 × 10−5, C = 0.1 M gives α = √(1.8 × 10−5/0.1) = √(1.8 × 10−4) = 0.0134, well below 1, so the approximation holds.

 C(1-a)        Ca       Ca

 Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA] = Ca^2/(1-a) ~ Ca^2
 a  = sqrt(Ka / C)

 Ka : ionisation constant of the acid
 C  : molar concentration (mol/L)
 -----------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick Ka changes only with temperature, never with C. So when a question dilutes a weak acid, keep Ka fixed and recompute α from the new C. Do not confuse C (initial molarity) with the equilibrium [HA]: in this formula C is the amount taken.
Answer: Ka is the ionisation (dissociation) constant of the acid; C is the molar concentration of the acid.

Concept A weak base BOH ionises as BOH ⇌ B+ + OH−, exactly mirroring HA. Its equilibrium constant Kb is the base’s ionisation (dissociation) constant, and the same algebra gives α = √(Kb/C). The trap is to call Kb the “hydrolysis” constant, which belongs to salts.
Key relation Kb = [B+][OH−]/[BOH] = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 Same mass-action form as Ka, with OH− in place of H+.
Solution
1. Ionisation of the weak base: BOH ⇌ B+ + OH−, e.g. NH4OH ⇌ NH4+ + OH−.
2. With C mol/L taken: [BOH] = C(1 − α), [B+] = [OH−] = Cα.
3. The equilibrium constant Kb = [B+][OH−]/[BOH] is the base ionisation (dissociation) constant.
4. Kb = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 when α ≪ 1, so α = √(Kb/C).
5. Then [OH−] = Cα = √(KbC). Example: Kb = 1.8 × 10−5, C = 0.1 M gives [OH−] = √(1.8 × 10−6) = 1.34 × 10−3 M.
6. For a conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C, which links the two constants.

Answer: Kb is the ionisation (dissociation) constant of the base.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: A key limitation of Ostwald's dilution law is that it assumes that the concentrations of the ions and the ________ do not change significantly during the reaction.Limitations of dilution lawOstwald's Dilution Law: Derivation, Verification & Limitations (Weak vs Strong ) | Mootechie🟡 Similarundissociated (un-ionised) molecules
Concept The law treats a solution of HA as holding just two kinds of species: the ions (H+, A−) and the undissociated molecules (HA). It uses their plain molar concentrations in K, assuming ideal behaviour so that concentration stands in for activity. When ion concentrations grow, interionic attraction makes that assumption fail.
Key relation K = [H+][A−]/[HA] = Cα2/(1 − α) Only the ions and the undissociated HA appear, each as a simple concentration.
Solution
1. The law starts from the mass-action expression K = [H+][A−]/[HA].
2. The numerator holds the ions; the denominator holds the undissociated (un-ionised) molecules, [HA] = C(1 − α).
3. It treats these concentrations as the effective (active) concentrations, i.e. it assumes they are not altered by interactions in solution. Strictly, activities a = γc should be used.
4. For a weak electrolyte the ion concentration is tiny, interionic forces are negligible, γ ≈ 1, and the assumption is fine.
5. For a strong electrolyte or a concentrated solution, ions crowd together, γ drops well below 1, and K computed from concentrations is no longer constant: the law breaks down.
6. So the blank is filled by the second species in K: the undissociated molecules.
 K = [H+][A-] / [HA]
      ions     undissociated molecules

 assumption: conc. = activity  (gamma ~ 1)
   weak, dilute       -> holds
   strong / conc.     -> fails
 -----------------------------------------
 ions and undissociated molecules <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question asks why Ostwald’s law fails, the root answer is the same: it ignores interionic attraction and uses concentrations instead of activities. That is why it works only for weak electrolytes in dilute solution, where the ion concentration is too low for those forces to matter.
Answer: undissociated (un-ionised) molecules.
1 (v)Fill in the blank: Another limitation is that Ostwald's law does not apply to ________ and ________ electrolytes.Limitations of dilution law, Ostwald's dilution law is not applicable for strongelectrolytes because:-(1) Strong electrolyte...🟡 Similarstrong; moderately strong
Concept The derivation needs two things: a true equilibrium between ions and molecules, and α small enough that ion–ion attraction is negligible. Strong electrolytes are almost fully ionised (α ≈ 1) and moderately strong ones have large α and high ion concentration, so for both the “constant” K drifts with C.
Key relation K = Cα2/(1 − α) For α → 1 the denominator → 0 and the expression stops being a constant.
Solution
1. For a weak acid such as CH3COOH, K = Cα2/(1 − α) gives the same value (1.8 × 10−5) at every dilution, so the law holds.
2. A strong electrolyte (HCl, NaCl, NaOH) is completely ionised; there is essentially no undissociated molecule, so [HA] → 0 and K is meaningless.
3. If you force the formula on measured conductance data for KCl, the computed “K” changes many-fold with concentration instead of staying fixed.
4. A moderately strong electrolyte has a large α, so the ion concentration is high and interionic attraction lowers activities; again K is not constant. The approximation 1 − α ≈ 1 also fails.
5. Hence the law is restricted to weak electrolytes, and fails for strong and moderately strong ones.
6. These are the two blanks.

 strong (HCl)   : a ~ 1, no HA left        fails
 mod. strong    : big a, ion attraction    fails
 -----------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick If a question offers “strong electrolytes” as a case where Ostwald’s law applies, it is wrong. Strong electrolytes are handled by the Debye–Hückel–Onsager treatment (Λ = Λ° − b√C), not by Ostwald’s law.
Answer: strong and moderately strong electrolytes.
2 (i)True or false: Ostwald's dilution law is only applicable for weak electrolytes.Applicability of dilution lawOstwald Dilution law for Weak electrolytes- IIT JEE & NEET | Vineet Khatri | ATP STAR🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Ostwald’s law needs a genuine equilibrium between ions and undissociated molecules, with α small. Only weak electrolytes satisfy that: weak acids (CH3COOH, HCN), weak bases (NH4OH). Strong electrolytes are fully ionised, so the law does not describe them.
Key relation K = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2 ⇒ α = √(K/C) The approximation 1 − α ≈ 1 is only true when α ≪ 1, i.e. for a weak electrolyte.
Solution
1. Take 0.1 M acetic acid, Ka = 1.8 × 10−5: α = √(1.8 × 10−5/0.1) = √(1.8 × 10−4) = 0.0134.
2. At 0.001 M: α = √(1.8 × 10−5/0.001) = 0.134; still small, and K recalculated from either point is the same, so the law holds.
3. Now 0.1 M HCl: it is ~100% ionised, α = 1, so [HCl] undissociated ≈ 0.
4. K = Cα2/(1 − α) = 0.1 × 1/(1 − 1) is undefined: there is no equilibrium constant to speak of. The law has nothing to describe.
5. Even partially ionised strong electrolytes give a K that varies with C, because interionic attraction at high ion concentration makes activities differ from concentrations.
6. Therefore the law is applicable only to weak electrolytes: the statement is True.
 CH3COOH, Ka = 1.8e-5
   C = 0.1   : a = sqrt(1.8e-4) = 0.0134
   C = 0.001 : a = sqrt(1.8e-2) = 0.134
   K constant -> law holds

 HCl : a = 1, 1 - a = 0, K undefined
 -----------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick “Ostwald” + “strong electrolyte” in the same option is almost always the wrong option. The law is the weak-electrolyte law; the word “only” here is correct, not a trap.
Answer: True.
2 (ii)True or false: The law assumes that the degree of ionization is independent of temperature.Temperature and ionizationDegree of Ionization | Ionic Equilibrium bsc 2nd year | Factors affecting degree of Ionization | L2🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept The law makes no claim that α is independent of temperature. It applies at a fixed temperature, where K is constant. Ionisation is endothermic, so raising T increases Ka and hence α. The trap is to confuse “applied at constant T” with “independent of T”.
Key relation α = √(K(T)/C) K is a function of temperature (van’t Hoff), so α is too.
Solution
1. From K = Cα2/(1 − α), α ≈ √(K/C).
2. An equilibrium constant depends on temperature: log(K2/K1) = (ΔH°/2.303R)(1/T1 − 1/T2).
3. Dissociation of a weak electrolyte absorbs heat (bonds broken), ΔH° > 0, so K increases with T. Example: Kw rises from 10−14 at 25 °C to about 5.5 × 10−13 at 100 °C.
4. With C fixed, a larger K gives a larger α: if K doubles, α rises by √2 = 1.41 times.
5. So α does depend on temperature; the law simply has to be used with the K value for that temperature.
6. The law’s real assumptions are different: α ≪ 1 and ideal behaviour (concentration used in place of activity). Temperature constancy is a condition of use, not an assumption about α.
7. The statement that the law assumes α is independent of temperature is False.
 a = sqrt(K/C)
 K = K(T)  (van't Hoff), dH > 0 for ionisation
 T up -> K up -> a up
 K x 2 at same C -> a x sqrt(2) = a x 1.41

 law used at constant T, not "a independent of T"
 -----------------------------------------
 statement : FALSE              <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Only two things change α of a given weak electrolyte: concentration (via √(1/C)) and temperature (via K). A common ion also suppresses it. Any statement saying α ignores one of these is false.
Answer: False.
2 (iii)True or false: The degree of ionization increases with the decrease in concentration according to Ostwald's law.Dilution increases ionization2 Ionic Equilibrium | Ostwald's Dilution Law | IIT Advanced | JEE Main | Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Lowering the concentration (diluting) spreads the ions apart, so they recombine less often and the equilibrium HA ⇌ H+ + A− shifts right. Ostwald’s law quantifies this: α grows as 1/√C. The trap: [H+] itself still falls on dilution, even though α rises.
Key relation α = √(K/C) ⇒ α2/α1 = √(C1/C2) K is fixed at constant temperature, so only C changes.
Solution
1. For a weak acid, K = Cα2/(1 − α) ≈ Cα2, so α = √(K/C).
2. Since K is constant, α ∝ 1/√C: a smaller C gives a larger α.
3. Numerical check with Ka = 1.8 × 10−5: at C = 0.1 M, α = √(1.8 × 10−5/0.1) = 0.0134.
4. At C = 0.001 M, α = √(1.8 × 10−5/0.001) = 0.134: a 100-fold dilution raises α by √100 = 10 times.
5. Meanwhile [H+] = Cα = √(KC) goes from 1.34 × 10−3 to 1.34 × 10−4 M, so the solution becomes less acidic. More fraction ionised, but of far fewer molecules.
6. Le Chatelier agrees: dilution lowers every concentration, and the side with more particles (the ions) is favoured.
7. So α increases as concentration decreases: True.

 C = 0.001 : a = 0.134  , [H+] = 1.34e-4

⚡ JEE Trick Dilution: α up by √n, [H+] down by √n, pH up by (1/2)log n. If an option claims “dilution increases [H+] because α increases”, it is the classic distractor.
Answer: True.
2 (iv)True or false: Ostwald's law does not apply to strong acids and bases.Strong electrolytes excluded, Ostwald's dilution law is not applicable for strongelectrolytes because:-(1) Strong electrolyte...🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Strong acids (HCl, HNO3, H2SO4 first step) and strong bases (NaOH, KOH) are completely ionised in water. There is no measurable equilibrium between ions and molecules, so there is no constant K for Ostwald’s law to use.
Key relation K = Cα2/(1 − α); for α = 1 the denominator is 0 The law has a meaning only when an undissociated fraction (1 − α) exists in equilibrium.
Solution
1. For 0.01 M HCl: HCl → H+ + Cl− goes to completion, so [H+] = 0.01 M and pH = 2, with α = 1.
2. Put α = 1 into K = Cα2/(1 − α) = 0.01/0: undefined. No ionisation constant exists.
3. Diluting a strong acid does not change α (already 1), so the law’s central prediction, α ∝ √V, fails. [H+] simply falls in proportion to C.
4. The measured conductance of strong electrolytes follows Debye–Hückel–Onsager (Λ = Λ° − b√C), caused by interionic attraction, not by changing α.
5. The same reasoning applies to strong bases: 0.01 M NaOH gives [OH−] = 0.01 M, pOH = 2, pH = 12, with no Kb to use.
6. Hence Ostwald’s law does not apply to strong acids and bases: True.

⚡ JEE Trick For a strong acid or base, skip Ostwald entirely: [H+] or [OH−] equals n × C straight away. Only at extreme dilution (C < 10−6 M) must you also add water’s own 10−7 M.
Answer: True.
