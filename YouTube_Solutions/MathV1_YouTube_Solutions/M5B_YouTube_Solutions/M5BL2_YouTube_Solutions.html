1Which of the following correctly represents the product-to-sum identity for (cos A cos B)? (Level 1) (a) cos A cos B = 1/2[sin(A+B)+sin(A-B)] (b) cos A cos B = 1/2[cos(A+B)+cos(A-B)] (c) cos A cos B = 1/2[cos(A+B)-cos(A-B)] (d) cos A cos B = 1/2[sin(A+B)-sin(A-B)]Product-to-sum identity for cos·cosProduct-to-sum: cosA cosB = 1/2[cos(A-B)+cos(A+B)]🟢 Exactbcos A cos B = 1/2[cos(A+B)+cos(A-B)]
Concept This is a pure identity-recognition question: which sum of cosines equals the product cos A cos B? The four product-to-sum identities all come from adding or subtracting the compound-angle expansions of cos(A±B) and sin(A±B). The trap is the sign pattern — cos·cos and sin·sin both produce a pair of cosines, but cos·cos takes the PLUS between them while sin·sin takes the MINUS (and in reversed order). Deriving it in two lines is safer than memorising.
Formula cos(A+B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB and cos(A−B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB. Adding these two expansions cancels the sinA sinB terms and doubles the cosA cosB term — that addition IS the identity being asked for.
Solution
1. Write both compound-angle expansions: cos(A+B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB and cos(A−B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB.
2. Add them term by term: cos(A+B) + cos(A−B) = 2 cosA cosB + (−sinA sinB + sinA sinB) = 2 cosA cosB.The sine products are equal and opposite, so they vanish — only the cosine product survives, doubled.
3. Divide by 2: cosA cosB = ½[cos(A+B) + cos(A−B)] — exactly option (b).
4. Eliminate the others: (a) and (d) use sines — impossible, since a cos·cos product is even in both A and B while sin(A±B) is odd in them; (c) has a minus, which is the sin·sin pattern: ½[cos(A−B) − cos(A+B)] = sinA sinB, not cosA cosB.
5. Sanity check with A = B = 0: LHS = 1·1 = 1; option (b) gives ½[cos0 + cos0] = ½(1+1) = 1 ✓, while (c) gives ½(1−1) = 0 ✗.
   cos(A+B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB
 + cos(A−B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB
 ─────────────────────────────────
   sum      = 2 cosA cosB
 ⇒ cosA cosB = ½[cos(A+B)+cos(A−B)]   <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Never trust memory on these four — test the option with A = B = 0° (or 45°) in five seconds; only the correct identity survives. Remember the parity rule: cos·cos and sin·sin are even functions, so their expansions can contain only cosines. Trap: option (c) is the sin·sin identity wearing a cos·cos label — the minus sign is the giveaway.
Answer: (b) cos A cos B = ½[cos(A+B) + cos(A−B)].

6Show that (sinx - sin3x + sin5x - sin7x)/(cosx - cos3x - cos5x + cos7x) = cot2x. (Level 5)Sum-to-product ratio giving cot 2x(sinx+sin3x+sin5x)/(cosx+cos3x+cos5x)=tan3x🟡 Similar—
Concept Four sine terms over four cosine terms — far too messy to attack head-on. The winning idea is strategic pairing: choose which two terms to combine so that every sum-to-product step throws out the same common factor top and bottom. Pair the terms whose angles are symmetric about 4x (namely x with 7x, and 3x with 5x), because then each pair produces a factor involving 4x, and after a second round of pairing the entire numerator and denominator share the factor sinx·cos4x, leaving only cos2x/sin2x = cot2x.
Formula sin C − sin D = 2 cos((C+D)/2) sin((C−D)/2); cos C − cos D = −2 sin((C+D)/2) sin((C−D)/2); and sin(−θ) = −sinθ. Both are "difference" formulae, so both grouped pairs must be written as differences.
Solution
1. Numerator — regroup as (sinx − sin7x) + (sin5x − sin3x).x and 7x average to 4x; 3x and 5x also average to 4x — so both pairs will produce cos4x.
2. sinx − sin7x = 2 cos((x+7x)/2) sin((x−7x)/2) = 2 cos4x sin(−3x) = −2 cos4x sin3x.
3. sin5x − sin3x = 2 cos((5x+3x)/2) sin((5x−3x)/2) = 2 cos4x sinx.
4. Add and pull out the common 2cos4x: Numerator = 2cos4x(sinx − sin3x).
5. Reduce the bracket: sinx − sin3x = 2 cos((x+3x)/2) sin((x−3x)/2) = 2cos2x sin(−x) = −2 cos2x sinx.
6. So Numerator = 2cos4x × (−2cos2x sinx) = −4 sinx cos2x cos4x.
7. Denominator — regroup as (cosx − cos3x) + (cos7x − cos5x).The printed signs are +cosx −cos3x −cos5x +cos7x, so these two differences reproduce them exactly.
8. cosx − cos3x = −2 sin((x+3x)/2) sin((x−3x)/2) = −2 sin2x sin(−x) = 2 sin2x sinx.
9. cos7x − cos5x = −2 sin((7x+5x)/2) sin((7x−5x)/2) = −2 sin6x sinx.
10. Add and pull out 2sinx: Denominator = 2sinx(sin2x − sin6x).
11. Reduce the bracket: sin2x − sin6x = 2 cos((2x+6x)/2) sin((2x−6x)/2) = 2cos4x sin(−2x) = −2 cos4x sin2x.
12. So Denominator = 2sinx × (−2cos4x sin2x) = −4 sinx sin2x cos4x.
13. Divide: (−4 sinx cos2x cos4x) ÷ (−4 sinx sin2x cos4x). The factors −4, sinx and cos4x cancel completely.This is exactly why the pairing was chosen — the junk is identical top and bottom.
14. Left with cos2x / sin2x = cot 2x. Identity proved.

Trap: signs. sin C − sin D keeps a plus outside, but cos C − cos D carries a leading minus; and sin(−θ) = −sinθ flips again — three sign slips are possible in one line, so write each substitution out.

4. Denominator: sin(2θ+φ) − sinφ = 2 cos((2θ+2φ)/2) sin((2θ)/2) = 2 cos(θ+φ) sinθ.

Concept A four-term sine expression must become a triple product of sines of half-sums of angle pairs. The strategy is to pair strategically: combine sinα + sinβ (giving the factor sin((α+β)/2)), and combine sinγ − sin(α+β+γ) (which also produces sin((α+β)/2), with a minus). Formula sinC + sinD = 2 sin((C+D)/2) cos((C−D)/2), sinC − sinD = 2 cos((C+D)/2) sin((C−D)/2), cosC − cosD = 2 sin((C+D)/2) sin((D−C)/2). Each pairing is chosen so that sin((α+β)/2) appears as a common factor — the target's first factor tells you what to engineer.
