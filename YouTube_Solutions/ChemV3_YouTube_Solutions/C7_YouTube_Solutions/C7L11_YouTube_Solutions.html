1 (i)Fill in the blank: The ______ effect refers to the decrease in solubility of an ionic precipitate when a soluble compound containing a common ion is added.Common ion effect definitionThe Common Ion Effect🟡 Similarcommon ion
Concept When a sparingly soluble salt is in equilibrium with its ions, adding a strong electrolyte that supplies one of those same ions pushes the equilibrium back towards the solid. Less salt dissolves. This suppression is called the common ion effect; the trap is to confuse it with the salt effect (an unrelated ion slightly raises solubility).
Key relation Ksp = [M+][X−] = constant at fixed T Because Ksp is fixed, raising one ion concentration forces the other (and hence the solubility) down.
Solution
1. Take a sparingly soluble salt MX: MX(s) ⇒ M+(aq) + X−(aq), with Ksp = [M+][X−].
2. In pure water the solubility is s, so Ksp = s × s = s2 and s = √Ksp.
3. Now dissolve it in a solution already holding c mol/L of X− (from a soluble salt NaX). Then Ksp = s′(s′ + c) ≈ s′c, so s′ = Ksp/c.
4. Example: AgCl, Ksp = 1.8×10−10; s = √(1.8×10−10) = 1.34×10−5 M in water.
5. In 0.1 M NaCl: s′ = Ksp/c = 1.8×10−10/0.1 = 1.8×10−9 M, about 7400 times smaller.
6. The added Cl− is the ion common to both solutes, so the name of the effect is the common ion effect.
 AgCl(s) <=> Ag+ + Cl-        Ksp = 1.8e-10

 pure water : s  = sqrt(Ksp)       = 1.34e-5 M
 0.1 M NaCl : s' = Ksp / [Cl-]     = 1.8e-9  M

 s / s' ~ 7400  -> solubility falls sharply
 cause : Cl- is common to AgCl and NaCl
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = common ion                <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Le Chatelier in one line: adding a product ion shifts the dissolution equilibrium left. The same effect suppresses the ionisation of a weak acid (CH3COOH + CH3COONa), which is why buffers work. Do not write “salt effect” — that is the opposite trend caused by a non-common ion.
Answer: common ion effect.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: The pH of a mixture of a weak acid and a weak base depends on the ______ and ______ of the two substances.Weak acid weak base pH14 Salt of Weak Acid And Weak Base Ph Calculation | IIT Advanced | JEE Main | Chemistry Class 11🟡 Similarconcentrations; strengths
Concept When a weak acid HA and a weak base B are mixed, they partly neutralise each other. Whatever is left over (excess acid, excess base, or only the salt BH+A−) decides the pH. So the pH depends on how much of each is present (concentration) and how strong each is (Ka, Kb).
Key relation equimolar mix (only salt left): pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) The salt of a weak acid and a weak base hydrolyses at both ions, so both constants appear.
Solution
1. Mix n1 mol HA with n2 mol B: HA + B → BH+ + A−. The amounts n1, n2 (concentration × volume) decide which species is in excess.
2. If acid is in excess, the mixture is an acid buffer: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) — depends on Ka and on the concentrations.
3. If base is in excess, it is a basic buffer: pOH = pKb + log([BH+]/[B]) — depends on Kb and the concentrations.
4. If they are equimolar, only the salt remains: pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb), which depends on the two strengths.
5. Check with CH3COONH4: pKa = 4.74, pKb = 4.74, pH = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 − 4.74) = 7.00.
6. So the two blanks are the concentrations and the strengths (Ka, Kb) of the two substances — matching Column-II (q) of Q3.
 HA + B -> BH+ + A-

 acid excess : pH  = pKa + log([A-]/[HA])
 base excess : pOH = pKb + log([BH+]/[B])
 equimolar   : pH  = 7 + (pKa - pKb)/2

 CH3COONH4 : pH = 7 + (4.74 - 4.74)/2 = 7.00
 -----------------------------------------
 blanks = concentrations, strengths   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Every formula for such a mixture contains either a ratio of concentrations or pKa/pKb, so both words must appear. The trap is answering “volume and temperature”: volume matters only through concentration (amount), and T only through Ka, Kb, Kw.
Answer: concentrations and strengths (Ka, Kb).

Concept Hydrolysis is the reverse of neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water, so salt + water ⇒ acid + base. The ion of the salt that came from a weak partner reacts with water, regenerating some of the weak acid or weak base and releasing H+ or OH−.

1 (v)Fill in the blank: The common ion effect is applied in the process of ______, which is used to separate a mixture of salts.Common ion effect applicationCommon Ion Effect and it's Applications | Ion Equilibrium | Applications in Salt Analysis #CommonIon🟡 Similarfractional crystallisation
Concept Adding a common ion lowers the solubility of the salt that shares it, so that salt can be made to crystallise out selectively while the others stay in solution. The book (Column-I A ↔ Column-II t of Q3) names this separation process fractional crystallisation; the classic textbook case is purifying NaCl by passing HCl gas (salting out).
Key relation Qip = [M+][X−] > Ksp ⇒ that salt precipitates The common ion raises the ionic product only of the salt that contains it.
Solution
1. Take a solution saturated with NaCl and containing soluble impurities (MgCl2, CaCl2, Na2SO4).
2. Pass HCl gas: [Cl−] rises sharply, so [Na+][Cl−] exceeds the solubility product of NaCl.
3. Pure NaCl crystallises out; the more soluble impurities remain dissolved and are removed with the mother liquor.
4. The same idea separates salts in qualitative analysis: NH4Cl (common NH4+) suppresses NH4OH so only Group-III hydroxides precipitate.
5. Repeating the dissolve–crystallise cycle with the common ion added gives fractions enriched in one salt — hence “fractional” crystallisation.
6. So the blank is fractional crystallisation, consistent with the book’s own match (A)–(t).

2 (i)True or false: The common ion effect decreases the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt.Common ion effect solubilityWhat is Common Ion Effect? Common ion effect lowers solubility?🤔 - Dr K🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept For a sparingly soluble salt in equilibrium with its ions, Ksp is fixed at a given temperature. Supplying one of its ions from outside pushes the dissolution equilibrium back to the left, so less of the salt can dissolve. The statement is true; the trap is the salt effect of an unrelated ion, which slightly raises solubility instead.
Key relation Ksp = s′(s′ + c) ≈ s′c ⇒ s′ = Ksp/c Valid when s′ ≪ c, which is always so for a sparingly soluble salt in a strong electrolyte.
Solution
1. Take BaSO4: BaSO4(s) ⇒ Ba2+ + SO42−, Ksp = 1.0×10−10.
2. In pure water: s = √Ksp = √(1.0×10−10) = 1.0×10−5 M.
3. In 0.01 M Na2SO4 (c = 0.01 M common SO42−): s′ = Ksp/c = 1.0×10−10/0.01 = 1.0×10−8 M.
4. Check the approximation: s′ = 10−8 ≪ c = 10−2, so s′ + c ≈ c is valid.
5. Solubility drops by a factor 10−5/10−8 = 1000.
6. Hence the common ion effect decreases the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt — the statement is True.

 water       : s  = sqrt(1e-10)     = 1.0e-5 M
 0.01 M Na2SO4: s' = 1e-10 / 0.01   = 1.0e-8 M

 s'(1e-8) << c(1e-2)  -> approximation OK
 s / s' = 1000  -> solubility decreased

Exception to remember: a very large excess can raise solubility if a soluble complex forms (AgCl in excess Cl− → AgCl2−), but that is complexation, not the common ion effect.

2 (ii)True or false: A weak acid will always lower the pH of a weak base when mixed together.Weak acid weak base mixingHydrolysis of Salt of Weak Acid and Weak Base - Chemical Equilibrium - Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Mixing a weak acid into a weak base solution transfers protons from the acid to the base (HA + B ⇒ A− + BH+). Some base is consumed, so [OH−] falls and the pH goes down. The word “always” is the trap: the pH always decreases, but the mixture need not become acidic — that part depends on the concentrations and strengths.
Key relation pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) for an equimolar mix Compare this with the pH of the base alone to see the fall.
Solution
1. Take 0.1 M NH3 alone (pKb = 4.74): [OH−] = √(KbC) = √(10−4.74 × 0.1) = 10−2.87, pOH = 2.87, pH = 11.13.
2. Add an equal amount of CH3COOH (pKa = 4.74): HA + B → A− + BH+, leaving the salt CH3COONH4.
3. pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 − 4.74) = 7.00 — lower than 11.13.
4. Add instead an equal amount of HCN (pKa = 9.21): pH = 7 + (1/2)(9.21 − 4.74) = 9.24 — still basic, yet lower than 11.13.
5. Add only half an equivalent of acid: the result is the NH3/NH4+ buffer, pOH = pKb = 4.74, pH = 9.26 — again lower.
6. In every case the pH of the base is lowered (by acid consumption and dilution), so the statement as written is True; only “makes it acidic” would be false.

Answer: True (the pH always falls, though the mixture may remain basic).

2 (iv)True or false: The common ion effect is directly related to Le Chatelier's Principle.Common ion Le ChatelierIonic Equilibrium 04 || Common - Ion Effect IIT JEE MAINS/ NEET ||🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept The common ion effect is Le Chatelier’s principle applied to an ionic equilibrium: adding a product ion is a stress, and the system relieves it by shifting backwards — less ionisation of a weak electrolyte, or less dissolution of a sparingly soluble salt. The statement is true.
Key relation Q = [products]/[reactants] > K ⇒ reaction shifts left Adding a common (product) ion raises Q above K; equilibrium is restored only by backward reaction.
Solution
1. Take 0.1 M CH3COOH: CH3COOH ⇒ CH3COO− + H+, Ka = 1.8×10−5.
2. Alone: α = √(Ka/C) = √(1.8×10−5/0.1) = 1.34×10−2 (1.34 %).
3. Add 0.1 M CH3COONa: [CH3COO−] jumps, so Q = [A−][H+]/[HA] > Ka.
4. Le Chatelier: the equilibrium shifts left, consuming H+. New α = Ka/[A−] = 1.8×10−5/0.1 = 1.8×10−4.
5. Ionisation is suppressed about 75 times (1.34×10−2/1.8×10−4 ≈ 74), yet Ka itself is unchanged.
6. The suppression is exactly the backward shift predicted by Le Chatelier → the statement is True.
 CH3COOH <=> CH3COO- + H+    Ka = 1.8e-5

 alone      : a = sqrt(1.8e-5/0.1) = 1.34e-2
 + 0.1 M A- : Q > Ka -> shift left (Le Chatelier)
              a = 1.8e-5 / 0.1     = 1.8e-4

 a falls ~74x , Ka unchanged
 -----------------------------------------
 statement : True                  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember: the common ion changes the position of equilibrium (α, solubility), never the constant (Ka, Ksp) — only temperature changes K. A JEE option saying “common ion decreases Ka” is the standard wrong distractor.
Answer: True.

Key relation WA + WB salt: pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) pKa < pKb (acid stronger than base) ⇒ pH < 7.
Solution
1. (a) Ammonium formate HCOONH4: pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) = 7 + (1/2)(3.75 − 4.74) = 7 − 0.495 = 6.5. Slightly acidic.
2. Reason: Ka(HCOOH) = 1.8×10−4 is ten times Kb(NH4OH) = 1.8×10−5, so NH4+ hydrolysis (Kh 5.6×10−10) beats HCOO− hydrolysis (Kh 5.6×10−11).
3. (b) (NH4)2SO4: strong H2SO4 + weak NH4OH. [NH4+] = 0.2 M, [H+] = √(5.6×10−10×0.2) = 1.06×10−5, pH ≈ 4.98. Acidic.
4. (c) NH4Cl: strong HCl + weak base. pH = 7 − (1/2)pKb − (1/2)log C = 7 − 2.37 + 0.5 = 5.13. Acidic.
5. (d) HCOONa: weak acid + strong base. pH = 7 + (1/2)pKa + (1/2)log C = 7 + 1.875 − 0.5 = 8.38. Basic.
6. Acidic set = (a), (b), (c). The key lists only (b), (c), omitting ammonium formate even though the data it supplies make that salt acidic (pH 6.5).
