1Velocity of electron in nth orbit of a hydrogen atom is proportional to?Velocity ∝ 1/nA-VEL🟡 Similarb1/n
Concept In the Bohr model the electron is held in a circular orbit by the Coulomb pull of the nucleus. Balancing that electrostatic force against the centripetal requirement, together with Bohr's quantization of angular momentum, fixes the orbital speed. The result is a compact formula in which the speed depends only on the atomic number Z and the orbit number n — for a given atom (fixed Z) the speed is controlled entirely by n.
Formula v = 2.18×10⁶ · Z/n m s⁻¹, where Z = atomic number (pure number), n = principal quantum number (1, 2, 3, …). Z sits on top and n on the bottom, so at fixed Z the speed is inversely proportional to n.
Solution
1. Write the Bohr speed law: v = 2.18×10⁶ · Z/n.This is the standard result of force-balance plus mvr = nh/2π.
2. Fix the atom as hydrogen, so Z = 1 is a constant: v = 2.18×10⁶ · (1/n).
3. With every factor except n now constant, v ∝ 1/n — option (b).Doubling n halves the speed; the electron in higher orbits moves slower.
 orbit n   v = 2.18×10⁶ · 1/n     relative speed
 1         2.18×10⁶ m/s           1
 2         1.09×10⁶ m/s           1/2
 3         0.727×10⁶ m/s          1/3
⚡ JEE Trick For a hydrogen-like atom just read the exponents off v ∝ Z/n: speed goes as 1/n and as Z. Trap: do not confuse this with radius (r ∝ n²/Z) — velocity falls with n while radius grows with n², so faster electrons live in the inner orbits.
Answer: (b) 1/n.
2Radius ∝ n², velocity ∝ 1/n → orbital frequency varies as?Frequency ∝ 1/n³A-FREQ🟡 Similara1/n³
Concept The orbital (revolution) frequency is how many full loops the electron completes per second. Since it travels a circle of circumference 2πr at speed v, the frequency is speed divided by distance-per-loop: f = v/(2πr). To see how f scales with n, substitute the known n-dependence of v and r and let the powers of n combine.
Formula f = v ÷ (2πr), with v ∝ 1/n and r ∝ n². Dividing a "1/n" numerator by an "n²" denominator stacks the powers: 1/n ÷ n² = 1/n³.
Solution
1. Start from f = v/(2πr) — revolutions per second = speed ÷ circumference.
2. Insert the scalings: v ∝ 1/n and r ∝ n², so f ∝ (1/n) ÷ (n²).
3. Combine the powers of n: (1/n) ÷ n² = 1/(n·n²) = 1/n³.Dividing by n² adds two more powers of n to the single power already in the denominator.
4. The constant 2π does not affect the n-dependence, so f ∝ 1/n³ — option (a).
 quantity   n-dependence
 v          1/n
 r          n²
 f = v/2πr  (1/n)/(n²) = 1/n³
⚡ JEE Trick Track only the powers of n: f ∝ v/r ∝ (1/n)/(n²) = 1/n³ (and with Z, f ∝ Z²/n³). Trap: don't stop at 1/n² — the radius contributes n², not n, so the exponent is 3, not 2.
Answer: (a) 1/n³.

Concept All electromagnetic radiation travels through vacuum at the same fixed speed c = 3×10⁸ m s⁻¹. That single speed ties together a wave's frequency (loops per second) and its wavelength (length of one loop) — their product must always equal c. So once the wavelength is known, the frequency is forced. The only subtlety is unit conversion: nanometres must be turned into metres before dividing.
Key relation c = f·λ ⇒ f = c ÷ λ, with c = 3×10⁸ m s⁻¹, λ in m, f in Hz (s⁻¹). Dividing a speed (m s⁻¹) by a length (m) leaves s⁻¹, the correct unit for frequency. Also 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.

4. Subtract the exponents: 10⁸ ÷ 10⁻⁷ = 10⁸⁺⁷ = 10¹⁵.Dividing by 10⁻⁷ is the same as multiplying by 10⁺⁷, so the exponents add.
5. Combine: f = 0.667×10¹⁵ = 6.67×10¹⁴ Hz.

2Obtain a relation for angular momentummvr = nh/2πA-ANGMOM🟡 Similar—
Concept Bohr's second postulate says an electron can only occupy orbits in which its angular momentum is quantized — it cannot take any value, only whole-number multiples of a basic unit. The relation is anchored in de Broglie's idea that the electron behaves as a standing wave wrapped around the orbit: only orbits whose circumference holds a whole number of wavelengths are stable, and that whole-number condition is exactly what quantizes the angular momentum.
Key relation L = mvr = n·(h/2π), where m = electron mass, v = orbital speed, r = orbit radius, n = 1, 2, 3, … (principal quantum number), h = Planck's constant. Angular momentum for circular motion is (mass)×(speed)×(radius); Bohr restricts it to integer multiples of h/2π.
Solution
1. For a mass m moving at speed v in a circle of radius r, angular momentum L = m·v·r (linear momentum mv times the lever arm r).
2. de Broglie: the electron has wavelength λ = h/(mv), and a stable orbit fits a whole number of these waves around its circumference.A non-integer number of waves would interfere destructively and cancel the orbit.
3. Standing-wave condition: circumference = n wavelengths ⇒ 2πr = n·λ = n·(h/mv).
4. Rearrange to isolate mvr: multiply both sides by mv ÷ 2π ⇒ mvr = n·(h/2π).2πr = nh/mv ⇒ (mv)(2πr) = nh ⇒ mvr = nh/2π.
5. Thus L = mvr = nh/2π — angular momentum comes only in units of h/2π.
 2πr = n·λ ,  λ = h/(mv)
 ⇒ 2πr = n·h/(mv)
 ⇒ mvr = n·h/2π           (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember angular momentum is quantized in units of ħ = h/2π: L_n = n·h/2π, so the gap between successive orbits is always h/2π, independent of n and Z. Trap: writing nh (missing the 2π) — the correct quantum unit is h/2π, not h.
Key point: mvr = nh/2π — the electron's angular momentum is quantized in integer multiples of h/2π (from the de Broglie standing-wave condition 2πr = nλ).

Concept When an excited electron in level n falls back to the ground state, it need not jump straight down — it can cascade through every intermediate level. Each distinct pair of levels gives one possible spectral line, so the maximum number of emission lines equals the number of ways to choose 2 levels out of the n available, which is n(n−1)/2. Here the electron starts in n = 6, so levels 1 through 6 are all in play.
Formula N = n(n−1)/2 for a drop from level n to the ground state. Every unordered pair of levels (upper, lower) contributes one line; counting pairs among n levels gives n(n−1)/2.
Solution
1. Levels available during the cascade: n = 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 — six levels in total.
2. Apply the pair-count formula: N = n(n−1)/2 = 6 × 5 ÷ 2.
3. Compute: 6 × 5 = 30, then 30 ÷ 2 = 15 lines.
4. Cross-check by counting drops level by level: from 6 there are 5 possible lower levels, from 5 there are 4, then 3, 2, 1 — total 5+4+3+2+1 = 15. ✓The triangular-number sum 1+2+…+(n−1) is exactly n(n−1)/2, confirming the formula.
5. These 15 lines group into the familiar series: 5 Lyman (→1), 4 Balmer (→2), 3 Paschen (→3), 2 Brackett (→4), 1 Pfund (→5).

 4              4→3,2,1       = 3
