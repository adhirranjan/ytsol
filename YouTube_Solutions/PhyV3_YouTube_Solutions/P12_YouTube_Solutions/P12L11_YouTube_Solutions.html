1If a street light of mass M is suspended from the end of a uniform rod of length L in the different possible patterns as shown in figure, then — in (A) the cable runs from the wall to the far end of the rod (distance L from the hinge), in (B) to the point at 3L/4 and in (C) to the point at L/2. (a) pattern A is more sturdy (b) pattern B is more sturdy (c) pattern C is more sturdy (d) all will have same sturdiness — (Level-1)Rotational equilibrium of a hinged rodToppling & torque equilibrium about a hinge (JEE)🟡 Similarapattern A is more sturdy
Concept The rod is a rigid body in equilibrium, hinged to the wall at H and held up by one cable. Three torques act about H: the lamp's weight Mg at the free end, the rod's own weight mg at its midpoint, and the cable tension T where the cable meets the rod. The two weights pull the rod down; only the cable can balance them, so the cable's moment arm alone decides how hard it must pull. "More sturdy" simply means the smallest force in the cable, the rod and the wall bracket.
Formula ΣτH = 0 ⇒ T·d·sinφ = MgL + mg(L/2). Each torque is force × perpendicular distance from the hinge; d is where the cable grips the rod and φ the angle it makes with the rod.
Solution
1. In all three patterns the wall, the rod and the cable form the same shape, so φ and the load are unchanged — only the grip distance d changes.That is the whole point of the question: one variable, three values.
2. Solving the balance: T = [MgL + mgL/2] / (d sinφ) = kL/d, with k = (M + m/2)g/sinφ a constant for all three.
3. Pattern A, d = L: TA = kL/L = k — the baseline.
4. Pattern B, d = 3L/4: TB = kL/(3L/4) = 4k/3 = 1.33 TA.
5. Pattern C, d = L/2: TC = kL/(L/2) = 2k = 2 TA — double the pull of A.T ∝ 1/d, so halving the moment arm doubles the tension.
6. The hinge reaction is found from ΣF = 0 and grows with T as well, so pattern A loads cable, rod and wall bracket least; (b), (c) reverse the trend and (d) ignores that d differs.

 --------------------------------------------------
    A          d = L            k           1.00
    B          d = 3L/4        4k/3         1.33
    C          d = L/2          2k          2.00

Whenever a figure changes only one distance, write the torque balance once and read the answer off the 1/d.
Answer: (a) pattern A is more sturdy.
2A cylinder is given angular velocity ω0 and kept on a horizontal rough surface. The initial velocity is zero. Find out distance travelled by the cylinder before it performs pure rolling and work done by friction forces. — (Level-2)Spinning cylinder settling into pure rollingDisc released with ω0 on a rough surface → pure rolling🟡 SimilarS = ω02R2/(18μg), Wf = −mω02R2/6
Concept The cylinder is spun up but its centre is at rest, so the contact point skids backwards. Kinetic friction therefore acts forwards: it drives the centre while its torque bleeds the spin away, until v = ωR and slipping stops. Sliding means energy is lost, and that loss is exactly the work done by friction.
Formula f = μmg, a = μg, α = fR/I = 2μg/R, Wf = ΔKE. Icm = ½mR²; the work–energy theorem beats f×distance because the contact point slips.
Solution
1. Translation: a = μg so v = μgt; rotation: α = μmgR/(½mR²) = 2μg/R so ω = ω0 − (2μg/R)t.Friction is forward on the body but its torque about the centre still opposes the spin.
2. Rolling starts when v = ωR: μgt = ω0R − 2μgt ⇒ 3μgt = ω0R ⇒ t = ω0R/(3μg).
3. Distance: S = ½at² = ½(μg)ω02R²/(9μ²g²) = ω02R²/(18μg).
4. Final state: v = μgt = ω0R/3 and ω = v/R = ω0/3 — the spin has dropped to a third.A neat check: for a solid cylinder started this way, one third of the initial ω always survives.
5. KEf = ½m(ω0R/3)² + ¼mR²(ω0/3)² = (1/18 + 1/36)mω02R² = mω02R²/12.
6. KEi = ¼mR²ω02, so Wf = (1/12 − 3/12)mω02R² = −mω02R²/6 — two thirds of the start energy is burnt.
 quantity        start        at rolling
 -----------------------------------------
 v                0           w0*R/3
 w                w0          w0/3
 KE            m w0^2 R^2/4  m w0^2 R^2/12
 -----------------------------------------
 t = w0*R/(3 mu g),  S = w0^2 R^2/(18 mu g)
 W_friction = -m w0^2 R^2 / 6
⚡ JEE Trick The classic trap is computing Wf as −μmg·S: while the body slips, friction does positive work on the translation and negative work on the spin, so only Wf = ΔKE is safe. The second trap is the sign of α — friction pushes the cylinder forward yet still slows its rotation.
Answer: S = ω02R2/(18μg) and Wf = −mω02R2/6.
3A cubical block of side 30 cm is placed on a rough horizontal surface of coefficient of friction 0.6. At what minimum height above the ground, a horizontal force must be applied on the block so that it starts toppling before slipping? — (Level-3)Toppling before slippingToppling: condition for sliding before toppling (JEE)🟡 Similarh ≥ 25 cm
Concept A block pushed sideways can fail in two rival ways. It slides when F beats the limiting friction μmg; it tips when the torque of F about the front bottom edge E beats the restoring torque of its own weight about E. Raising the point of application increases F's moment arm without changing the friction limit, so a high push topples and a low push slides. The question asks for the crossover height where toppling wins.
Formula topple: F·h ≥ mg·(a/2); slip: F ≥ μmg. The normal reaction can shift at most to the edge E, half a side a/2 from the weight's line, which fixes the maximum restoring torque.
Solution
1. Data: a = 0.30 m, μ = 0.6, so a/2 = 0.15 m and the friction ceiling is Fslip = 0.6 mg.
2. Force needed to just topple at height h: Ftopple = mg(a/2)/h = 0.15 mg/h.Torques taken about the far bottom edge E, where N sits at the instant of tipping.
3. Toppling happens first only if the tipping force is reached before the sliding force: Ftopple ≤ Fslip.
4. 0.15 mg/h ≤ 0.6 mg ⇒ h ≥ 0.15/0.6 = 0.25 m = 25 cm.The mass cancels — the answer depends only on the geometry and μ.
5. Sanity check at h = 25 cm: Ftopple = 0.15mg/0.25 = 0.6 mg, exactly the slipping force, so 25 cm is the crossover.
6. Push lower, say h = 0.15 m: Ftopple = mg > 0.6 mg, so the block slides away long before it tips.

 -------------------------------------------------
   0.15 m              1.00 mg          slides
   0.20 m              0.75 mg          slides
   0.25 m              0.60 mg          crossover

⚡ JEE Trick Note the general result hidden here: h ≥ a/(2μ). Since a cube's top is only a = 30 cm high, h = 25 cm is inside the block — if μ had been below 0.5 the required height would exceed the cube and the block could never be toppled by a horizontal push. Always check the answer physically fits on the body.
Answer: h ≥ 25 cm (i.e. a/2μ).

Angular momentum about the hinge is, because the hinge force has zero moment arm about itself and gravity acts vertically on a horizontal plane. The collision is inelastic and the mass sticks, so afterwards the rod plus the embedded particle turn together as one rigid body about H.

5A wedge in the form of equilateral triangle is placed on a rough horizontal surface as shown in the figure (a horizontal force F is applied at the apex P, the base QS resting on the ground). For what value of coefficient of friction, can the block topple without slipping? — (Level-5)Toppling of a triangular wedgeToppling Cases for JEE Main and Advanced (Physics Galaxy)🟡 Similarμ ≥ 1/√3 ≈ 0.58
Concept Same race as the cube, but the geometry is a triangle. The wedge tips about its downstream base corner S when the torque of F about S overcomes that of the weight; it slides when F exceeds μmg. Toppling first therefore demands that the tipping force be no larger than the friction ceiling. The centroid of a triangle sits on the vertical through the midpoint of the base, and the apex is at height (√3/2)a.
Formula F·hapex = mg·xG and F ≤ μmg. Torques about the corner S; xG is the horizontal offset of the centroid from S, hapex the height of F's line of action.
Solution
1. Put the base as QS with Q at the origin and S at (a, 0). The apex P is then at (a/2, √3a/2).
2. Centroid G is at x = a/2, so its horizontal distance from the tipping corner S is xG = a − a/2 = a/2.For any triangle the centroid's x is the mean of the vertices: (0 + a + a/2)/3 × ... = a/2 here by symmetry.
3. Torque balance at the point of tipping: F(√3a/2) = mg(a/2) ⇒ F = mg/√3 = 0.577 mg.
4. Translational equilibrium up to that instant needs friction to hold: f = F = mg/√3, and f cannot exceed μmg.If friction saturates first the wedge simply skids away and never tips.
5. Hence mg/√3 ≤ μmg ⇒ μ ≥ 1/√3 ≈ 0.577.
6. With μ = 0.5 the wedge slides at F = 0.5mg, below the 0.577mg needed to tip — so a rougher floor, not a bigger push, is what makes it topple.

⚡ JEE Trick The book reaches the same place by resolving F along the slant face as F sin30° — either route is fine, but mixing the two (using the slant length with the vertical height) is the standard error. Remember the pattern: toppling condition sets the required F, friction sets the available F, and the answer is always an inequality between them.
Answer: μ ≥ 1/√3 (about 0.58).

1A disc is rolling, the velocity of its centre of mass is Vcm, then which one will be correct? (a) The velocity of highest point is 2Vcm and point of contact is zero (b) the velocity of highest point is 2Vcm and point of contact is Vcm (c) the velocity of highest point is 2Vcm and point of contact is Vcm (d) the velocity of highest point is 2Vcm and point of contact is 2Vcm — (Level-1)Velocity distribution in pure rollingRolling Motion — Combined Rotational + Translational Motion (JEE)🟢 Exactatop 2Vcm, contact zero
Concept A rolling disc is doing two motions at once: the whole body slides forward with the centre's velocity Vcm, and at the same time it spins about that centre with ω. The velocity of any material point is the vector sum of those two pieces, so points on top get the spin velocity added to Vcm while points at the bottom get it subtracted. "Rolling without slipping" is precisely the statement that the subtraction at the bottom cancels exactly. That single condition fixes ω and therefore fixes every other point's speed.
Formula v⃗P = v⃗cm + ω⃗ × r⃗P/C, with ω = Vcm/R. The cross-product term is the rotational part, of magnitude ω×(distance from the centre), directed tangentially.
Solution
1. Impose the no-slip condition at the contact point: its total velocity must be zero, so Vcm − ωR = 0 ⇒ ω = Vcm/R.This is the definition of rolling; if the surface were slippery ω would be free and none of the following numbers would hold.
2. Top point, a distance R above the centre: the rotational part points forward, so vtop = Vcm + ωR = Vcm + Vcm = 2Vcm.
3. Contact point, a distance R below the centre: the rotational part points backward, so vbottom = Vcm − ωR = Vcm − Vcm = 0.
4. A point at the side (level with the centre) has a vertical rotational part, so its speed is √(Vcm² + Vcm²) = √2 Vcm — the numbers 0, √2Vcm, 2Vcm are the standard trio.Speed grows linearly with distance from the contact point, which is why the top (2R away) is exactly twice the centre (R away).
5. Only statement (a) carries both correct values; (b) and (c) are the same printed sentence and both give the contact point Vcm, which would mean the disc is skidding.
6. Option (d) makes the contact point move as fast as the top — that is a disc spinning in mid-air with no ground constraint at all.Recognising the contact point as the instantaneous axis of rotation kills (b), (c) and (d) in one line.
 rolling disc, w = V/R, contact point is the IAR
 -----------------------------------------------

 contact           0              0
 centre            R              V
 side point     sqrt2 R      sqrt2 V = 1.41 V
 top              2R             2 V
 -----------------------------------------------
⚡ JEE Trick Treat the contact point as the instantaneous axis of rotation and every speed is just ω × (distance from that axis) — no vector addition needed. The trap is remembering "2V at the top" but then guessing V at the bottom because the disc is obviously moving forward; the bottom point is momentarily frozen against the ground. Options (b) and (c) being word-for-word identical is a printing slip, and both are wrong anyway.
Answer: (a) velocity of the highest point is 2Vcm and of the contact point is zero.
2A drum of radius R and mass M, rolls down without slipping along an inclined plane of angle θ. The frictional force: (a) decreases the rotational and translational motion (b) dissipates energy as heat (c) decreases the rotational motion (d) converts translational energy to rotational energy — (Level-1)Role of static friction in rollingHow to find the direction of friction in rolling / slipping🟡 Similardconverts translational energy to rotational energy
Concept On a rolling body the contact point is instantaneously at rest, so the friction acting there is static, not kinetic. A static friction force never slides through a distance, so the work it does on the body is zero and no heat is produced. What it does instead is supply the only torque about the centre of mass, converting some of the gravitational energy that would have gone into pure sliding into spin. The net effect is a share-out of the same total energy between translation and rotation.
Formula Mg sinθ − f = Ma, f R = Icmα, a = αR. Three equations, three unknowns (a, α, f) — the rolling constraint is what makes friction determinate.
Solution
1. For a drum (hollow cylinder) Icm = MR²; solving the three equations gives a = g sinθ/(1 + I/MR²) = g sinθ/2.
2. Friction follows as f = Ma⋅(I/MR²) = Mg sinθ/2, acting up the incline.Up-slope is forced: friction must torque the drum forward, and only an up-slope force at the bottom does that.
3. Power delivered by friction to the body = f × vcontact = f × 0 = 0, so friction dissipates nothing — (b) is out.
4. At the same time the torque fR is non-zero, so friction is steadily increasing the angular speed — it does not "decrease the rotational motion", which kills (c) and half of (a).
5. Comparing with a frictionless slide, a drops from g sinθ to g sinθ/2, so friction removes exactly half the linear KE and hands it to spin: at the bottom ½Mv² = ½Iω².For the drum the two energies are equal; for a disc the split is 2:1 and for a sphere 5:2.
6. That transfer, with total mechanical energy conserved, is exactly statement (d).Static friction is a "broker", not a "tax": it moves energy between accounts without burning any.
 drum on incline, I = M R^2
 -----------------------------------------
 a          = g sin(th) / 2
 f          = M g sin(th) / 2 , up-slope
 v_contact  = 0  ->  P_friction = f*0 = 0
 -----------------------------------------
 at bottom: (1/2)Mv^2 = (1/2)I w^2
 half of the sliding KE is now spin
⚡ JEE Trick The word "friction" reflexively suggests heat, which is why (b) traps so many; heat needs relative sliding, and pure rolling has none. Equally, (a) and (c) tempt because friction points up the slope and therefore "opposes" the motion — it opposes the translation but drives the rotation. Only when the body actually skids does friction turn kinetic and start dissipating.
Answer: (d) it converts translational energy to rotational energy.
3A tube of length L is filled completely with an incompressible liquid of mass M and closed at both the ends. The tube is then rotated in a horizontal plane about one of its ends with uniform angular velocity ω. The force exerted by the liquid at the other end is: (a) MLω²/2 (b) ML²ω/2 (c) MLω² (d) ML²ω²/2 — (Level-1)Centripetal force on a rotating distributed massCentripetal Force on a Rod Rotating About Its End🟡 SimilaraMLω²/2
Concept Every element of the liquid moves on its own circle, so each needs its own centripetal force — and the required force grows linearly with the distance x from the axis. Because the required force is linear in x, the integral happens to give the same answer as putting all the mass at the centre of mass — a coincidence worth knowing but not worth trusting in general.
Formula dF = (dm)ω²x with dm = (M/L)dx. M/L is the linear mass density of an incompressible liquid filling the tube uniformly.

4A solid sphere of radius R is placed on a smooth horizontal surface. A horizontal force ‘F’ is applied at height ‘h’ from the lowest point. For the maximum acceleration of centre of mass, which is correct? (a) h = R (b) h = 2R (c) h = 0 (d) No relation between h and R — (Level-1)Line of action and centre-of-mass accelerationRolling Motion Explained: Slipping vs. No-Slip Cases🟡 SimilardNo relation between h and R
Concept Newton's second law for the centre of mass, ΣF⃗ = Ma⃗cm, cares only about the vector sum of the external forces — never about where they are applied. Where a force is applied decides the torque, and therefore the angular acceleration, but it cannot change acm at all. On a smooth surface the only horizontal force is F itself, so acm = F/M whatever h is. Height h matters here only for how fast the sphere spins up, and for what the surface has to do.
Formula acm = ΣFext/M and α = τcm/Icm = F(h − R)/[(2/5)MR²]. The torque arm about the centre is the offset (h − R), since the centre sits a height R above the lowest point.
Solution
1. Free-body list on a smooth floor: weight Mg down, normal N up, applied F horizontal. There is no friction — that is what "smooth" buys us.
2. Horizontal equation: F = Macm ⇒ acm = F/M, with h appearing nowhere.The translational equation is blind to the point of application; only the resultant enters.
3. So acm is the same for h = 0, h = R and h = 2R alike — there is no maximum to hunt for, and (a), (b), (c) are all equally false.
4. What h does change: α = F(h − R)/[(2/5)MR²] = 5F(h − R)/(2MR²), which is zero at h = R, negative below the centre and positive above.

5A cylinder rolls up an inclined plane, reaches some height and then rolls down without slipping throughout these motions. The direction of the frictional force acting on the cylinder are: (a) up the incline while ascending and down the incline while descending (b) down the incline while ascending and up the incline while descending (c) up the incline while ascending as well as descending (d) down the incline while ascending as well as descending — (Level-1)Direction of friction in pure rolling on an inclineHow to find the direction of friction in rolling / slipping🟢 Exactcup the incline while ascending as well as descending
Concept Static friction on a rolling body is a constraint force: it takes whatever value and direction are needed to keep a = αR true. So do not ask "which way is the cylinder moving?" — ask "which way must friction point so that the linear and angular equations stay consistent?" Gravity's component along the slope is down the incline in both phases, and gravity has no torque about the centre, so friction has to be the sole torque provider in both phases too. That symmetry is what makes the answer the same going up and coming down.
Formula Mg sinθ − f = Ma, fR = Icmα, a = αR ⇒ f = Mg sinθ/3. For a solid cylinder Icm = ½MR²; taking down-slope as positive and solving gives f positive, i.e. up the slope.
Solution
1. Set the down-slope direction as positive for both phases; then a = +g sinθ/(1 + ½) = +2g sinθ/3 whether the cylinder is going up (decelerating) or coming down (speeding up).Acceleration is a vector; its sign does not flip just because the velocity does.
2. Solving the pair of equations: f = Ma(I/MR²) = M(2g sinθ/3)(½) = Mg sinθ/3, and the positive sign means f points up the incline.
3. Descending: gravity alone would make the cylinder slide without spinning; friction up the slope supplies the forward torque that spins it up to match. Up-slope friction is mandatory.
4. Ascending: the cylinder is slowing down, so its spin must also slow down, meaning the torque must oppose the rotation — and a friction force up the incline at the contact point does exactly that.Same force, same direction, opposite job: it accelerates the spin going down and brakes it going up.
5. Numerically the magnitude is identical in both phases, Mg sinθ/3, because nothing in the derivation used the sign of the velocity.
