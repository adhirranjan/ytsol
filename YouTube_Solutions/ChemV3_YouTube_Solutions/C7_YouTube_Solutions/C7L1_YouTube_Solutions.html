1 (i)Fill in the blank: ______ reactions never go for completion.Reversible reactionsIrreversible and Reversible Chemical Reaction - Chemical Equilibrium - Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarReversible
Concept The chapter opens by splitting every reaction into two families. A reversible reaction is one in which the entire amount of reactant is not converted into product; an irreversible reaction is one in which it is. The blank therefore asks for the family whose defining property is incompleteness.
Key relation Reactant ⇌ Product  (forward and backward both running) The double half-arrow is the printed signal that the backward step exists, and a backward step is exactly what stops the forward step from finishing.
Solution
1. Read the claim literally: “never go for completion” means some reactant is still present no matter how long you wait.
2. In an irreversible reaction the product cannot turn back, so the forward step keeps consuming reactant until none is left — completion is reached. So the blank is not “irreversible”.
3. In a reversible reaction the product can turn back into reactant. As product accumulates the backward rate climbs, and it climbs until it matches the forward rate.
4. From that instant onwards the two rates cancel, so the reactant concentration stops falling while it is still non-zero. The reaction has stopped changing, not stopped running.
5. The book states this as characteristic (b) of reversible reactions on p.7: “These reactions are never complete”, and characteristic (d): the mixture always contains both reactants and products.
6. Hence the blank is Reversible. Examples the chapter lists: esterification, salt hydrolysis, PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2.
 irreversible : A --------> B      A finally = 0
 reversible   : A <=======> B      A finally > 0

 backward rate grows as B grows
 -> forward and backward meet -> no further change
⚡ JEE Trick “Never complete”, “can be started from either side” and “attains equilibrium” are three ways of saying the same one word. If a fill-in-the-blank uses any of them, write reversible without further thought.
Answer: Reversible reactions never go for completion.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: At equilibrium stage, the rate of forward reaction is ______ to the rate of backward reaction.Condition for equilibriumChemical Equilibrium......ATTAINMENT OF EQUILIBRIUM IN CHEMICAL REACTIONS🟡 Similarequal
Concept Equilibrium is defined kinetically, not by amounts. The definition on p.8 reads: equilibrium is the state where the rates of the forward and backward reactions are equal, so there is no net change in the amounts of reactants and products.
Key relation rf = rb  at equilibrium (both non-zero) This is the condition, not a consequence — every other property of the equilibrium state follows from it.
Solution
1. Start the reaction with pure reactant. Forward rate is at its maximum; backward rate is zero because no product exists yet.
2. As reactant is used up the forward rate falls; as product builds up the backward rate rises.
3. One falling curve and one rising curve must meet. The meeting point is where rf = rb.
4. Beyond that point every molecule of product formed is matched by one decomposing, so the net change is zero and the concentrations freeze.
5. Note the word carefully: the rates are equal, not zero, and not “proportional” or “maximum”. Zero rates would describe a dead reaction, which is the classic wrong answer.
6. This is exactly the point marked (rf = rb) on the rate-vs-time graph printed on p.8 of the lecture.

  |  '-.__________ equilibrium state
  |   ,--'''''''''  (rf = rb, both > 0)
  | ,'  backward
  |'________________ time
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question offers “rates become zero” and “rates become equal” as neighbouring options, the answer is always equal. “Zero” describes a completed irreversible reaction, never an equilibrium.
Answer: the rate of forward reaction is equal to the rate of backward reaction.
1 (iii)Fill in the blank: The equilibrium established in physical processes is called ______.Physical equilibriumEquilibria Involving Physical Processes - Equilibrium | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6🟡 Similarphysical equilibrium
Concept The chapter divides equilibrium into two types by asking one question: did the chemical identity of the substance change? If only the physical state (phase) changed, the equilibrium is called physical equilibrium; if a new substance was formed, it is chemical equilibrium.
Key relation Equilibrium in a physical process → physical equilibrium The test is chemical identity, not the number of phases — that is a separate homogeneous/heterogeneous label.
Solution
1. The tree on p.9 branches Equilibrium into Physical Equilibrium and Chemical Equilibrium.
2. Physical Equilibrium is defined there as “equilibrium in physical process”: only the phase changes, no chemical change occurs.
3. The lecture then lists five standard physical equilibria: liquid–vapour, solid–liquid, solid–vapour, saturated solution of a solid, and saturated solution of a gas.
4. In every one of them the substance on both sides is the same — H2O(l) ⇌ H2O(g), I2(s) ⇌ I2(g), NaCl(s) ⇌ NaCl(aq).
5. So the blank is physical equilibrium. Writing “heterogeneous equilibrium” is a trap: it happens to be true (all physical equilibria are heterogeneous) but it is not the name asked for.
6. Chemical equilibrium is the other branch, e.g. H2(g) + Cl2(g) ⇌ 2HCl(g), where a new substance appears.
             Equilibrium
             /         \
   Physical eqm          Chemical eqm
   phase changes         new substance
   H2O(l) <=> H2O(g)     H2 + Cl2 <=> 2HCl
⚡ JEE Trick Two independent labels get confused constantly. Physical/chemical answers “did the identity change?”; homogeneous/heterogeneous answers “how many phases?”. A single equilibrium carries one label of each kind.
Answer: the equilibrium established in physical processes is called physical equilibrium.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: Equilibrium state is not static but ______ in nature.Dynamic natureDynamic Nature of Equilibrium | Chemical Equilibrium🟡 Similardynamic
Concept Equilibrium looks frozen from outside but is running flat out on the inside. The lecture calls this the dynamic nature of equilibrium: macroscopic properties are static, molecular activity is not.
Key relation rf = rb ≠ 0  ⇒  no net change, but continuous change Equality of two non-zero rates is precisely what produces stillness on top of motion.
Solution
1. “Static” would mean nothing is happening at all — both rates zero, molecules parked.
2. But the chapter states that at equilibrium “the reactions continue to happen at the molecular level” even though the macroscopic properties appear unchanging.
3. For A ⇌ B, A keeps turning into B and B keeps turning back into A, at the same speed, all the time.
4. Because the two conversions cancel exactly, [A] and [B] never change — which is why the outside observer sees nothing.
5. The experimental proof is isotopic labelling: put D2 into an H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI mixture already at equilibrium and HD appears, although no concentration changes.
6. So equilibrium is a balance achieved by ongoing activity, not by everything coming to a stop — the blank is dynamic.
 static  : nothing moves          (rf = rb = 0)  X
 dynamic : both directions run    (rf = rb > 0)  OK

 observable: constant
 molecular : busy
⚡ JEE Trick “Not static but ______” is a fixed phrase in this chapter and the answer is always dynamic. Remember the reason too — examiners follow this blank with “why?” and want “because both reactions continue at equal rates”.
Answer: equilibrium state is not static but dynamic in nature.
1 (v)Fill in the blank: On changing the temperature ______ state is disturbed.Effect of temperatureLe Chatelier's Principle | Effect of Concentration | Effect of Pressure | Effect of Temperature🟡 Similarequilibrium
Concept An equilibrium is defined at a fixed temperature. Change the temperature and the forward and backward rate constants change by different amounts, so the balance rf = rb breaks and the system has to move to a new equilibrium position.
Key relation T changes ⇒ kf and kb change unequally ⇒ rf ≠ rb The activation energies of the two directions differ by ΔH, so the two rate constants can never respond to heating by the same factor.
Solution
1. The chapter’s general characteristic (4) of physical equilibria: at equilibrium the concentrations become constant at constant temperature.
2. That proviso is doing real work. The constancy is guaranteed only while T is held fixed.
3. Raise T. Both rate constants rise, but the endothermic direction — the one with the larger activation energy — rises more.
4. The two rates are now unequal, so a net reaction restarts and the concentrations begin to change again.
5. The system settles at a new set of concentrations where the rates match again: a new equilibrium, not the old one.
6. So what got disturbed by the temperature change is the equilibrium state itself. This is the seed of Le Chatelier’s principle, taken up in the later lectures of C-7.
 at T1 : rf = rb        -> equilibrium 1
 heat  : kf, kb rise unequally
         rf =/= rb      -> net reaction restarts
 at T2 : rf = rb again  -> equilibrium 2 (new position)
⚡ JEE Trick Temperature is the only variable that changes the equilibrium constant. Pressure, concentration and catalysts shift the position or the speed but leave K alone — a favourite one-line JEE Main statement question.
Answer: on changing the temperature, the equilibrium state is disturbed.
2 (i)True or false: Equilibrium reactions can take place only in closed vessels.Closed-system requirementEquilibrium can be achieved only in open vessel. | 11 | CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM | CHEMISTRY | CENGA…🟢 ExactTrue
Concept An equilibrium needs both directions to be able to run. If any species can leave the vessel, the backward step is starved and the reaction is driven one way to completion. This is why the chapter’s general characteristic (2) demands a closed container.
Key relation closed system ⇒ nothing escapes ⇒ backward reaction stays possible “Closed” means no matter crosses the boundary; energy may still cross, so heating a sealed tube is allowed.
Solution
1. Take the chapter’s own pair of examples on p.7–8: CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)↑ in an open vessel is written with a single arrow.
2. The CO2 escapes, so it is never available to recombine with CaO. No backward reaction, no equilibrium — the decomposition simply goes to completion.
3. The book then adds: “if the above reaction is carried out in a closed container… the reaction shows reversible nature”.
4. Same for the physical equilibria: water in an open beaker evaporates away entirely, water in a stoppered flask reaches a constant vapour pressure.
5. So the statement is True as the chapter means it — a closed vessel is what makes the equilibrium state attainable.
6. Strictly, the requirement bites only when a gas or vapour is involved; a solid–liquid or solution equilibrium loses nothing to the air and can sit in an open beaker. The book’s own characteristic (2) is worded for gases. Treat the blanket statement as True in the chapter’s sense — nothing may leave the system.
 open   : CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2 (up)     completion
 closed : CaCO3 <=> CaO + CO2         equilibrium

 escape of any species kills the backward step
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a stem says “open vessel”, cross out equilibrium immediately for any gas-producing system — boiling water on a stove and freezing in an open dish are the two decoys this chapter uses.
Answer: True — if the system is not closed, the escaping species prevents the backward reaction and no equilibrium is reached.
2 (ii)True or false: The equilibrium state can be attained from both sides of the chemical reaction.ReversibilityThe equilibrium state can be attained from both sides of the chemical reaction.🟢 ExactTrue
Concept A reversible reaction has no privileged starting side. Because both directions are always available, you can load the vessel with pure reactants or with pure products and end at the same equilibrium position. This is characteristic (a) on p.7: “These reactions can be started from either side.”
Key relation same T, same total composition ⇒ same equilibrium state, from either direction The equilibrium constant fixes the ratio of concentrations; it does not remember which side you started on.
Solution
1. Take H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) in a sealed bulb at a fixed temperature.
2. Run A: charge 1 mol H2 + 1 mol I2. Forward rate is high, backward zero, and HI builds up until the rates match.
3. Run B: charge 2 mol HI into an identical bulb at the same temperature. Now the backward rate is high and the forward rate is zero, so HI decomposes until the rates match.
4. Both runs end at the same three concentrations, because the same K governs both and the total atoms of H and I are the same.
5. Equilibrium is therefore a destination, approached from above or from below — exactly what the statement asserts.
6. Contrast an irreversible reaction: start from the products of NaOH + HCl and nothing happens, because there is no path back.
 from reactants: H2 + I2 ---> ... --->  |
                                        |  same
 from products : 2HI     ---> ... --->  |  equilibrium
                                           mixture
⚡ JEE Trick This is the fastest test for reversibility in a word problem: ask “could I have started from the right-hand side?”. If yes, the reaction is reversible and will reach equilibrium.
Answer: True — the same equilibrium state is reached from the reactant side or the product side.
2 (iii)True or false: A reaction continues even after the attainment of equilibrium.Dynamic natureDEMONSTRATING DYNAMIC NATURE OF EQUILIBRIUM🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept This statement is the dynamic nature of equilibrium written as a true/false. Attaining equilibrium stops the net change; it does not stop the reaction. Both directions keep running, permanently, at the same speed.
Key relation net rate = rf − rb = 0, while rf = rb ≠ 0 Zero net rate and zero rate are entirely different things, and this question is built on that difference.
Solution
1. The chapter’s own words on p.8: “even though the macroscopic properties appear static, the reactions continue to happen at the molecular level”.
2. Suppose the reaction really did stop at equilibrium. Then rf = rb = 0, and the mixture would be indistinguishable from an inert one.
3. But add more reactant to an equilibrium mixture and it responds immediately — a dead mixture could not. So the machinery is clearly still running.
4. The classic experimental proof: introduce radioactive I2* into the equilibrium H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI. Radioactive iodine soon shows up in the HI although no concentration changes.
5. Similarly, in a saturated NaCl solution labelled solid slowly exchanges with the dissolved ions — dissolution and crystallisation both continue.
6. So the reaction continues after equilibrium is attained: the statement is True.
 before eqm : rf > rb   -> net forward change
 at eqm     : rf = rb   -> net change zero
              but each is still large

 labelled atoms scramble = proof it never stopped
⚡ JEE Trick Any option containing the words “the reaction stops”, “processes cease” or “rates become zero” is wrong in this chapter — without exception. Strike them out before reading the rest.
Answer: True — both opposing reactions continue indefinitely at equal rates.
2 (iv)True or false: The concentrations of reactants and products become equal when equilibrium is attained.Constant vs equalThe concentrations of reactants and products become equalhen equilibrium is attained.🟢 ExactFalse
Concept The single most common misconception in this chapter. At equilibrium the concentrations become constant; there is no reason at all for them to become equal. The book flags this explicitly with the parenthesis “(not same necessarily)” on p.7.
Key relation [A], [B] constant at equilibrium, with [B]/[A] fixed by K — not by 1 The equality that holds at equilibrium is between rates, and rates depend on both concentration and rate constant.
Solution
1. For A ⇌ B, equilibrium means kf[A] = kb[B].
2. Rearrange: [B]/[A] = kf/kb = K.
3. The concentrations are equal only in the special case K = 1, i.e. only if the two rate constants happen to coincide.
4. For a real system K is almost never 1. In the Haber process at 500 °C, NH3 is a small minority; in the H2 + Cl2 reaction HCl overwhelmingly dominates. Both are perfectly good equilibria.
5. So “concentrations become equal” is not a property of equilibrium at all — it is an accident of one particular K value.
6. The statement is therefore False; the correct word is constant.
 kf[A] = kb[B]  ->  [B]/[A] = kf/kb = K

 K = 1    : [A] = [B]   (a coincidence)
 K = 100  : [B] >> [A]  (still equilibrium)
 K = 0.01 : [A] >> [B]  (still equilibrium)
⚡ JEE Trick Swap-test every equilibrium statement: replace “equal” with “constant” and see whether it becomes true. Rates are equal; concentrations are constant. Confusing the two costs marks in almost every equilibrium paper.
Answer: False — concentrations become constant, not equal (they are equal only if K = 1).
2 (v)True or false: The properties of the system become constant at equilibrium and remain unchanged thereafter.Macroscopic constancyCharacteristics of Equilibria Involving Physical Processes🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Constancy of measurable properties is the operational definition of equilibrium: it is how you know equilibrium has arrived, since you cannot see the molecules. General characteristic (1) on p.10 states it directly.
Key relation at equilibrium: P, T, colour, density, concentration all constant with time The proviso is “so long as the conditions are not disturbed” — change T or volume and a new equilibrium takes over.
Solution
1. Once rf = rb, every concentration stops changing.
2. Every macroscopic property is a function of the concentrations and the temperature, so all of them stop changing too.
3. In the iodine sublimation experiment on p.9 the intensity of the violet colour rises and then “becomes constant” — that constancy is the observable signal of equilibrium.
4. The chapter’s own summary of the equilibrium state (p.8): “system loses its tendency for a change and all the properties… become constant and do not vary without external stimulation”.
5. The phrase “without external stimulation” is the qualification hidden inside “remain unchanged thereafter”: the constancy holds while the system is left alone.
6. Read that way, the statement is True.
 t < te : colour deepening, P rising, [A] falling
 t = te : rf = rb
 t > te : colour fixed, P fixed, [A] fixed
          (unchanged unless T or V is altered)
⚡ JEE Trick “Constant with time” is necessary but not sufficient on its own — a completed irreversible reaction also has constant properties. The extra ingredient is that both reactants and products are still present.
Answer: True — all measurable properties become constant and stay so unless the system is disturbed.
3 (i)Classify as physical or chemical equilibrium: H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l)Solid–liquid equilibriumEquilibria Involving Physical Processes - Equilibrium | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6🟡 SimilarPhysical equilibrium
Concept Apply the identity test: H2O on the left, H2O on the right. Only the phase label changed, from solid to liquid, so this is a phase change and the equilibrium is physical.
Key relation H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l)  at the melting point Solid–liquid equilibrium exists only at the melting point; above or below it one phase eats the other.
Solution
1. Compare formulas across the arrow: H2O and H2O. Same substance, so no chemical change has occurred.
2. Compare state symbols: (s) and (l). A phase change has occurred.
3. By the definition on p.9, equilibrium in a physical process is physical equilibrium. This is the chapter’s solid–liquid equilibrium, type (ii) of the five listed.
4. Dynamically: at 0 °C the rate at which water molecules leave the ice surface equals the rate at which they attach to it, so the masses of ice and water hold steady.
5. The property that becomes constant here is the melting point at constant pressure, as the table of physical equilibria on p.10 records.
6. Note it is also a heterogeneous equilibrium (two phases) — but the question asks for physical vs chemical, so the answer is physical equilibrium.

Concept NH4Cl(s) ⇌ NH4Cl(l) is the fusion of ammonium chloride. Both sides carry the same formula, so once again nothing chemical has happened — only the phase changed.
Key relation same formula both sides ⇒ physical equilibrium Do not be distracted by the fact that NH4Cl can also decompose — that is a different reaction, not this one.
Solution
1. Left side: NH4Cl(s). Right side: NH4Cl(l). Formula identical.
2. No bonds inside the ammonium or chloride species are broken or made; only the lattice forces holding them in the solid are overcome.
3. That is the very definition of melting, hence a physical process, hence physical equilibrium.
4. It is the solid–liquid type again, and like all solid–liquid equilibria it exists only at the melting point of the substance.
5. Do not confuse this with the equilibrium the chapter lists on p.9, NH4Cl(solid) ⇌ NH4Cl(vapour), which is a solid–vapour physical equilibrium — also physical.
6. And do not confuse either with NH4Cl(s) ⇌ NH3(g) + HCl(g), which is chemical because the formulas differ across the arrow.
7. Dynamically, at the melting point the rate at which ions leave the crystal lattice for the melt equals the rate at which they return to it, so the amounts of solid and liquid hold steady — the constant property being the melting point at fixed pressure.

3 (iii)Classify as physical or chemical equilibrium: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)Homogeneous chemical equilibriumHomogeneous and Heterogeneous Equilibrium - Chemical Equilibrium - Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarChemical equilibrium
Concept H2 and I2 go in, HI comes out: the formulas differ across the arrow, so new substances are formed. That makes it a chemical equilibrium — and since everything is a gas, a homogeneous one.
Key relation H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), all one phase This reaction is the textbook example of both labels at once: chemical and homogeneous.
Solution
1. Identity test: H2 and I2 on the left, HI on the right. Different substances ⇒ chemical change.
2. So the equilibrium is chemical equilibrium, the second branch of the tree on p.9.
3. H–H and I–I bonds break and H–I bonds form, which is exactly what “chemical” means.
4. It is reversible because HI decomposes back into H2 and I2 at the same temperature, so the mixture never contains HI alone.
5. Adding the second label: all three species are gases, so there is a single phase and the equilibrium is homogeneous — the chapter’s first example of homogeneous equilibrium on p.10.
6. Full description: a homogeneous chemical equilibrium. The answer asked for here is chemical equilibrium.
7. Watch the contrast with 3(iv): CaCO3 ⇌ CaO + CO2 is also chemical, but its species sit in three different phases, so it is heterogeneous. Same first label, different second label — which is why the two labels must be assigned separately.

Concept The question asks for a property that holds for all reversible reactions, so a single counter-example kills an option. The chapter lists four such universal properties on p.7; incompleteness is one of them.

Concept Three candidate systems, and the question is which one is genuinely closed and genuinely balanced. An open vessel losing vapour to the room can never reach equilibrium, however steady its temperature looks.
Key relation equilibrium needs a closed system + equal opposing rates Constant temperature by itself proves nothing — a steadily boiling pot is at constant temperature and is nowhere near equilibrium.
Solution
1. (a) Freezing of ice in an open vessel. “Freezing” describes a process in progress: heat is being removed and liquid is steadily turning to solid. A net change is occurring, so this is not equilibrium.
2. (c) Water boiling in an open vessel over a stove. The temperature is pinned at 100 °C, but steam escapes continuously into the room and heat pours in from below. Matter is leaving the system, so the condensation rate can never match the evaporation rate. Not equilibrium.
3. (b) A few drops of water with air inside a balloon at constant temperature. The balloon is sealed, so nothing escapes; the water evaporates until the vapour reaches its saturated pressure and then revap = rcond.
4. That is a proper liquid–vapour physical equilibrium in a closed system at constant temperature. Accept (b).
5. (d) “All the statements are correct” fails because (a) and (c) fail.
6. Hence option (b). Note the deliberate trap: every option mentions a constant temperature, so temperature is not the discriminator — the closed/open distinction is.
 (a) open, freezing in progress   net change   X
 (c) open, steam escaping         matter lost  X
 (b) sealed balloon, drops + air
     r(evap) = r(cond) at saturation           OK
⚡ JEE Trick Boiling water at a fixed 100 °C is the classic decoy for equilibrium. Constant temperature is a symptom of a phase transition, not proof of a balance — check whether the vessel is sealed first.
Answer: (b) — the sealed balloon is the only closed system in which the opposing rates can equalise.
3In the chemical reaction N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 at equilibrium point, state whether
(a) Equal volumes of N2 and H2 are reacting   (b) Equal masses of N2 and H2 are reacting   (c) The reaction has stopped   (d) The same amount of ammonia is formed as is decomposed into N2 and H2		Dynamic equilibrium in the Haber processIn a chemical reaction N_(2)+3H_(2) hArr 2NH_(3), at equilibrium point🟢 Exact(d) The same amount of ammonia is formed as is decomposed into N2 and H2
Concept The Haber equilibrium used to test the dynamic picture. At equilibrium the rate of NH3 formation equals the rate of NH3 decomposition, so the amount of ammonia stays put while both reactions run on.
Key relation N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3; at equilibrium rate of formation = rate of decomposition This is rf = rb expressed in terms of a single species, which is how the option is worded.
Solution
1. (a) “Equal volumes of N2 and H2 are reacting.” The equation demands 1 volume of N2 to 3 volumes of H2, never equal volumes. Reject.
2. (b) “Equal masses of N2 and H2 are reacting.” The mass ratio is 28 : 6 = 14 : 3, not 1 : 1. Reject.
3. (c) “The reaction has stopped.” The standard static misconception; the chapter insists both reactions continue at the molecular level. Reject.
4. (d) “The same amount of ammonia is formed as is decomposed.” Formation and decomposition proceeding at the same rate is exactly rf = rb, which is why [NH3] is constant. Accept.
5. Note how (d) explains the observation without claiming anything has halted — it is the dynamic statement, and it is the only one consistent with p.8.
6. Hence option (d).
 N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3

 volumes reacting : 1 : 3   (not equal)  -> (a) X
 masses reacting  : 28 : 6  (not equal)  -> (b) X
 reaction stopped : never                -> (c) X
 NH3 formed = NH3 decomposed             -> (d) OK
⚡ JEE Trick Options (a) and (b) can be killed by stoichiometry alone, without any equilibrium theory. Balance the equation first — in mixed questions it usually eliminates half the options for free.
Answer: (d) The same amount of ammonia is formed as is decomposed — the dynamic condition rf = rb.
4Identify the ‘trick’ element in this reaction classification: 3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) ⇌ Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)
(a) It is heterogeneous because it is a redox reaction   (b) It is homogeneous because the number of moles of gas is equal on both sides   (c) It is homogeneous because the solids ‘cancel out’ in the K expression   (d) It is heterogeneous because it contains both solid and gaseous phases		Basis of the homo/heterogeneous label2 Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Equilibrium | IIT Advanced | JEE Main | Chemistry Class 11🟡 Similar(d) It is heterogeneous because it contains both solid and gaseous phases
Concept The “trick” is that this reaction has equal moles of gas on both sides (4 H2O vs 4 H2), which tempts you to call it homogeneous. But homo/hetero is decided by phases, never by mole counts.
Key relation 3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) ⇌ Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g): 2 solids + 1 gas phase = 3 phases Δngas = 0 affects the Kp–Kc relation, not the phase classification.
Solution
1. (b) “Homogeneous because the number of moles of gas is equal on both sides.” This is the trap. Equal gas moles means Δng = 0, so Kp = Kc — a completely different fact. Reject.
2. (c) “Homogeneous because the solids cancel out in the K expression.” Solids are omitted from K because their activity is 1, but they are still physically present as separate phases. Omission from an expression is not disappearance from the vessel. Reject.
3. (a) “Heterogeneous because it is a redox reaction.” The conclusion is right but the reason is wrong — plenty of redox reactions are homogeneous, e.g. SO2(g) + NO2(g) ⇌ SO3(g) + NO(g). Reject.
4. (d) “Heterogeneous because it contains both solid and gaseous phases.” Right conclusion, right reason. Accept.
5. Phase count: Fe(s) is one, Fe3O4(s) is a second (two different solids never share a phase), and H2O(g) + H2(g) together are a third. Three phases.
6. The chapter prints this very reaction as its first example of heterogeneous equilibrium on p.10. Hence option (d).
 3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g) <=> Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g)

 gas moles : 4 = 4  -> dn(g) = 0  -> Kp = Kc
                       (NOT a phase statement)
 phases    : Fe(s), Fe3O4(s), gases = 3
             -> HETEROGENEOUS
⚡ JEE Trick “Equal moles of gas on both sides” is a Kp = Kc clue, and nothing else. Whenever it appears in a classification question it is planted as a distractor.
Answer: (d) It is heterogeneous because both solid and gaseous phases are present (three phases in all).
5In the reaction NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq), why is this considered a homogeneous equilibrium despite the ‘l’ and ‘aq’ symbols?
(a) Because water acts as a pure solvent and the system exists as a single liquid solution phase   (b) Because the concentration of water is ignored in the Kc expression   (c) Because all reactants are eventually converted to gases   (d) It is actually a heterogeneous equilibrium		Phase vs state symbolHomogeneous Equilibrium | Heterogeneous Equilibrium | Chemistry🟡 Similar(a) Because water acts as a pure solvent and the system exists as a single liquid solution phase
Concept State symbols and phases are not the same thing. “(aq)” means dissolved in water and “(l)” here means the solvent water itself — but the solvent and everything dissolved in it form one liquid phase.
Key relation solvent (l) + all solutes (aq) = one homogeneous liquid solution phase A phase is a physically distinct, uniform region; a solution is uniform throughout, so it counts once.
Solution
1. Species present: NH3 dissolved in water, water itself, NH4+ and OH− dissolved in water.
2. Every one of them is inside the same body of liquid. There is no interface anywhere in the flask, no second layer and no undissolved solid.
3. Therefore the phase count is one, and the equilibrium is homogeneous. That is exactly option (a). Accept.
4. (b) “Because the concentration of water is ignored in the Kc expression.” That omission happens because water is present in vast excess and its concentration is effectively constant — a separate point, and it does not create or remove a phase. Reject.
5. (c) “Because all reactants are eventually converted to gases.” Nothing here becomes a gas; ammonia stays in solution. Reject.
6. (d) “It is actually heterogeneous.” False, by the one-phase argument in steps 1–3. Hence option (a).
 NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)

 (aq) = dissolved in the water
 (l)  = the water itself
 -> ONE uniform liquid solution -> 1 phase
 -> HOMOGENEOUS
⚡ JEE Trick Count phases, not state symbols. A mixture of (aq) and (l) is one phase; a mixture of (s) and (aq), or (l) and (g), is two. This one rule settles every homo/hetero question in the chapter.
Answer: (a) — water is the solvent and everything sits in a single uniform liquid phase.
6Statement I: A net reaction can occur only if a system is not at equilibrium.
Statement II: All reactions occur to reach a state of equilibrium.
(a) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I   (b) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct but Statement II is not the correct explanation of Statement I   (c) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect   (d) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct		Assertion–reason on net reactionAssertion (A): A net reaction can occur only if a system is not at equilibrium.Reason (R): All r.…🟢 Exact(a) Both statements correct and II is the correct explanation of I
Concept An assertion–reason pair. Judge each statement independently first, then ask whether the second actually explains the first — not merely whether both happen to be true.
Key relation net rate = rf − rb; at equilibrium this is zero, so a net reaction requires rf ≠ rb A net reaction is by definition an imbalance, and equilibrium is by definition the absence of imbalance.
Solution
1. Statement I: “A net reaction can occur only if a system is not at equilibrium.” At equilibrium rf = rb, so the net rate is zero and no net change is possible. Away from equilibrium the rates differ and a net reaction proceeds. Statement I is correct.
2. Statement II: “All reactions occur to reach a state of equilibrium.” Reactions run in the direction that closes the gap between Q and K, and they stop changing only when equilibrium is reached. Equilibrium is the destination of every reversible process. Statement II is correct.
3. Now the explanation test. Statement II says equilibrium is the endpoint every reaction moves towards; that is precisely why net reaction happens only while the system is still short of it.
4. Put the other way: because equilibrium is the destination, being at the destination means there is nowhere left to go — no net reaction. II therefore explains I.
5. So both statements are correct and II is the correct explanation of I: option (a).
6. (b) fails because II does explain I; (c) and (d) each declare one of the two statements false, and both statements are true. Hence (a).
 not at equilibrium : rf =/= rb -> net reaction
 at equilibrium     : rf =  rb  -> no net change

 II: equilibrium is where every reaction is heading
 => that is exactly why I holds  -> option (a)
⚡ JEE Trick In assertion–reason questions the split between (a) and (b) is decided by one question: does the reason supply the mechanism behind the assertion, or is it just another true fact? Say the reason out loud beginning with “because…” and see if the assertion follows.
Answer: (a) Both statements are correct and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I.
7[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following statements are true for a system at equilibrium?
(a) Observable properties of the system remain constant   (b) Forward and backward reactions stop completely   (c) The system shows no net macroscopic change   (d) Opposing processes continue at the same rate		Properties of the equilibrium stateSelect all the true statements regarding chemical equilibrium🟡 Similar(a), (c), (d) observable properties constant; no net macroscopic change; opposing processes at equal rates
Concept A multiple-correct question with one planted falsehood. Statements about constancy and about continuing opposed activity are true; any statement that shuts the reactions down is false.
Key relation equilibrium: rf = rb ≠ 0 ⇒ observables constant, net change zero One condition generates all three correct options, which is why they stand or fall together.
Solution
1. (a) “Observable properties of the system remain constant.” General characteristic (1) on p.10 and the practical test for equilibrium. True.
2. (b) “Forward and backward reactions stop completely.” Denies the dynamic nature stated on p.8, where the reactions “continue to happen at the molecular level”. False.
3. (c) “The system shows no net macroscopic change.” Since rf − rb = 0, the net change is zero. Note the word net — it makes this compatible with continuing molecular activity. True.
4. (d) “Opposing processes continue at the same rate.” The definition of dynamic equilibrium itself. True.
5. Internal consistency check: (b) contradicts (d) outright, so at most one of them can be in the answer set. Since (d) is the chapter’s definition, (b) must be the false one.
6. Hence the correct set is (a), (c), (d).
 (a) observables constant       TRUE
 (b) reactions stop completely  FALSE
 (c) no NET macroscopic change  TRUE
 (d) opposing rates equal       TRUE

 note (c): "net" is what saves it
⚡ JEE Trick Watch for the word “net”. “No net change” is true at equilibrium; “no change” is false, because change is happening constantly in both directions. Examiners drop or keep that one word deliberately.
Answer: (a), (c) and (d); (b) is false because the opposing reactions never stop.
8[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following situation(s) can represent physical equilibrium?
(a) Ice and water coexisting at melting point in a closed system   (b) Water evaporating continuously in an open vessel   (c) Solid iodine and iodine vapour in a closed container   (d) Gas escaping continuously from a solution kept open		Identifying physical equilibriaGeneral Characteristics of Equilibria Involving Physical Process | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6🟡 Similar(a), (c) ice and water at the melting point in a closed system; solid iodine and its vapour in a closed container
Concept A physical equilibrium needs two things: a phase change with no chemical change, and a closed system in which the two opposing processes can balance. Two of the four options fail the second test outright.
Key relation same substance, two phases, closed vessel, equal opposing rates The word “continuously” in options (b) and (d) is the giveaway — a continuing one-way process is the opposite of a balance.
Solution
1. (a) Ice and water coexisting at the melting point in a closed system: H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l), the solid–liquid equilibrium listed on p.9. Melting and freezing occur at equal rates. True.
2. (b) Water evaporating continuously in an open vessel. Vapour escapes into the room, so condensation can never catch evaporation and the water eventually disappears. False.
3. (c) Solid iodine and iodine vapour in a closed container: I2(s) ⇌ I2(g), the solid–vapour equilibrium described on p.9, where the violet colour deepens and then stops changing. True.
4. (d) Gas escaping continuously from a solution kept open. Matter is leaving the system, so this is a one-way process, not an equilibrium. False.
5. Pattern to notice: the two true options say “closed”; the two false ones say “continuously” and “open”. Reading those three words answers the question.
6. Hence the correct set is (a) and (c).
 (a) closed, ice <=> water        equilibrium
 (c) closed, I2(s) <=> I2(g)      equilibrium
 (b) open, evaporating away        one-way  X
 (d) open, gas escaping            one-way  X
⚡ JEE Trick “Continuously” in the stem of an equilibrium option almost always signals a net one-way process. Equilibrium language is “coexisting”, “constant”, “in a closed container”.
Answer: (a) and (c) — the two closed systems in which a substance coexists with another phase of itself.
9[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following statements are correct about irreversible reactions?
(a) They proceed only in the forward direction   (b) They can be started from either reactant or product side   (c) They can proceed to completion   (d) They never attain equilibrium		Irreversible reactionsReversible and Irreversible Processes 🔴 Chemistry for Class 11🟡 Similar(a), (c), (d) proceed only in the forward direction; can proceed to completion; never attain equilibrium
Concept Straight recall of the four characteristics of irreversible reactions from p.7. Three of the options are on that list; the remaining one belongs to the reversible list and has been transplanted here.
Key relation Reactant → Product: no backward path ⇒ completion, no equilibrium Every true statement here is a consequence of the same fact: the backward reaction does not exist.
Solution
1. (a) “They proceed only in the forward direction.” Characteristic (a) on p.7, word for word. True.
2. (b) “They can be started from either reactant or product side.” This is characteristic (a) of reversible reactions. An irreversible reaction cannot be run backwards at all — charge NaCl and water into a flask and no NaOH and HCl appear. False.
3. (c) “They can proceed to completion.” Characteristic (b): the reactant is completely converted into product. True.
4. (d) “They never attain equilibrium.” Characteristic (d), and it follows from (a): with rb permanently zero, rf = rb can never be met while reactant remains. True.
5. Coherence check: (a), (c) and (d) all flow from the absence of a backward reaction, while (b) presupposes one — so (b) is the outsider.
6. Hence the correct set is (a), (c), (d).

10[One or more than one option may be correct] Which observation(s) support the dynamic nature of equilibrium?
(a) Observable properties of the system remain constant   (b) Forward and backward reactions continue simultaneously   (c) Reaction stops after equilibrium is reached   (d) Opposing processes occur at equal rates		Evidence for dynamic equilibriumChemistry Equilibrium part 5 (Dynamic nature of chemical equilibrium) CBSE class 11 XI🟡 Similar(b), (d) forward and backward reactions continue simultaneously; opposing processes occur at equal rates⚠
Concept The question asks for evidence of the dynamic nature specifically, not evidence of equilibrium in general. Only statements about continuing molecular activity qualify; statements about outward constancy describe the static-looking face.
Key relation dynamic ≡ both reactions still running, at equal rates Constancy of observables is necessary for equilibrium but is equally true of a finished irreversible reaction, so it cannot be evidence for dynamism.
Solution
1. (b) “Forward and backward reactions continue simultaneously.” This is the dynamic claim itself — the reactions have not stopped. Supports.
2. (d) “Opposing processes occur at equal rates.” Equal, non-zero rates are what let ceaseless activity coexist with an unchanging composition. Supports.
3. (c) “Reaction stops after equilibrium is reached.” This denies the dynamic nature outright. Does not support.
4. (a) “Observable properties of the system remain constant.” True at equilibrium, but it is exactly what makes equilibrium look static. Pour NaCl and water together after a completed neutralisation and its properties are constant too, with no dynamism at all. So constancy on its own is not evidence of a dynamic state. Does not support.
5. What would count as real evidence for (a)’s claim is the isotopic-labelling experiment — constancy plus scrambling of labelled atoms. Constancy alone is not enough.
6. Hence the observations supporting the dynamic nature are (b) and (d).
 evidence FOR dynamism:
   (b) both reactions still running      YES
   (d) equal, non-zero opposing rates    YES

 not evidence:
   (a) constant observables (looks static)
   (c) "reaction stops" (contradicts it)
⚡ JEE Trick Split every equilibrium statement into “macroscopic evidence” and “molecular evidence”. A question about the dynamic nature is asking for the molecular column only.
Answer: (b) and (d). ⚠ The key appears to read “abd”; option (a) describes the constancy of macroscopic properties, which is evidence of equilibrium but not of its dynamic character.
