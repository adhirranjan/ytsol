1A frame of reference moving with constant velocity is classified as: Accelerated / Non-inertial / Rotating / Inertial frameInertial frameA frame is inertial if it moves at constant velocity🟡 SimilardInertial frame
Concept A frame of reference is a coordinate system from which we measure motion. Newton's laws hold in their simple form F = ma only in frames that are not accelerating — these are called inertial frames. The test is entirely about the frame's acceleration, never about its speed: a frame can be racing along at 1000 m s⁻¹ and still be inertial, provided that velocity never changes in magnitude or direction. Since "constant velocity" means exactly dv⃗/dt = 0, the frame's acceleration is zero and the frame is inertial.
Formula Frame test: a⃗frame = dv⃗frame/dt in m s⁻². If a⃗frame = 0 → inertial (no pseudo force needed); if a⃗frame ≠ 0 → non-inertial (pseudo force F⃗pseudo = −m·a⃗0 must be added, in N). Note v⃗ is a vector, so "constant velocity" demands constant speed and constant direction.
Solution
1. Read the given condition: the frame moves with constant velocity, i.e. v⃗ = constant (m s⁻¹).This is the only datum in the question, so the whole answer must follow from it.
2. Differentiate to get the frame's acceleration: a⃗frame = dv⃗/dt = d(constant)/dt = 0 m s⁻².The derivative of any constant is zero — this is the decisive step.
3. Apply the definition: a frame with zero acceleration is an inertial frame.The lesson text states it directly: "a frame which moves with constant velocity is an inertial frame of reference."
4. Since a⃗0 = 0 m s⁻², the pseudo force is F⃗pseudo = −m(0) = 0 N — no fictitious force is needed, confirming the frame is inertial.
5. Eliminate the distractors: (a) "accelerated" needs a⃗ ≠ 0 ✗; (b) "non-inertial" is by definition an accelerating frame ✗; (c) "rotating" is always non-inertial because the direction of v⃗ keeps changing, so a⃗ ≠ 0 even at constant speed ✗.

⚡ JEE Trick Circle the word "acceleration", never "speed" or "motion". At rest and constant-velocity frames are both inertial and are physically indistinguishable — that is the whole content of Galilean relativity. Trap: a rotating frame is non-inertial even at constant angular speed, because v⃗ changes direction so a⃗ ≠ 0 — this is why centrifugal force (a pseudo force) exists.
Answer: (d) Inertial frame.
2Inclined plane moved right at 5 m/s²; force (N) that a 5 kg block exerts on the incline (all smooth, blocks move together)Force on accelerated inclineBlock on a smooth wedge accelerated horizontally (HCV)🟡 Similar55 N
Concept When a wedge is accelerated horizontally, the block riding on it is viewed most easily from the wedge's own (non-inertial) frame. In that frame we add a pseudo force −m a⃗0 pointing opposite to the wedge's acceleration, and then the block has no acceleration perpendicular to the incline surface — it neither sinks into the wedge nor lifts off it. That single perpendicular condition is enough to find the normal reaction N, and Newton's third law hands us the force the block exerts back on the incline.
Formula Perpendicular to a smooth incline of angle θ, on a wedge accelerating horizontally away from the slope face: N = m(g cosθ + a₀ sinθ), in N. mg cosθ (N) is the component of weight pressing into the surface; ma₀ sinθ (N) is the component of the pseudo force also pressing in — both add because here the pseudo force points into the slope face. Data: m = 5 kg, a₀ = 5 m s⁻², θ = 37°, g = 10 m s⁻², sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8.
Solution
1. Set up the geometry from the printed figure: the incline descends to the right with the 37° angle at the bottom-right, and the wedge is driven to the right at a₀ = 5 m s⁻².The outward normal to this face points up-and-to-the-right, so a leftward pseudo force has a component pressing the block INTO the surface.
2. Move into the wedge frame and add the pseudo force on the block: Fpseudo = m a₀ = 5 kg × 5 m s⁻² = 25 N, directed left (opposite to a⃗0).Units: kg × m s⁻² = N ✓.
3. List the forces perpendicular to the incline (taking "out of the surface" as positive): +N, −mg cosθ (weight component), −m a₀ sinθ (pseudo-force component).
4. Apply equilibrium perpendicular to the surface — the block stays on the face, so its perpendicular acceleration is 0 m s⁻²: N − mg cosθ − m a₀ sinθ = 0.
5. Substitute with units: N = 5(10 × 0.8) + 5(5 × 0.6) = 5(8) + 5(3) = 40 N + 15 N = 55 N.5 kg × 8 m s⁻² = 40 N from gravity; 5 kg × 3 m s⁻² = 15 N from the pseudo force.
6. Apply Newton's third law: the incline pushes the block with 55 N, so the block pushes the incline with 55 N, directed perpendicular into the slope face.
7. Dimensional check: [M][L][T]⁻² = N on both sides ✓. Sanity: with a₀ = 0 we would get N = mg cos37° = 40 N, so the acceleration correctly increases the contact force to 55 N.

Concept The chamber holding the incline is pushed horizontally with acceleration a, so the incline is a non-inertial frame. Work inside that frame and add a pseudo force m a on the block, directed opposite to a⃗ (horizontally backwards). The block then starts from rest on the slope and slides straight down it, so only its acceleration along the incline matters. Gravity and the pseudo force each have a down-slope component, and they simply add.
Formula Down-slope acceleration: a∥ = g sinθ + a cosθ (m s⁻²). g sinθ is gravity's component down the slope; a cosθ is the pseudo force resolved along the slope — the horizontal push also drives the block downward. From rest: L = ½ a∥ t² (m), since the block is initially at rest relative to the chamber.
Solution
1. Enter the chamber frame; the block feels gravity mg down and a pseudo force ma horizontal, pointing opposite to the chamber's acceleration.In a non-inertial frame, F = ma holds only after inserting the pseudo force −ma⃗.
2. Resolve along the incline (down-slope positive): gravity contributes g sinθ, the pseudo force contributes a cosθ.
3. Add: a∥ = g sinθ + a cosθ (m s⁻²), which is constant in time.
4. The block is initially at rest relative to the chamber, so use L = ½ a∥ t² with u = 0.
5. Solve for t: t² = 2L ÷ a∥ = 2L ÷ (g sinθ + a cosθ) ⇒ t = √(2L / (g sinθ + a cosθ)).
6. Limit check: a = 0 gives t = √(2L / g sinθ), the ordinary fixed-incline result ✓; larger a shortens t, as expected.

⚡ JEE Trick Resolve along the sliding direction only; the perpendicular equation just fixes N, which you never need here. Trap: students either forget the pseudo force (getting t = √(2L/g sinθ)) or resolve it as a sinθ — the horizontal push contributes cosθ along the slope, not sinθ.

4Two wooden blocks on a smooth surface; m stays stationary w.r.t. M — find force P (m on incline β of M)Force to hold block on wedgeTwo wooden blocks, m stationary relative to M — force P🟢 Exact(M + m)g tanβ
Concept A block m rests on the smooth incline face (angle β) of a wedge M, the whole assembly on a frictionless floor. A horizontal force P pushes the system so that m does not slide relative to M. That means m and M share one common horizontal acceleration a. For the small block, the only horizontal force available is the horizontal component of the normal reaction N from the incline — that component is exactly what must supply ma.
Formula For m on the smooth face: vertical N cosβ = mg, horizontal N sinβ = m a. The surface normal makes angle β with the vertical, so N splits into N cosβ (up) and N sinβ (horizontal). Dividing gives a = g tanβ; then for the whole system P = (M + m) a.
Solution
1. Since m stays fixed on M, treat both as one body with common horizontal acceleration a (no relative sliding).
2. Free-body the block m alone: forces are weight mg (down) and normal N (perpendicular to the incline). No friction, no applied force touches m directly.
3. Vertical balance (m has no vertical acceleration): N cosβ = mg.The block only accelerates horizontally, so vertical forces must cancel.
4. Horizontal equation: the horizontal component of N drives m ⇒ N sinβ = m a.
5. Divide the two: tanβ = a ÷ g ⇒ a = g tanβ — the acceleration that keeps m glued to the wedge.
6. Apply P to the full mass (M + m): P = (M + m) a = (M + m) g tanβ.

⚡ JEE Trick Solve the small block first; its two equations alone give a = g tanβ without ever touching M. Then P just accelerates the total mass. Trap: writing P = m g tanβ (forgetting M rides along too) or resolving N with the wrong angle — the normal tilts β from the vertical, so the horizontal part is N sinβ.
Answer: P = (M + m) g tanβ.

The cube's two faces supply a contact force R⃗ on the sphere; together with gravity that force must produce m a⃗. Find R⃗ from Newton's second law, then Newton's third law gives the equal-and-opposite force the sphere presses on the cube.

Concept A spherical cavity of radius R/2 is scooped from a solid sphere of radius R, with one end of the hole at the centre and the other touching the surface. Use the negative-mass (superposition) trick: remaining sphere = full solid sphere + a sphere of the cavity's size carrying negative mass, both uniform. The full sphere's CM is at the geometric centre; the cavity's centre lies halfway out along a radius. Combine to locate the CM of what is left.
Formula xcm = (M·0 − m·d) / (M − m). M = full mass at x = 0, m = cavity mass (removed) at x = d; masses go as radius³ for uniform density. Cavity radius R/2 ⇒ m = M(R/2)³/R³ = M/8; its centre sits at d = R/2 from the main centre.
Solution
1. Geometry of the hole: it spans from the centre (r = 0) to the surface (r = R) along one radius, so its diameter is R and its centre is at d = R/2.
2. Mass scaling (uniform density): cavity mass m = M × (R/2)³ ÷ R³ = M/8; remaining mass = M − M/8 = 7M/8.
3. Put the origin at the sphere's centre, +x toward the cavity. Full sphere CM at x = 0; removed piece at x = +R/2.
4. Superpose with negative mass: xcm = [M(0) − (M/8)(R/2)] ÷ (7M/8).
5. Simplify: xcm = −(M R/16) ÷ (7M/8) = −(R/16)(8/7) = −R/14.The minus sign means the CM shifts away from the hole, into the solid part.
6. Result: the CM lies at a distance R/14 from the centre, on the side opposite the cavity.

Concept A ladder leaning against a wall is a rigid body in static equilibrium, so three conditions must all hold at once: net horizontal force = 0, net vertical force = 0, and net torque about any point = 0. The wall is smooth, so it can only push horizontally (no friction there); the ground is rough, so it supplies both a normal force and the friction that stops the base sliding out. As the monkey climbs higher, the torque it applies grows, forcing the wall reaction — and therefore the required friction — to grow with it. The ladder slips at the exact height where the required friction reaches its cap.
Formula Equilibrium: ΣFx = 0 → f = Nwall (N); ΣFy = 0 → Nground = (M + m)g (N); Στ = 0 (N m) about the base. Friction cap: fmax = ½ Nground. Data: ladder L = 2 m, M = 10 kg; base 1.2 m from the wall; monkey m = 20 kg; g = 9.8 m s⁻².
Solution
1. Fix the geometry by Pythagoras — height reached on the wall: h = √(L² − b²) = √(2² − 1.2²) = √(4 − 1.44) = √2.56 = 1.6 m.The ladder, the wall and the ground form a 1.2–1.6–2.0 m right triangle (a scaled 3-4-5).
2. Vertical forces: only the ground pushes up, and it must carry both weights. Nground = (10 + 20) kg × 9.8 m s⁻² = 294 N.The smooth wall is horizontal-only, so it contributes nothing vertically.
3. Cap the friction: fmax = ½ × 294 N = 147 N.Given: the ground offers a maximum horizontal resistive force half the normal reaction.
4. Horizontal forces: the only two are the wall's push Nwall (away from the wall) and friction f (toward the wall), so f = Nwall. At the verge of slipping f = fmax, hence Nwall = 147 N — this answers part (i).
5. For part (ii) take torques about the base (this kills the two unknown ground forces, which have zero moment arm there). Horizontal lever arms scale as 1.2/2 = 0.6 per metre climbed along the ladder, so a monkey x m up the ladder sits 0.6x m horizontally from the base; the ladder's own centre of mass (at 1 m along) sits 0.6 m out.
6. Write Στ = 0 about the base — the wall's push (147 N at height 1.6 m) turns the ladder one way, the two weights the other:
  Nwall × 1.6 = Mg × 0.6 + mg × 0.6x
7. Substitute (take g = 9.8 as a common factor: Nwall = 15g, Mg = 10g, mg = 20g):
  15g × 1.6 = 10g × 0.6 + 20g × 0.6x → 24 = 6 + 12xg cancels from every term, so the answer is independent of g.
8. Solve: 12x = 18 → x = 1.5 m along the ladder.Units: all terms are N m ÷ N = m ✓.
9. Sanity check: 1.5 m < 2 m, so the monkey slips before reaching the top — physically sensible for a ladder this close to vertical-limit friction.

3Plank 2 m, 20 kg, pivoted 0.5 m from each end; 40 kg person walks toward the farther end. How far before it topples?Toppling / torque about pivotPlank on supports — torque & tipping analysis🟡 Similar—
Concept A plank resting on two pivots topples the instant the supports can no longer hold it down. While the person stays between the two pivots, both supports simply push up and nothing rotates. Once the person walks past the far pivot, that pivot becomes a fulcrum: the person's weight on the overhanging side tries to rotate the plank about it, while the plank's own weight (acting at its centre, on the near side) resists. Toppling begins the moment the person's torque exceeds the plank's restoring torque — equivalently, the moment the near support's reaction would have to turn negative (pull down), which a simple pivot cannot do.
Formula Tipping condition about the far pivot: mperson g · d = Mplank g · s, in N m, where d (m) is the person's overhang beyond the far pivot and s (m) is the distance from that pivot to the plank's centre of mass. g appears on both sides and cancels, so the tipping point depends only on the mass ratio and the geometry. Data: plank L = 2 m, M = 20 kg, pivots 0.5 m from each end; person m = 40 kg.
Solution
1. Lay out coordinates from the left end: pivot P₁ at 0.5 m, pivot P₂ at 2 − 0.5 = 1.5 m, plank centre of mass (uniform plank) at L/2 = 1.0 m.
2. Identify the motion: the person starts at P₁ (0.5 m) and walks toward the farther end (the right end, at 2.0 m)."Farther end" from P₁ is the right-hand end, so P₂ is the pivot he will pass.
3. Identify the fulcrum: the plank can only tip about P₂ (at 1.5 m), lifting the left-hand part off P₁.Only once he is beyond P₂ does his weight act on the overhanging side and produce a tipping torque.
4. Find the plank's restoring lever arm about P₂: s = 1.5 m − 1.0 m = 0.5 m (its centre of mass lies on the far side of P₂ from the person).
5. Let d (m) be the person's distance beyond P₂. Balance torques about P₂ at the verge of toppling:
  40 kg × g × d = 20 kg × g × 0.5 m
6. Cancel g and solve: 40 d = 10 → d = 0.25 m beyond P₂.Units: kg·m on both sides after g cancels ✓. The person is twice as heavy as the plank, so his overhang need only be a quarter of the plank's 0.5 m arm.
7. Convert to distance walked from his starting point at P₁: distance = (1.5 m + 0.25 m) − 0.5 m = 1.25 m.
8. Check it is physically reachable: he stops at 1.75 m from the left end, which is still on the plank (length 2 m) ✓ — so the plank really does topple before he runs out of plank.

Concept "Measured weight" is the reading of the scale/seat, i.e. the normal reaction N pushing up on the person — not the true gravitational pull mg. When the lift accelerates upward with a, the floor must both support the person's weight and supply the extra upward force to accelerate them, so N exceeds mg. The person feels heavier; that felt heaviness is the apparent weight.
Formula Newton on the person (up positive): N − mg = m a ⇒ N = m(g + a) (N). N is the apparent (measured) weight; mg is the true weight; the term ma is the extra push needed for upward acceleration. Since a > 0 upward, N > mg always.
Solution
1. Identify the forces on the person: true weight mg down, normal reaction N up from the seat/floor.
2. The lift (and person) accelerate upward with a, so the net upward force is ma: N − mg = m a.Take up as positive; the unbalanced force points along the acceleration.
3. Solve for the reading: N = m(g + a).
4. Compare with the true weight mg: N = mg + ma, and ma > 0, so N > mg — the scale reads more than the actual weight.
5. Eliminate options: "less" needs downward acceleration ✗; "equal" needs a = 0 (rest or constant velocity) ✗; "none" is wrong since a definite value m(g+a) exists ✗.
