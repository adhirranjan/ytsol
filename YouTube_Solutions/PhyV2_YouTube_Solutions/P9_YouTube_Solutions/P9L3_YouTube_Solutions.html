Concept "Perfectly inelastic" is the extreme end of the inelastic family: the two colliding bodies deform permanently and never separate again, so after impact they share one common velocity. Momentum is still conserved (no external horizontal force acts during the brief impact), but kinetic energy is not — the missing energy goes into heat, sound and permanent deformation of the bodies.
Formula m1v1 + m2v2 = (m1+m2)v' — masses in kg, velocities in m/s, v' the single common velocity after impact. e = 0 for this class of collision. kg·m/s on both sides so momentum balances; the "stick" condition is exactly what forces v1'=v2'=v'.
Solution
1. Option (a) "bounce apart with the same speed" describes a perfectly elastic collision (e=1), the opposite extreme — rejected.Bouncing apart at all contradicts "perfectly inelastic", which by definition ends with zero relative velocity.
2. Option (b) "move in opposite directions" cannot happen once two bodies are stuck together — a single combined mass has only one velocity, one direction, at one instant.
3. Option (d) "retain all kinetic energy" is also an elastic-collision signature; a perfectly inelastic collision loses the maximum KE consistent with momentum conservation, never zero KE loss.The lost KE equals ½·(m1m2/(m1+m2))·(v1−v2)² — always positive unless v1=v2 already.
4. Option (c): "stick together and move with the same velocity" matches the definition exactly — the combined mass (m1+m2) moves with one shared v'.

Concept This is the routine engine of every perfectly-inelastic problem: the two masses fuse into one, so a single momentum equation with a single unknown (v') solves it completely. No energy equation is needed — in fact using one would be wrong, since KE is not conserved here.
Formula v' = (m1v1 + m2v2)/(m1+m2) — masses in kg, velocities in m/s, v' in m/s. kg·(m/s) ÷ kg = m/s on both sides ✓, so v' is a genuine velocity, not a momentum.
Solution

5A sphere of mass 'm' collides against a pendulum of mass 'm' with horizontal velocity v₀. (i) Calculate the maximum height reached by the pendulum if the masses scatter elastically along the line of initial motion. (ii) What is the maximum height reached by the pendulum if the two masses stick together? — (Level-5)Elastic vs inelastic → max heightBallistic Pendulum Physics Problems - Conservation of Momentum & Energy - Inelastic Collisions🟡 Similar(i) v₀²/2g  ·  (ii) v₀²/8g
Concept This question is a two-in-one comparison of the two extreme collision types with identical masses. Part (i) is the special elastic case m1=m2 (theory page), where the striker stops dead and the target inherits the entire velocity. Part (ii) is the perfectly-inelastic case (e=0): both masses share the velocity, halved. Both then convert their post-collision KE into gravitational PE at the top of the swing.
Formula Elastic, equal masses, target at rest: v1'=0, v2'=v0. Perfectly inelastic: v' = (m·v0+m·0)/(2m) = v0/2. Energy–height conversion for the pendulum bob: ½mv² = mgh ⇒ h = v²/(2g) — v in m/s, g in m/s², h in m. (m/s)²÷(m/s²) = m ✓, a length, as h must be.
Solution
1. (i) Elastic case. For equal masses colliding elastically with the target at rest, the theory page's special-case formulas give v1' = 0 (sphere stops dead) and v2' = v0 (pendulum inherits the full incoming velocity).This full velocity-exchange between equal masses is the textbook "Newton's cradle" signature.
2. Pendulum then rises converting all its KE to PE: ½mv0² = mgh₁ ⇒ h₁ = v0²/(2g).
3. (ii) Perfectly inelastic case. The sphere embeds in (sticks to) the pendulum: v' = (m·v0 + m·0)/(m+m) = v0/2.Half the speed, because the momentum m·v0 is now shared by double the mass.
4. Combined mass 2m rises using its post-collision KE: ½(2m)(v0/2)² = (2m)gh₂ ⇒ mv0²/4 = 2mgh₂.
5. Solve: h₂ = v0²/(8g).Notice h₂ is exactly ¼ of h₁ — sticking together costs a factor of 4 in height, even though it only halved the speed, because height depends on v².
6. Physical read: the elastic case is "lossless" for the pendulum (it gets the sphere's full KE), while the perfectly-inelastic case throws away half the system's KE as heat/deformation in the very act of sticking, on top of the momentum-sharing penalty.

Concept The single fact that holds across every collision — elastic, partially inelastic, or perfectly inelastic — is Newton's third law applied over the brief contact time: whatever momentum one body gains, the other loses exactly. External forces (gravity, friction) are negligible during the instant of impact, so the system's total momentum is unchanged. 3. Option (d) temperature: rises during inelastic collisions (lost KE becomes heat) and stays essentially unchanged in elastic ones — clearly not a conserved quantity in general. Rejected.

1A body is dropped from a height of 80 m onto a horizontal floor and bounces repeatedly. If it stops in t seconds, find the value of t (in seconds). (g = 10 ms⁻², e = 0.5) — (Level-5)Total time to stop bouncing (infinite series)Ball dropped from 2 m height bounce 80 percent Find distance travelled till it stops MCR3U🟡 Similart = 12 s
Concept A bouncing ball technically bounces infinitely many times as e<1, yet the total time to "stop" is finite — each successive bounce's up-down time shrinks by a factor e, forming a convergent geometric series that sums in closed form. The trick is recognising the total time as t1 + 2(e·t1 + e²·t1 + ...), i.e. the first fall plus twice the sum of a geometric series of subsequent half-bounce times.
Formula First fall time: t1 = √(2h/g) (s). Total time (standard closed form): T = t1[(1+e)/(1−e)] (s). Both t1 and T are seconds; the bracket (1+e)/(1−e) is dimensionless, so multiplying preserves units — and the bracket is always ≥1 for 0≤e<1, correctly making T longer than the first fall alone.
Solution
1. First fall time: t1 = √(2×80/10) = √16 = 4 s.
2. After the first impact, the ball rebounds at et1's corresponding speed and executes a full up-down cycle of duration 2e·t1 before the next impact; after the 2nd impact, 2e²t1; and so on — an infinite geometric series with first term 2e·t1 and ratio e.
3. Sum of the (infinite) series of post-first-impact bounce durations: 2t1(e+e²+e³+...) = 2t1 × e/(1−e).The geometric series e+e²+e³+... sums to e/(1−e) for 0
4. Total time: T = t1 + 2t1·e/(1−e) = t1[1 + 2e/(1−e)] = t1[(1−e+2e)/(1−e)] = t1[(1+e)/(1−e)].
5. Substitute: T = 4 × [(1+0.5)/(1−0.5)] = 4 × [1.5/0.5] = 4 × 3 = 12 s.
6. Sanity check on the multiplier: (1+e)/(1−e) at e=0.5 gives exactly 3 — a clean number, and physically sensible since a moderately bouncy ball (e=0.5) should take a few multiples of its first-fall time to fully settle, not an enormous or vanishingly small factor.
   t1=4s(fall) → 2e·t1=4s(bounce1) → 2e²t1=2s(bounce2) → 2e³t1=1s → ...

   T = t1[(1+e)/(1−e)] = 4×(1.5/0.5) = 4×3 = 12 s
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the closed-form total-stopping-time formula T = t1(1+e)/(1−e) directly — deriving the geometric series from scratch each time wastes exam minutes. Trap: forgetting the factor of 2 in the bounce-cycle terms (each bounce goes up AND comes back down) is the most common setup error.
Answer: t = 12 s.

Concept An oblique impact with the ground is handled by splitting the velocity into components: the component perpendicular to the ground (normal) obeys the restitution law exactly like a vertical drop, while the component parallel to the ground (tangential) is completely unaffected by the impact (no friction is mentioned, so nothing changes it). This split-and-recombine method is the standard tool for every "angle of incidence" bounce question.
Formula Incoming speed u at 45° to the normal: normal component u cos45° = u/√2, tangential component u sin45° = u/√2. After impact: normal component becomes e·u cos45°, tangential stays u sin45°. Both components carry units of m/s; recombining them via Pythagoras keeps the final speed in m/s too.
Solution
1. Resolve the incoming velocity u at 45° to the normal (vertical): normal (into ground) component = u cos45° = u/√2; tangential (along ground) component = u sin45° = u/√2.At exactly 45° the two components happen to be numerically equal, which is a special feature of this particular angle — not generally true.
2. After impact, the normal component reverses and shrinks by the restitution factor: v_normal' = e × (u/√2) = (1/√2)(u/√2) = u/2 (now pointing away from the ground).

Concept Since the target (deuteron) starts at rest, ALL of the system's kinetic energy initially belongs to the neutron — so the neutron's "fractional energy loss" is exactly equal to the "fraction of KE transferred to the target" in an elastic collision. This is a standard nuclear-physics formula worth memorising directly: it drives moderator design in nuclear reactors (light nuclei like deuterium/hydrogen slow neutrons far more efficiently per collision than heavy nuclei).
Formula Fraction of KE transferred to a stationary target in an elastic collision: f = 4m1m2/(m1+m2)² — masses in any consistent unit (ratios only matter), f dimensionless. Take m1 (neutron) = 1 unit, m2 (deuteron) = 2 units (a deuteron is a proton+neutron, so ≈2× a neutron's mass). f is a pure ratio of mass² terms over mass² terms, hence dimensionless — exactly what a "fraction" must be.
Solution
1. Assign relative masses: neutron m1 = 1 (atomic mass units), deuteron m2 = 2 (a deuteron nucleus has one proton + one neutron, so ≈2 u).
2. Apply the energy-transfer-fraction formula: f = 4m1m2/(m1+m2)² = 4(1)(2)/(1+2)² = 8/9.
3. Since the deuteron starts completely at rest, the total initial KE of the system is entirely the neutron's KE — so the KE gained by the deuteron equals exactly the KE lost by the neutron (energy conservation in an elastic collision, no energy is destroyed, only redistributed).
4. 5. 9A stationary particle explodes into two particles of masses m₁ and m₂ which move in opposite directions with velocities v₁ and v₂. The ratio of their kinetic energies E₁/E₂ is: (a) m₂/m₁ (b) m₁/m₂ (c) 1 (d) m₁v₂/m₂v₁ — (Level-5)Explosion — momentum conservation, KE ratioA stationary particle explodes into two particles of masses m1 and m2 which move in opposite🟢 Exactam₂/m₁
Concept An explosion is the mirror image of a perfectly inelastic collision run backward in time: a single stationary object splits into pieces, and momentum conservation still applies (the internal explosive force is an internal force, exerting no net external impulse on the system). Since the total momentum starts at zero, the two fragments must carry exactly equal and opposite momenta — that single constraint is enough to find the KE ratio without knowing any actual numbers.
Formula Momentum conservation (initial momentum zero): m1v1 = m2v2 (magnitudes, opposite directions). Kinetic energy in terms of momentum: E = p²/(2m) — p in kg·m/s, m in kg, E in J. Since both fragments share the identical momentum magnitude p, expressing E as p²/(2m) makes the mass-dependence explicit and the ratio trivial to take.
Solution
1. Before the explosion, the particle is stationary: total momentum = 0.
2. After exploding into two pieces moving in opposite directions, momentum conservation demands the vector sum still be zero: m1v1 (one direction) must exactly cancel m2v2 (opposite direction) in magnitude ⇒ m1v1 = m2v2 = p (say).
3. Write each particle's KE using the momentum form: E1 = p²/(2m1), E2 = p²/(2m2) — both share the same p, since their momenta are equal in magnitude.This is the whole trick: instead of KE = ½mv² (which needs both m and v separately for each particle), writing KE = p²/2m lets the common p cancel cleanly in the ratio step.
4. Take the ratio: E1/E2 = [p²/(2m1)] / [p²/(2m2)] = m2/m1.
5. So the lighter fragment ends up with the larger share of the kinetic energy — an inverse relationship with mass, since equal momentum p split by a smaller mass gives a proportionally larger v², and KE grows with v² while only shrinking linearly with m.

10n elastic balls are placed at rest on a smooth horizontal plane which is circular at the ends with radius 'r' as shown in the figure. The masses of the balls are m, m/2, m/2², ..., m/2ⁿ⁻¹ respectively. What is the minimum velocity which should be imparted to the first ball of mass m such that the nᵗʰ ball completes the vertical circle? (a) (3/4)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr (b) (4/3)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr (c) (3/2)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr (d) (2/3)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr — (Level-5)Chain of elastic collisions, velocity amplification`n` elastic balls are placed at rest on a smooth horizontal plane which is circular at the ends ...🟢 Exacta(3/4)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr
Concept Each ball is exactly half the mass of the one before it, and each elastic collision between a moving ball and its (half-mass) stationary neighbour amplifies velocity by a fixed factor — repeating this n−1 times (from ball 1 to ball n) gives a geometric progression of velocities. The final condition, "completes the vertical circle", pins down the minimum required speed of the last ball via the standard vertical-circular-motion result — then the chain is walked backward to find what the first ball needs.
Formula Elastic collision, target exactly half the striker's mass (m2 = m1/2): v2' = [2m1/(m1+m2)]v1 = [2m1/(1.5m1)]v1 = (4/3)v1 — a fixed amplification factor of 4/3 per collision. Minimum speed at the bottom of a vertical circular loop of radius r: v_min = √(5gr) (m/s). Both formulas are standard, previously-derived results reused here rather than re-derived — the elastic-collision fraction is dimensionless, and √(5gr) has units √(m/s²·m)=m/s ✓.
Solution
1. Consider any one collision in the chain: ball i (mass m_i) strikes stationary ball i+1 (mass m_i/2, exactly half). Using the elastic-collision target-velocity formula: v_(i+1) = [2m_i/(m_i+m_i/2)]v_i = [2/(1.5)]v_i = (4/3)v_i.
2. This 4/3 amplification is identical for every consecutive pair in the chain, since the mass ratio (each ball exactly half the previous) never changes.
3. After n−1 such collisions (ball 1 → ball 2 → ... → ball n), the nth ball's velocity is: v_n = (4/3)ⁿ⁻¹ × v1, where v1 is the velocity imparted to the first ball.The exponent is n−1, not n, because there are exactly n−1 collisions needed to pass the "impulse" from ball 1 all the way to ball n.
4. The nth ball must "complete the vertical circle" of radius r — the minimum speed for this at the bottom of the loop is the standard result v_n = √(5gr) (derived from requiring the string/track tension/normal force stay ≥0 at the top, where mg alone must supply the centripetal force).
5. Set the two expressions for v_n equal: (4/3)ⁿ⁻¹ v1 = √(5gr).
6. Solve for v1: v1 = √(5gr) / (4/3)ⁿ⁻¹ = (3/4)ⁿ⁻¹ √(5gr).Since 3/4 < 1, this factor shrinks as n grows — meaning a longer chain of balls needs a much smaller initial velocity to eventually deliver √(5gr) to the far end, because each collision amplifies the speed further along the chain.
   m→v1   m/2   m/4   ...   m/2ⁿ⁻¹(needs √5gr to loop)
     ●     ○     ○            ○──▭(loop, radius r)

   each collision: v_(i+1) = (4/3)v_i   (mass halves each step)
   v_n = (4/3)ⁿ⁻¹ v1 = √5gr
   ⇒  v1 = (3/4)ⁿ⁻¹ √5gr
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a chain of elastic collisions has a constant mass ratio between consecutive bodies, the velocity amplification factor is the same at every step — turning what looks like an n-step problem into a single geometric-progression formula. Trap: mixing up whether the exponent should be n or n−1 (there are always one fewer collisions than balls); and confusing which of √(5gr) or √(gr) applies (√5gr is for a full vertical circle, √gr is only for the top-point minimum condition alone).
Answer: (a) (3/4)ⁿ⁻¹√5gr.
