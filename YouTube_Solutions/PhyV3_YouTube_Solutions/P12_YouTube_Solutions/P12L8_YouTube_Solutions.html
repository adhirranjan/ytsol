1A cylinder rotating about its centre of mass with angular velocity ω0 is placed over a rough horizontal surface, as shown in the figure. State true or false against each of the following statements: (i) friction force will act in forward direction; (ii) net work done by friction is negative; (iii) total kinetic energy of the system is reduced — (Level-1)Spin dropped on rough floorRM-1 — How to find direction of friction in rolling / slipping🟡 Similar(i) True · (ii) True · (iii) True
Concept The cylinder is dropped spinning but with zero centre-of-mass velocity, so the material point at the contact is not at rest — it is skidding. For a clockwise spin the contact point sweeps backwards over the floor, and kinetic friction always opposes that relative sliding, so friction on the cylinder points forwards. That forward friction does two jobs at once: it pushes the centre of mass forward and its torque about the centre spins the body down, until v = Rω and the skidding stops. Because the two contacting surfaces slide past each other while a force acts between them, mechanical energy is destroyed at the interface.
Formula Lcontact = Icmω0 = (Icm + MR²)ωf. Friction acts at the contact line, so its torque about that line is zero and angular momentum there is conserved. Wfric = ΔKE. Friction is the only horizontal force, so the change in total KE is exactly its net work.
Solution
1. Contact point velocity before friction acts: vP = 0 + ω0R backwards, so the surface under the cylinder slips backwards. Sliding backwards ⇒ friction forwards on the cylinder: statement (i) is True.
2. Forward friction f gives a = f/M forwards while its torque −fR slows the spin, so v grows and ω falls until v = Rω.
3. Angular momentum about the contact line: ½MR²ω0 = (½MR² + MR²)ωf ⇒ ωf = ω0/3.
4. KEi = ½(½MR²)ω0² = MR²ω0²/4; KEf = ½(3/2)MR²(ω0/3)² = MR²ω0²/12.
5. ΔKE = MR²ω0²(1/12 − 1/4) = −MR²ω0²/6, i.e. two thirds of the spin energy is lost, so (iii) is True.
6. Since Wfric = ΔKE < 0, the net work done by friction is negative: (ii) is True. The forward push does positive work on the centre of mass, but the retarding torque does more negative rotational work.
7. All three statements therefore read True.

2A cylinder moving with velocity v0 (without spin) is placed over a rough horizontal surface as shown in the figure. State true or false against each of the following statements: (i) frictional force will act in backward direction; (ii) net work done by friction is zero; (iii) kinetic energy of system is reduced — (Level-2)Sliding cylinder becomes rollingRotational Motion L-10 — Friction in pure rolling motion🟡 Similar(i) True · (ii) False · (iii) True
Concept This is the mirror image of the previous problem: the cylinder is launched translating with no spin, so its contact point slides forwards over the floor at v0. Kinetic friction opposes that sliding, so it acts backwards on the cylinder. The backward force decelerates the centre while its torque about the centre spins the cylinder up, and the two meet when v = Rω. As long as a real slip exists at the contact the interface dissipates energy, so the total kinetic energy must fall and the net work of friction cannot be zero.
Formula Mv0R = (Icm + MR²)ωf. Friction passes through the contact line, so angular momentum about that line is unchanged from launch to pure rolling. KE = ½Mv² + ½Icmω². Combined motion always splits into a translational and a spin term about the centre of mass.
Solution
1. Contact point velocity at launch = v0 forwards (no spin to cancel it), so the cylinder skids forwards and friction acts backwards: (i) is True.
2. Angular momentum about the contact line: Mv0R = (½MR² + MR²)ωf = (3/2)MR²ωf ⇒ ωf = 2v0/3R.
3. Final centre speed vf = Rωf = 2v0/3, so the cylinder keeps two thirds of its original speed. A hollow cylinder, with larger I, would keep only half.
4. KEi = ½Mv0²; KEf = ½M(2v0/3)² + ½(½MR²)(2v0/3R)² = 2Mv0²/9 + Mv0²/9 = Mv0²/3.
5. ΔKE = Mv0²/3 − Mv0²/2 = −Mv0²/6, one third of the launch energy is dissipated, so (iii) is True.
6. Friction is the only horizontal force, so Wfric = ΔKE = −Mv0²/6 ≠ 0: statement (ii) is False. Only after pure rolling begins does static friction vanish and do zero work.
7. Reading down: (i) True, (ii) False, (iii) True.

3A wheel rolls without slipping over a rough surface and its centre moves with constant acceleration ‘a’. Find the acceleration of the given points O, P, Q and S when the velocity of the centre of the wheel is ‘v’. (O is the centre, Q the topmost point, P the contact point and S the rightmost point) — (Level-3)Acceleration of points on a rolling wheelRotational Motion L-11 — Pure rolling motion advanced questions🟡 SimilaraO = aî; aP = ω²Rĵ; aQ = 2aî − ω²Rĵ; aS = (a − ω²R)î − aĵ
Concept Combined motion is handled by superposing three pieces on every material point: the acceleration of the centre of mass, the tangential piece α×r due to the changing spin, and the centripetal piece −ω²r that any point on a rotating body carries. Pure rolling locks the spin to the translation twice over — v = ωR and a = αR — so both extra pieces have magnitudes fixed by a and v. Notice that a point of a rolling wheel is never at rest in acceleration, even the contact point, whose velocity is zero.
Formula a⃗P = a⃗cm + α⃗×r⃗ − ω²r⃗. Rigid-body acceleration relative to the centre of mass has a tangential and a radial part. ω = v/R, α = a/R. The rolling constraint and its time derivative.
Solution
1. Take î along a⃗ and ĵ up. The wheel rolls to the right, so ω⃗ = −(v/R)k̂ and α⃗ = −(a/R)k̂.
2. Point O is the centre, so r⃗ = 0 and aO = a⃗cm = aî. Only the centre feels the bare translational acceleration.
3. Point P (contact), r⃗ = −Rĵ: α⃗×r⃗ = −(a/R)k̂×(−Rĵ) = −aî, and −ω²r⃗ = +ω²Rĵ, so aP = aî − aî + ω²Rĵ = ω²Rĵ = (v²/R)ĵ.
4. Point Q (top), r⃗ = +Rĵ: α⃗×r⃗ = +aî and −ω²r⃗ = −ω²Rĵ, so aQ = 2aî + ω²R(−ĵ). The top point accelerates forward at twice the centre — the same doubling as its speed 2v.
5. Point S (rightmost), r⃗ = +Rî: α⃗×r⃗ = −(a/R)k̂×(Rî) = −aĵ and −ω²r⃗ = −ω²Rî, so aS = (a − ω²R)î + a(−ĵ).
6. Check the contact point: its tangential term exactly cancels a⃗cm, leaving only the centripetal ω²R pointing at the centre — straight up. Zero velocity, non-zero acceleration: that is why the contact point instantly leaves the ground direction.
7. Substituting ω = v/R gives all four answers in terms of the printed data a, v and R.

Zero velocity at an instant says nothing about acceleration; the contact point is momentarily at rest but is being whipped upward at v²/R. The second trap is forgetting the centripetal term for Q and S because "the centre is not accelerating sideways" — every off-centre point carries ω²r regardless.

4A disc rolls without slipping on a horizontal surface. A point on the disc at a distance R/2 from the centre moves in a curved path. The radius of curvature at the highest point of the path is (a) 4R (b) R/2 (c) 3R/2 (d) 4.5 R — (Level-4)Radius of curvature of a curtate cycloidJEE Advanced — Cycloid analysis and its radius of curvature🟡 Similard4.5 R
Concept An interior point of a rolling disc traces a curtate cycloid: a wavy curve whose highest point occurs when the point sits directly above the centre. There the velocity is purely horizontal and the acceleration is purely vertical, so the acceleration is entirely normal to the path — exactly the situation in which ρ = v²/an can be read off in one line. Because the centre moves at constant speed, the only acceleration any point has is its own centripetal term about the centre, ω²d.
Formula ρ = vP²/a⊥. Definition of radius of curvature: the component of acceleration perpendicular to the velocity bends the path. vP = v + ωd, aP = ω²d. At the top of the point’s own circle the rotational velocity adds to the translation, and with constant ω only the centripetal term survives.
Solution
1. Write d = R/2 for the point’s distance from the centre and use the rolling constraint v = ωR, so ω = v/R.
2. The path reaches its highest point when the particle is directly above the centre; there its rotational velocity ωd points forwards, parallel to v.
3. vP = v + ωd = v + (v/R)(R/2) = 3v/2. Not 2v — the doubling only happens for a point on the rim, where d = R.
4. Since ω is constant, acm = 0 and α = 0, so aP = ω²d = (v/R)²(R/2) = v²/2R, directed straight down toward the centre.
5. At that instant vP is horizontal and aP is vertical, so the whole acceleration is normal: a⊥ = v²/2R. This is exactly why the problem asks for the highest point — anywhere else you would first have to resolve a.
6. ρ = vP²/a⊥ = (3v/2)²/(v²/2R) = (9v²/4)(2R/v²) = 9R/2 = 4.5R ⇒ option (d).
7. Decoding the distractors: (b) R/2 just repeats the given distance, (c) 3R/2 is vP/ω (a speed-over-ω slip), and (a) 4R is the rim answer ρ = (2v)²/(v²/R) obtained by putting d = R.

Assuming ρ equals the distance to the instantaneous axis (here 3R/2, option c) is also wrong: the instantaneous axis gives velocity, never curvature, because it moves.

5A disc of radius R is rolling purely on a flat horizontal surface, with a constant angular velocity. The angle between the velocity and acceleration vectors of point P is (P is the rightmost point of the disc, on the horizontal diameter through the centre C) (a) zero (b) 45° (c) 135° (d) tan−1(½) — (Level-5)Angle between v and a of a rim pointApplying concepts — Instantaneous axis of rotation | JEE🟡 Similarc135°
Concept With constant angular velocity the centre travels at constant speed, so acm = 0 and α = 0. That strips the general rolling formula down to a single surviving term: every point carries only its centripetal acceleration ω²r pointing at the centre. Velocity, however, still has both parts — translation v plus rotation ωR — and for the point on the horizontal diameter these two are perpendicular to each other, which sets the 45° that ends up in the answer.
Formula v⃗P = v⃗cm + ω⃗×r⃗. Velocity of a point of a rigid body is translation of the centre plus rotation about it. a⃗P = −ω²r⃗. With acm = 0 and α = 0 only the radial term is left, so the acceleration points from P to the centre.
Solution
1. Set î along the direction of motion, ĵ up, centre at the origin; then P is at r⃗ = Rî and the disc spins clockwise, ω⃗ = −ωk̂.
2. Rotational part: ω⃗×r⃗ = (−ωk̂)×(Rî) = −ωRĵ, i.e. straight down with magnitude ωR = v.
3. v⃗P = vî − vĵ, so P moves at √2 v directed 45° below the forward horizontal. This is the same √2 v that Level-5 Q1 asks for as a magnitude.
4. a⃗P = −ω²Rî, i.e. horizontally backwards, pointing from P toward the centre C. Constant ω kills the tangential piece, so the acceleration is purely centripetal.
5. Direction of v⃗P is −45° from the +x axis; direction of a⃗P is 180° from the +x axis.
6. Angle between them = 180° − 45° = 135°, which matches option (c). Equivalently cosθ = (v⃗·a⃗)/(|v⃗||a⃗|) = (−vω²R)/(√2 v · ω²R) = −1/√2.
7. Distractors: (a) zero would need v parallel to a (only possible for a point where the rotational velocity vanishes), (b) 45° is the angle v makes with the horizontal, and (d) tan−1(½) comes from wrongly using ωR = 2v.

1What does the (2/5) factor represent in the moment of inertia formula for a solid sphere? (a) The torque of the body (b) The fraction of the total mass at the center (c) The fraction of total mass concentrated at the outer shell (d) A universal constant — (Level-1)Meaning of the shape factor in I = kMR²Moment of inertia of a ring and of a disk🟡 SimilarcThe fraction of total mass concentrated at the outer shell (equivalent-ring fraction)
Concept Every moment of inertia about a symmetry axis can be written as I = kMR², where M and R carry the physical size and k is a pure number set only by how the mass is spread. A thin ring, with all of its mass at the maximum distance R, is the reference case with k = 1. Any solid body has mass sitting at smaller radii too, so its k must be less than 1; for a uniform solid sphere the integration gives k = 2/5. So 2/5 is telling you what fraction of the mass would have to sit on a thin outer shell to reproduce the same rotational inertia.
Formula I = Σmiri² = MK². Definition of the moment of inertia and of the radius of gyration K. Isphere = (2/5)MR², Iring = MR². Comparing the two identifies 2/5 as an equivalent-ring mass fraction.
Solution
1. Split the answer into two questions: is 2/5 dimensional, and what does the comparison body look like?
2. In I = (2/5)MR² the M and the R² already carry kg and m², so 2/5 must be dimensionless — it can never be a torque, which kills option (a).
3. A universal constant would be the same for every shape, but a ring gives 1, a disc ½ and a hollow sphere 2/3, so option (d) is out. The number is a geometry factor, tied to one particular mass distribution.
4. Mass sitting at the centre contributes r = 0 and adds nothing to Σmiri², so a "fraction at the centre" could never set the value of I: option (b) fails.
5. Set (2/5)MR² = meqR² for an equivalent thin shell at radius R: meq = (2/5)M = 0.4M. Two fifths of the mass parked on the rim reproduces the sphere’s inertia exactly.
6. That reading matches option (c), and it also gives the radius of gyration K = √(2/5) R ≈ 0.632R, the single radius at which all the mass could sit.
7. Strictly the factor is a shape constant obtained by integration; option (c) is the only one of the four that captures it correctly.

2The moment of inertia of a ring about its central axis (perpendicular to its plane) is: (a) MR² (b) (1/2)MR² (c) (2/5)MR² (d) (2/3)MR² — (Level-1)Moment of inertia of a ringMoment of inertia of a ring and of a disk🟢 ExactaMR²

3Which factor does the moment of inertia depend on? Concept Moment of inertia is the rotational analogue of mass: it measures how hard a body resists being angularly accelerated. Its definition, I = Σmiri², contains only two ingredients — how much mass there is and how far each piece of it lies from the chosen axis. Nothing about the present motion enters, which is why a wheel at rest and the same wheel spinning at 100 rad/s have identical I. Change the axis, however, and I changes even though the body has not.
Formula I = Σmiri² = ∫r²dm. Only masses and perpendicular distances appear — no ω, no α, no time. I = Icm + Md². The parallel-axis theorem shows explicitly that I is a property of body and axis together.
Solution
1. Write the definition I = Σmiri² and list its variables: the masses mi and their perpendicular distances ri from the axis. That is the whole list.
2. Option (a) fails on inspection — ω never appears. Angular momentum L = Iω and KE = ½Iω² depend on the speed, but I itself does not.
3. Option (b) is half right: distance matters, but a distance without a mass attached to it contributes nothing, so mass distribution is equally essential.
4. Option (d) is wrong as stated: it is the shape of the body, not of the surface it rolls on, that matters. A disc on ice and the same disc on tarmac have the same I.
5. Option (c) names both ingredients and is therefore correct. Two bodies of equal mass, a ring and a disc of the same R, differ by a factor of 2 purely through distribution.
6. Numerical check: 4 kg spread as a ring of R = 0.5 m gives I = 4(0.25) = 1 kg m², while the same 4 kg as a disc gives ½(4)(0.25) = 0.5 kg m².
7. The parallel-axis theorem seals the point: moving the axis 0.5 m off centre adds Md² = 1 kg m² without changing a single gram.

4In the formula I = Σmiri², what does ri represent? (a) Radius of sphere (b) Perpendicular distance from axis (c) Radius of curvature (d) Rotational inertia — (Level-1)Meaning of ri in I = Σmiri²Torque, moment of inertia and rotational kinetic energy🟡 SimilarbPerpendicular distance from the axis
Concept The moment of inertia is built from the kinetic energy of a rotating body. A particle at perpendicular distance ri from the axis moves in a circle of that radius with speed vi = ωri, so its kinetic energy is ½miω²ri². Summing over the body and pulling out the common ω leaves ½(Σmiri²)ω², and the bracket is I. The ri in it therefore has to be exactly the radius of that particle’s circular path — the perpendicular distance to the axis, not to any point.
Formula vi = ωri. Only the perpendicular offset from the axis sets the speed of a rotating particle; the component along the axis does not move. KE = ½Σmivi² = ½Iω². Matching this against ½Iω² forces I = Σmiri².
Solution
1. Take a particle of mass mi in a body spinning at ω about a fixed axis; it traces a circle whose plane is perpendicular to that axis.
2. The radius of that circle is the perpendicular distance from the particle to the axis, so vi = ωri with that same ri.
3. KE = ½Σmivi² = ½ω²Σmiri², so identifying ½Iω² gives I = Σmiri² with ri the perpendicular distance ⇒ option (b).
4. Option (a) is wrong because the formula applies to rods, plates and irregular lumps, none of which has a "radius of sphere". Even inside a sphere, most particles are nearer the axis than R.
5. Option (c) confuses two unrelated ideas: radius of curvature describes the bending of a trajectory, and never enters the definition of I.
6. Option (d) is circular — rotational inertia is I, so ri cannot also be it; besides, ri has units of metres while I is kg m².
7. A worked check: for a rod of length L about one end, r for the element at x is x, and I = ∫0Lx²(M/L)dx = ML²/3, the standard result. Using a distance to the centre instead of to the axis would give the wrong integral.
