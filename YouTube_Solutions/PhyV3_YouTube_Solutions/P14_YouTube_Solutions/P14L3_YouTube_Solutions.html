1What is meant by a deforming force? (a) A force that causes translational motion (b) A force that alters the molecular structure permanently (c) A force that changes relative positions of molecules without moving the body as a whole (d) A force that stops the motion of a body (Level-1)Definition of deforming forceDeforming Force and Restoring Force | Mechanical Properties of Solids Class 11🟡 SimilarcA force that changes relative positions of molecules without moving the body as a whole
Concept A deforming force is an external force that tends to change the size or shape of a body by displacing its constituent molecules from their equilibrium positions, while the body as a whole does not move. Internal restoring forces (the origin of elasticity) oppose it. The key is to separate deformation (internal rearrangement) from translation (bodily motion): a net force on a free body produces acceleration, but a deforming force is balanced overall and merely strains the body.
Formula stress = F/A measures the deforming effect, and strain = ΔL/L measures the molecular displacement it produces. A deforming force is defined by the strain it creates, not by any translation it causes.
Solution
1. Read each option against the definition of a deforming force.
2. (a) “causes translational motion” — that is simply Newton’s second law for a free body; it moves the body but need not deform it. Reject.Translation is bulk motion, the opposite of what a deforming force targets.
3. (b) “alters the molecular structure permanently” — that describes a plastic (permanent) change beyond the elastic limit, a special case, not the general deforming force. Reject.
4. (d) “stops the motion of a body” — that is a retarding/braking force, unrelated to deformation. Reject.
5. (c) “changes relative positions of molecules without moving the body as a whole” — exactly the internal rearrangement that defines deformation. Accept.Changing the relative spacing of molecules is precisely what produces strain.
6. Hence the deforming force acts internally, balanced so the centre of mass stays put, yet the shape/size changes.
  deforming force  -> molecules shift (strain), body stays put
  net force        -> whole body accelerates (translation)
  F/A = stress  ;  dL/L = strain  (measures of deformation)
⚡ JEE Trick Watch the phrase “without moving the body as a whole” — it is the fingerprint of a deforming force. Distractors describe translation (a), plastic/permanent change (b) and braking (d); only (c) keeps the body in place while rearranging its molecules.
Answer: (c) A force that changes relative positions of molecules without moving the body as a whole.
2A wire is stretched to produce a strain of 0.002. If Young’s modulus is 1 × 1011 N/m2, what is the stress? (a) 2 × 108 N/m2 (b) 5 × 107 N/m2 (c) 2 × 109 N/m2 (d) 1 × 108 N/m2 (Level-2)Stress from strain & YStress, strain and Young's modulus | Physics Class 11, Concepts Made Simple🟡 Similara2 × 108 N/m2
Concept Within the elastic (Hooke’s-law) region, stress and strain are directly proportional, and the constant of proportionality is Young’s modulus Y. So once the strain and Y are known, the stress is a single multiplication — no area, force or length is needed. The strain here (0.002) is dimensionless, so the stress carries the same units as Y, namely N/m2.
Formula Y = stress/strain ⇒ stress = Y × strain. This is just the definition of Young’s modulus rearranged; it holds only below the elastic limit, which is assumed here.
Solution
1. List the data: strain ε = 0.002 = 2 × 10−3, Young’s modulus Y = 1 × 1011 N/m2.
2. Apply stress = Y × strain directly.No conversion is needed because strain is already a pure number.
3. Substitute: stress = (1 × 1011) × (2 × 10−3).
4. Multiply the mantissas and add the exponents: 1 × 2 = 2, and 1011 × 10−3 = 108.
5. Result: stress = 2 × 108 N/m2, which is option (a).
6. Quick check of scale: a strain of 0.2% in a stiff metal (Y ~ 1011) gives a stress of order 108 Pa — typical of a loaded wire, so the magnitude is sensible.
  stress = Y * strain
         = (1e11 N/m^2) * (0.002)
         = 2e8 N/m^2
  check: exponents 11 + (-3) = 8
⚡ JEE Trick Keep the powers of ten in your head: 1011 × 10−3 = 108, so the only distractors that survive are those with 108. Option (d) 1×108 is the trap if you misread the mantissa 2 as 1; (b) and (c) carry the wrong exponent entirely.
Answer: (a) 2 × 108 N/m2.
3A uniform steel wire of length 3 m and area of cross section 2 mm2 is extended through 3 mm. Calculate the energy stored in the wire, if the elastic limit is not exceeded. (Ysteel = 20 × 1010 N/m2) (Level-3)Elastic PE of a stretched wireA steel wire of length 3.0 m is stretched through 3.0 mm - the cross-sectional area of the wire is 2 mm^2 - energy stored🟢 Exact0.6 J
Concept A wire stretched within its elastic limit stores elastic potential energy equal to the work done against the restoring force. Because the restoring force grows linearly from 0 to its final value F as the wire extends from 0 to ΔL, the stored energy is the area of a triangle, U = (1/2)FΔL — the factor 1/2 is the whole subtlety. Writing F through Young’s modulus turns this into U = YA(ΔL)2/(2L).
Formula U = (1/2)FΔL = YA(ΔL)2/(2L), using F = YAΔL/L. The single factor 1/2 separates stored energy from the naive FΔL; forgetting it doubles the answer.
Solution
1. Convert to SI: L = 3 m, A = 2 mm2 = 2 × 10−6 m2, ΔL = 3 mm = 3 × 10−3 m, Y = 20 × 1010 = 2 × 1011 N/m2.mm2 → m2 needs a factor 10−6, not 10−3.
2. Use U = YA(ΔL)2/(2L).
3. Numerator: YA = (2 × 1011)(2 × 10−6) = 4 × 105; times (ΔL)2 = (3 × 10−3)2 = 9 × 10−6 gives 4 × 105 × 9 × 10−6 = 3.6.
4. Divide by 2L = 6: U = 3.6/6 = 0.6 J.
5. Cross-check via F: F = YAΔL/L = (4 × 105)(3 × 10−3)/3 = 400 N; U = (1/2)FΔL = (1/2)(400)(3 × 10−3) = 0.6 J.Two routes agreeing is the cheapest error-check.
6. State the result: energy stored U = 0.6 J.
  YA = (2e11)(2e-6) = 4e5
  U = YA (dL)^2 / (2L)
    = 4e5 * (3e-3)^2 / (2*3)
    = 4e5 * 9e-6 / 6 = 3.6/6 = 0.6 J
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps: the 1/2 (energy, not FΔL) and the mm2→m2 conversion (10−6). Drop either and you land on 1.2 J. A fast sanity line is U = (1/2)(force)(extension) with force ~ a few hundred newtons and extension ~ a few mm giving tenths of a joule.
Answer: 0.6 J.
4The rubber cord of a catapult has a cross-sectional area 1 mm2 and a total unstretched length 10 cm. It is stretched to 12 cm and then released to project a missile of mass 5 g. Find the velocity of projection of the mass. (Y = 5 × 108 N/m2) (Level-4)Catapult: elastic PE → KEThe rubber cord of a catapult has cross-section area 1 mm^2 and total unstretched length 10 cm - velocity of the missile🟢 Exact20 m/s
Concept The catapult’s stretched rubber cord stores elastic potential energy; on release this energy is handed over to the missile as kinetic energy. So the problem is two steps: (1) find the strain energy in the cord from Young’s modulus, (2) equate it to (1/2)mv2 for the projectile. The cord is treated as an ideal elastic band obeying Hooke’s law, so U = YA(ΔL)2/(2L).
Formula U = YA(ΔL)2/(2L) and U = (1/2)mv2 ⇒ v = √(2U/m). Energy conservation assumes all stored strain energy becomes kinetic energy of the missile (massless, loss-free cord).
Solution
1. Convert to SI: A = 1 mm2 = 1 × 10−6 m2, L = 10 cm = 0.10 m, ΔL = 12 − 10 = 2 cm = 0.02 m, Y = 5 × 108 N/m2, m = 5 g = 5 × 10−3 kg.
2. Stored energy: U = YA(ΔL)2/(2L) = (5 × 108)(1 × 10−6)(0.02)2/(2 × 0.10).
3. Evaluate: (5 × 108)(1 × 10−6) = 500; (0.02)2 = 4 × 10−4; so 500 × 4 × 10−4 = 0.2; divide by 0.2 gives U = 1.0 J.Use ΔL = 0.02 m, the extra stretch, not the final length 0.12 m.
4. Convert to speed: (1/2)mv2 = 1.0 ⇒ v2 = 2(1.0)/(5 × 10−3) = 400.
5. Take the root: v = √400 = 20 m/s.
6. State the result: the missile leaves at 20 m/s.
  dL = 12 - 10 = 2 cm = 0.02 m
  U = YA dL^2/(2L) = 500 * (0.02)^2 / 0.2 = 1.0 J
  (1/2) m v^2 = U -> v^2 = 2*1.0/0.005 = 400
  v = 20 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick The classic slip is using the stretched length 12 cm instead of the extension 2 cm — the strain energy depends on (ΔL)2, so that error inflates U by a factor 36. Convert grams to kilograms before the final step, or the speed comes out √1000 times too large.
Answer: 20 m/s.
5A wire having a length ℓ0 and cross-sectional area A is suspended at one of its ends from a ceiling. Density and Young’s modulus of the material of the wire are ρ and Y respectively. Its strain energy due to its own weight is ρ2g2Aℓ03/(αY). Find the value of α. (Level-5)Self-weight strain energyClass 11 Physics | Elasticity #19 - Suspended Rod / wire under its own weight (for JEE & NEET)🟡 Similarα = 6
Concept A wire hanging from the ceiling is stretched by its own weight, but the tension is not uniform: a cross-section at height x from the bottom carries only the weight of the wire below it. So the strain, and hence the stored energy density, varies along the length and must be integrated, not multiplied. This integration is what produces the numerical factor α in the denominator.
Formula energy in a slice: dU = T2 dx/(2AY), with tension T(x) = (ρAx)g. Each element behaves like a little spring storing T2/(2AY) per unit length; T is the weight of material below it.
Solution
1. Measure x upward from the lower free end. Mass below a section at height x is ρAx, so T(x) = ρAxg.
2. Energy stored in an element dx: dU = T2/(2AY) dx = (ρAxg)2/(2AY) dx.Square the tension because strain energy goes as (force)2.
3. Simplify the integrand: (ρ2A2g2x2)/(2AY) = (ρ2Ag2/2Y) x2.
4. Integrate from 0 to ℓ0: U = (ρ2Ag2/2Y) ∫ x2 dx = (ρ2Ag2/2Y)(ℓ03/3).∫0ℓ0 x2 dx = ℓ03/3 supplies the 3 that combines with the 2.
5. Collect: U = ρ2g2Aℓ03/(6Y).
6. Compare with the given form ρ2g2Aℓ03/(αY): the denominators match when α = 6.
  T(x) = rho*A*x*g   (weight hanging below x)
  dU = T^2/(2AY) dx = (rho^2 A g^2 /2Y) x^2 dx
  U = (rho^2 A g^2 /2Y) * (L0^3/3) = rho^2 g^2 A L0^3 /(6Y)
  => alpha = 6
⚡ JEE Trick Do not treat the weight as acting at the centre and multiply — the tension varies linearly, so the energy (going as T2) needs the integral of x2, giving the 1/3 that makes 6, not 2. Remember the pair of standard results: elongation under self-weight uses ℓ02/2, but energy uses ℓ03/6.
Answer: α = 6.

1When a metal wire elongates by hanging a load Mg on it, the gravitational potential energy of mass M decreases by Mgl. This energy appears: (a) as elastic potential energy completely (b) as thermal energy completely (c) half as elastic potential energy and half as thermal energy (d) as kinetic energy of the load completelyEnergy distribution on loadingWhen a metal wire elongates by hanging a load Mg on it, the gravitational potential energy of mass M decreases by Mgl - this energy appears as🟢 Exactchalf as elastic potential energy and half as thermal energy
Concept When a load Mg is hung suddenly on a wire, the load falls by the full elongation l, so gravity does work Mgl. But the wire’s restoring force rises only from 0 to Mg, so it can store at most (1/2)Mgl as elastic energy. The missing half, Mgl − (1/2)Mgl = (1/2)Mgl, is not lost from the universe — it is dissipated as heat (and transient vibrations) inside the wire. So the energy “appears” split equally between elastic PE and thermal energy.
Formula loss in GPE = Mgl; elastic PE stored = (1/2)(Mg)(l) = (1/2)Mgl; heat = Mgl − (1/2)Mgl = (1/2)Mgl. The 1/2 appears because the elastic force averages Mg/2 while the load drops by l.
Solution
1. The mass descends by l under a constant pull Mg, so gravity releases Mgl of potential energy.
2. The elastic energy actually stored is the area under the (linear) force–extension line: (1/2)(Mg)(l).Tension climbs 0 → Mg, average Mg/2, over extension l.
3. Energy balance: Mgl = (elastic PE) + (heat) ⇒ heat = Mgl − (1/2)Mgl = (1/2)Mgl.
4. Therefore exactly half the released GPE is stored elastically and half becomes thermal energy.
5. Match to options: (a) “completely elastic” would violate energy accounting; (b) “completely thermal” ignores the stored strain energy; (d) “kinetic” is wrong once the mass finally rests.
6. So the correct statement is (c): half elastic, half thermal.
  GPE released   = Mgl
  elastic stored = (1/2) Mg * l
  heat           = Mgl - (1/2)Mgl = (1/2)Mgl
  => 50% elastic, 50% thermal
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is answer (a): students equate the GPE drop with the stored energy, forgetting the factor 1/2. Sudden loading always wastes half the gravitational energy as heat; only an infinitely slow (quasi-static) loading would store it all. The equal 50–50 split is the signature of sudden loading.
Answer: (c) half as elastic potential energy and half as thermal energy.

4The Young’s modulus of a wire of length L and radius r is Y. If the length is reduced to L/2 and radius to r/2, its Young’s modulus will be (a) Y/2 (b) Y (c) 2Y (d) 4YY independent of dimensionsThe Young's modulus of a wire of length L and radius r is Y - if the length is reduced to L/2 and radius to r/2🟢 ExactbY
Concept Young’s modulus measures the stiffness of the material, not of the particular specimen. It is the ratio of stress to strain, both of which are already normalised by area and length. So cutting a wire shorter or thinner changes its extension for a given load, but never its Young’s modulus — that depends only on what the wire is made of (and its temperature).
Formula Y = stress/strain = (F/A)/(ΔL/L). Both F/A and ΔL/L are per-unit quantities, so geometry (L, r) is already divided out; Y is intrinsic.
Solution
1. Recall the definition: Y is stress divided by strain, a ratio of two intensive quantities.
2. Stress F/A already accounts for the cross-sectional area; strain ΔL/L already accounts for the length.
3. Therefore changing the length L → L/2 or the radius r → r/2 alters the wire’s stiffness (force per unit extension) but leaves the material constant Y untouched.The spring constant k = AY/L changes; Y does not.
