1The formula for time of flight T in projectile motion is: (a) u²sin²θ/2g (b) 2u sinθ/g (c) 2u cosθ/g (d) u sin2θ/gTime-of-flight formulaTime of flight of a projectile : Motion in a plane for JEE and NEET Physics🟡 Similarb(2u sinθ)/g
Concept An oblique projectile is two independent 1-D motions running on the same clock: a uniform-velocity motion along x (no horizontal force) and a uniformly accelerated motion along y (acceleration g downward). The time of flight T is decided only by the vertical motion — the projectile stays in the air exactly as long as the vertical sub-problem keeps it above the launch level. So the horizontal component u cosθ can never appear in T, which already kills two options on sight.
Formula Vertical motion with u_y = u sinθ (m s⁻¹), a_y = −g (m s⁻²), and net vertical displacement over the whole flight s_y = 0 (m) (lands at launch level). Use s_y = u_y t + ½ a_y t² (m). Setting s_y = 0 asks "when is it back at launch height?" — that is precisely the definition of time of flight.
Solution
1. Resolve the launch velocity: u_x = u cosθ (m s⁻¹), u_y = u sinθ (m s⁻¹).Only u_y feels gravity; u_x stays constant for the entire flight.
2. Write the vertical displacement at time t: s_y = (u sinθ)t − ½ g t² (m).The minus sign carries the fact that a_y = −g opposes the upward u_y.
3. Impose the landing condition s_y = 0 m at t = T: 0 = (u sinθ)T − ½ g T².
4. Factor: T[(u sinθ) − ½ g T] = 0 ⇒ T = 0 s (the launch instant) or (u sinθ) = ½ g T.T = 0 is the trivial root — it only says the projectile started at launch level.
5. Solve the physical root: T = 2u sinθ / g; units (m s⁻¹)/(m s⁻²) = s ✓ dimensionally a time.Equivalently T = 2 × (time to the top, u sinθ/g) — ascent and descent take equal time in the ideal case.

2Fly ball hit to the outfield (ignore air): behaviour of (i) horizontal & (ii) vertical velocity components, and (iii) horizontal & (iv) vertical acceleration components during ascent, descent, and at the topmost pointIndependence of horizontal/vertical motionProjectile motion - vertical and horizontal velocity (two components) #3🟡 Similar(i) vₓ constant (ii) v_y initially +ve, decreases to zero and then becomes progressively more −ve (iii) aₓ = 0 throughout (iv) a_y = −g throughout
Concept Once the bat has left the ball and air is ignored, the only force on the fly ball is its weight mg, pointing vertically down. A purely vertical force can change only the vertical velocity — it has no horizontal component with which to change vx. This is the principle of independence of motions: the horizontal and vertical parts of a projectile evolve as two separate 1-D problems that share only the clock. Every one of the four parts follows from that single statement.
Formula With g = 9.8 m s⁻² downward: a_x = 0 m s⁻², a_y = −g = −9.8 m s⁻² (taking up as positive) at every instant of the flight. Hence v_x = u cosθ = constant (m s⁻¹) and v_y = u sinθ − g t (m s⁻¹). a_x = 0 because gravity has no horizontal component; a_y is constant because the weight mg is constant near the ground. Assumptions stated explicitly: no air drag, no wind, no spin/Magnus force, g uniform over the flight.
Solution
1. (i) Horizontal component of velocity, v_x. Newton's second law along x: m a_x = ΣF_x = 0 N ⇒ a_x = 0 m s⁻².Weight acts straight down, so it contributes nothing along x.
  • Therefore v_x = u cosθ stays constant in magnitude and direction throughout — during ascent, at the topmost point, and during descent. It is not zero at the top.
2. (ii) Vertical component of velocity, v_y = u sinθ − g t (m s⁻¹).g opposes the upward motion, so v_y falls linearly by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second.
  • Ascent: v_y points up and decreases from u sinθ toward 0 m s⁻¹.
  • Topmost point: v_y = 0 m s⁻¹ exactly — that is what defines the top. The ball is still moving, with speed v_x horizontally.
  • Descent: v_y points down and grows in magnitude, reaching u sinθ downward back at launch level.
3. (iii) Horizontal component of acceleration: a_x = 0 m s⁻² during ascent, at the top, and during descent — no horizontal force exists at any instant.
4. (iv) Vertical component of acceleration: a_y = g = 9.8 m s⁻², directed vertically downward during ascent, at the top, and during descent — constant in magnitude and direction.At the topmost point the velocity is momentarily horizontal but the acceleration is NOT zero — if it were, the ball would hang in the air forever.

⚡ JEE Trick Burn in one line: "a_x = 0 always, a_y = g down always — even at the top." The single commonest blunder in projectile motion is writing "at the highest point acceleration = 0" because the velocity looks momentarily flat; it is only v_y that vanishes there, never a_y. Corollary worth memorising: the projectile's minimum speed = u cosθ, and it occurs at the top; and its speed at any height h is the same going up as coming down.
Key point: v_x = u cosθ is constant throughout; v_y decreases to 0 m s⁻¹ at the top then grows downward; a_x = 0 m s⁻² at every instant; a_y = g = 9.8 m s⁻² vertically downward at every instant, including the topmost point.
3A projectile has a range of 50 m and reaches a maximum height of 10 m. What is the elevation (angle of projection)?Range–height relationEx-74 Motion in a plane: a projectile has a range of 50 m and reaches a maximum height of 10 m · A projectile has a range of 50 m and reaches a maximum height of 10 m🟢 Exacttan⁻¹(0.8)
Concept For an oblique projectile the range R and the maximum height H are both manufactured from the same two launch quantities — speed u and elevation θ. Take their ratio and u vanishes, and so does g, leaving a clean link between the shape of the trajectory (how tall vs how wide) and the angle alone. That is exactly why you can answer this without ever being told u or g: how far it goes compared with how high it rises fixes the elevation.
Formula R = u²sin2θ/g and H = u²sin²θ/2g, so H/R = tanθ/4 ⇒ tanθ = 4H/R. sin2θ = 2 sinθcosθ, hence H/R = sin²θ /(2·2 sinθcosθ) = sinθ/(4cosθ) = tanθ/4 — both u and g cancel. Data: R = 50 m, H = 10 m.
Solution
1. Form the height-to-range ratio: H/R = tanθ/4.Taking the ratio is the whole trick — it deletes the unknowns u and g in one stroke.
2. Rearrange for the elevation: tanθ = 4H/R.
3. Substitute: tanθ = 4×10/50 = 40/50 = 0.8.
4. Invert: θ = tan⁻¹(0.8) ≈ 38.7°.Between 30° and 45°, matching a fairly flat, long shot where R = 5H.
5. Sanity check: a 45° launch gives H/R = 1/4 ⇒ H = 12.5 m for R = 50 m; the actual H = 10 m < 12.5 m, so θ < 45° ✓.

⚡ JEE Trick Burn in tanθ = 4H/R: any "range and height given, find the angle" problem collapses to a single division, no u, no g. Trap: reaching for R = u²sin2θ/g and trying to find u first, then stalling because g is not given. Second trap: dropping the 4 and writing tanθ = H/R = 0.2, which gives a wrong ~11°.
Answer: θ = tan⁻¹(0.8) ≈ 38.7°.

Concept This is the classic NCERT oblique-projectile drill. Resolve the launch velocity into a horizontal part (uniform, no force) and a vertical part (uniformly retarded by g). The vertical sub-problem alone delivers the maximum height H and the time of flight T; the horizontal sub-problem, run for that same time T, delivers the landing distance R. Three answers, one resolution.
Formula u_x = u cosθ, u_y = u sinθ; then H = u_y²/2g, T = 2u_y/g, R = u_x·T = u²sin2θ/g. H uses v_y = 0 at the apex; T is twice the rise time; R is uniform horizontal speed × flight time. Data: u = 28 m s⁻¹, θ = 30°, g = 9.8 m s⁻², sin30° = 0.5, cos30° ≈ 0.866.
Solution

4. (iii) Landing distance: R = u_x·T = 24.25×2.857 ≈ 69.3 m.
5. Cross-check via R = u²sin2θ/g = 784×sin60°/9.8 = 784×0.866/9.8 ≈ 69.3 m ✓.2θ = 60°, not 30° — a very common slip.

5Figure shows projectile motion without air resistance. If air resistance is considered: (a) upward time < downward time (b) θ > α (c) height & range decrease (d) v < uEffect of air resistance on projectileMotion in a plane Problem with Solution: Impact of Air Resistance on Range of Projectile🟡 Similarabcdall four printed options — (a) time taken by projectile during upward motion is lesser than time taken during downward motion; (b) θ > α; (c) the value of height attained and of range of projectile decreases; (d) v < u
Concept The book's figure shows the ideal (drag-free) parabola: launched at angle α, apex height H, range R, landing at angle θ. Switching air resistance on adds a drag force that always points opposite to the instantaneous velocity and grows with speed. Two consequences drive every option: (1) drag has a horizontal component, so vx is no longer constant — it decays; (2) drag dissipates mechanical energy, so the ball can never come back as fast as it left. The path stops being a symmetric parabola and becomes lop-sided — steeper on the way down.
Formula With drag F_d = −k v (N), antiparallel to velocity: along x, m a_x = −F_{d,x} < 0 N ⇒ v_x decreases (m s⁻¹). Ascent: m a_y = −mg − F_{d,y} ⇒ retardation > g. Descent: m a_y = −mg + F_{d,y} ⇒ acceleration < g. On the way up gravity and drag both oppose the motion (they add); on the way down drag opposes gravity (they subtract) — this asymmetry is the whole question.
Solution
1. Option (a) — time up vs time down. Going up the retardation is (g + F_d/m) > 9.8 m s⁻²; coming down the acceleration is (g − F_d/m) < 9.8 m s⁻².A larger deceleration kills the upward velocity quickly, while a smaller downward acceleration builds speed slowly over the same height.
  • So t_up < t_down (s) — statement (a) is correct.
2. Option (b) — θ > α. Horizontal drag bleeds v_x continuously, so at landing v_x < u cosα while the vertical speed is still substantial. The landing angle satisfies tanθ = |v_y|/v_x with a smaller denominator ⇒ steeper.In the ideal case v_x is unchanged and symmetry gives θ = α exactly; drag breaks that symmetry in only one direction.
  • So θ > α — statement (b) is correct.
3. Option (c) — height and range decrease. Drag removes energy the whole way. Apex: the extra retardation destroys u_y over a shorter rise ⇒ H decreases (m). Range: v_x decays and the flight is cut short ⇒ R decreases (m).
  • So both H and R decrease — statement (c) is correct.
4. Option (d) — v < u. Work–energy theorem over the full flight (start and finish at the same height, so ΔPE = 0 J): ½mv² − ½mu² = W_drag < 0 J.Drag is always antiparallel to the displacement, so its work is strictly negative — the ball must return slower than it left.
  • So v < u (m s⁻¹) — statement (d) is correct.

From that picture alone you read off t_up < t_down, θ > α, smaller H and R, and v < u without a single equation. Trap: students assume v_x still stays constant "because gravity is vertical" — with drag it does not, and that is exactly why the descent is steeper.
