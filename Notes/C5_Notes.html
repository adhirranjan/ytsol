Chemical Bond Basics & the Ionic (Electrovalent) Bond

  • A chemical bond is a force that holds two or more atoms together as a stable molecule; it forms only if the molecule has lower energy (greater stability) than the separate atoms — bond formation is exothermic.
  • Greater the decrease in energy, stronger the bond. Stability ∝ 1/energy.
  • Classification: Strong (inter-atomic, ~200 kJ/mol) = ionic, covalent, coordinate, metallic; Weak (inter-molecular, ~2.42 kJ/mol) = hydrogen bond, van der Waals.
  • Cause of combination: tendency to acquire minimum energy & noble-gas configuration (stable ns² or ns²np⁶). Only ns, np and (n−1)d electrons take part; inert gases don't bond.

Ionic / electrovalent bond

  • Formed by complete transfer of one or more electrons from an electropositive atom (groups 1, 2, 13) to an electronegative atom (groups 15, 16, 17), giving a cation + anion held by electrostatic attraction.
  • Electrovalency = number of electrons lost or gained (Mg → Mg²⁺, electrovalency 2).
  • The attraction is equal in all directions ⇒ ionic bond is non-directional ⇒ a 3-D crystal lattice forms. Ionic solids have only an empirical formula (NaCl), no molecular formula.
  • Cross-cross (criss-cross) rule: write AxBy then swap valencies as subscripts → AyBx. e.g. Ca²⁺ + Cl⁻ → CaCl₂; Al³⁺ + PO₄³⁻ → AlPO₄.
factors favouring ionic bond (Born–Haber steps)A(g) + IE → A⁺(g) + e⁻ · B(g) + e⁻ → B⁻(g) + EGE · A⁺(g) + B⁻(g) → AB(s) + Lattice Energy
  • Favoured by low ionisation enthalpy of the metal, high (negative) electron-gain enthalpy of the non-metal, and high lattice energy.
  • Cation-forming tendency: Cs⁺ > Rb⁺ > K⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺ (lower IE ⇒ easier). Anion tendency: Cl⁻ > F⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻ (by EGE).
lattice energyLE ∝ (q⁺ × q⁻)/(r⁺ + r⁻) · charge ↑ ⇒ LE ↑ (NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃) · size ↑ ⇒ LE ↓ (LiCl > NaCl > KCl > RbCl > CsCl)

Properties of ionic compounds

  • Hard, crystalline, brittle; high melting/boiling points (strong electrostatic force).
  • Conductivity: non-conductors as solids (ions fixed), good conductors when fused or in aqueous solution. Order: solid < fused < aqueous.
  • Highly soluble in polar solvents (water), poorly in non-polar (benzene). Show isomorphism (similar ion configuration ⇒ similar crystal shape).
⚠ TrapSolid NaCl does not conduct electricity — the ions are locked in the lattice. It conducts only when molten or dissolved, where ions become free to move.

Lewis Structures, Covalent & Coordinate Bonds, Formal Charge, Fajan's Rules

Lewis symbols & dot structures

  • Lewis symbols show only the valence electrons as dots/crosses around the symbol. Group valence = number of dots, or 8 − (number of dots) for N, O, F.
  • Algorithm: (1) add valence e⁻ of all atoms; (2) add 1 e⁻ per unit negative charge, subtract 1 per unit positive charge; (3) put least-electronegative atom in the centre and draw single bonds; (4) complete octets of outer atoms with lone pairs; (5) shift remaining pairs into multiple bonds so every atom gets an octet (H a duplet).

Covalent bond

  • Formed by mutual sharing of electron pairs between atoms to complete octets (H completes a duplet). 2 / 4 / 6 shared electrons = single / double / triple bond.
  • Covalency = number of covalent bonds an atom forms. Completion of octet is not essential (BF₃, PCl₅).
  • Non-polar covalent bond: equal EN (H₂, Cl₂). Polar covalent bond: EN difference shifts the shared pair, giving partial charges (δ⁺H–Clδ⁻).
Variable valency & the role of d-orbitals
Atoms with vacant valence-shell orbitals unpair lone pairs into them (using promotion energy) to show higher covalency. N shows only 3 (no 2d orbital ⇒ NCl₅ does not exist); P shows 3 & 5 (PCl₃, PCl₅ both exist); S shows 2, 4, 6 (SF₂, SF₄, SF₆); I shows 1, 3, 5, 7. OF₂ exists but OF₄/OF₆ do not (O has no d-orbitals).

Coordinate (dative) bond

  • A covalent bond in which both shared electrons come from one atom (the donor); the other atom (electron-deficient acceptor) contributes none. Shown as D→A.
  • Conditions: donor has a complete octet with ≥1 lone pair; acceptor is short of an electron pair. Examples: H₃N→BF₃, NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, O₃, N₂O, CO, H₂O₂, H₂SO₄.
  • Compounds with all three bond types (ionic + covalent + coordinate): NH₄Cl, CuSO₄, K₄[Fe(CN)₆], KNO₃.

Formal charge

formal charge on an atom in a Lewis structureFC = V − L − ½S (V = valence e⁻ of free atom, L = lone-pair e⁻, S = shared/bonding e⁻)
💡 TipΣ formal charges = the overall charge of the species. Among competing Lewis structures the one with formal charges closest to zero is most stable.

Fajan's rules (polarisation)

  • An ionic bond gains covalent character when the cation polarises (distorts) the anion's electron cloud. Covalent character increases with: high charge on cation/anion, small cation, large anion, and an 18-electron (pseudo-inert-gas) cation.
  • e.g. CuCl (Cu⁺ = 2,8,18) is more covalent than NaCl (Na⁺ = 2,8) though the cations are almost the same size ⇒ CuCl m.p. 442 °C < NaCl 800 °C.
polarising power of cationφ = (ionic charge)/(radius)² · covalent character: NaF < NaCl < NaBr < NaI (anion size ↑) ; NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃ (cation charge ↑)

Valence Bond Theory (VBT) · Orbital Overlap

  • Heitler–London / Pauling–Slater idea: a covalent bond forms when a half-filled orbital (unpaired e⁻) of one atom overlaps a half-filled orbital of another with anti-parallel spins, giving a localised bond orbital of maximum electron density.
  • Greater the overlap ⇒ shorter the bond, stronger the attraction and higher the bond energy.
  • σ-bond = head-on (axial) overlap (s–s, s–p, p–p end-on); π-bond = sidewise (lateral) overlap of p–p (or p–d) orbitals. Head-on overlap is stronger than lateral.
  • Bond-energy order of overlaps: p–p (lateral, π) < s–s < s–p < p–p (head-on, σ).
  • N₂: three unpaired 2p e⁻ per N → one σ (head-on) + two π (lateral) = N≡N triple bond.
Overlap sign & the H₂ energy curve
Positive overlap = lobes of the same sign meet (bonding); negative = opposite signs meet; zero overlap = orbitals of different symmetry (e.g. s with p⊥, or px with py) — equal + and − contributions cancel. On the potential-energy vs internuclear-distance curve for H₂, energy falls to a minimum at d = 74 pm (the bond length); that minimum is where the bond forms.
⚠ Limitations of VBTCannot explain the paramagnetism of O₂, bonding in electron-deficient species (B₂H₆) or metals, and needs the extra ideas of resonance and hybridisation to fit real shapes.
★ Why hybridise?Ground-state C (2s²2p²) shows only 2 unpaired e⁻, yet CH₄ has four equivalent C–H bonds at 109°28′. Promotion (2s→2p) + hybridisation is introduced to explain the observed covalency, equal bond lengths and shape.

Hybridisation & Geometry of Molecules

  • Hybridisation = mixing of atomic orbitals of the same atom with slightly different energies to give the same number of new orbitals of equal energy and identical shape (hybrid orbitals).
  • No. of hybrid orbitals = no. of atomic orbitals mixed. Hybrid orbitals form only σ-bonds (never π). Orbitals (not electrons) hybridise; a lone pair on a hybrid orbital distorts the geometry.
  • More s-character ⇒ smaller hybrid orbital ⇒ more electronegative atom, shorter bond, higher bond energy.
inter-orbital anglecos α = −(1/m) where m = number of p-orbitals mixed → sp: 180° (50% s) · sp²: 120° (33.3% s) · sp³: 109°28′ (25% s)

VSEPR Theory & Molecular Shapes

  • VSEPR (Gillespie): electron pairs around the central atom arrange to minimise repulsion; the shape follows from the arrangement of bond pairs (lone pairs are placed but not "seen" in naming the shape).
  • Repulsion order: LP–LP > LP–BP > BP–BP; a double bond needs more room than a single bond (DB–DB > DB–SB > SB–SB).
  • With a lone pair on the central atom: raising the electronegativity of the surrounding atoms decreases the bond angle; raising that of the central atom increases it. Back bonding = a lone pair donated from a filled orbital into a vacant orbital of the adjacent bonded atom.
  • No lone pair ⇒ molecular shape = electron-pair geometry; lone pair(s) ⇒ angles distort and the visible shape changes (see the MEMORIZE VSEPR table).
total electron pairs around central atom½ [ (valence e⁻ of central atom) + (no. of atoms attached by single bonds) + (anion charge) − (cation charge) ] ⇒ lone pairs = total pairs − bond pairs

Molecular Orbital Theory — LCAO, Bonding & Antibonding MOs

  • MOT (Hund & Mulliken, 1932): atomic orbitals of comparable energy and proper symmetry combine to form molecular orbitals that belong to the whole molecule (polycentric). MOs fill by Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules.
  • LCAO: ψMO = ψA ± ψB. No. of MOs formed = no. of AOs combined.
  • Bonding MO (ψA + ψB, same-sign lobes): constructive interference ⇒ more electron density between nuclei ⇒ lower energy than the AOs.
  • Antibonding MO (ψA − ψB, opposite-sign lobes): destructive interference ⇒ a node between the nuclei ⇒ higher energy. The bonding MO is stabilised to the same extent the antibonding MO is destabilised.
  • σ vs π MO: σ = head-on overlap along the axis (maximum overlap, strong, axially symmetric); π = sidewise overlap perpendicular to the axis (less overlap, weaker, not axially symmetric).
Zero-overlap (non-bonding) & g/u symmetry
If the two AOs have different symmetry about the internuclear axis (e.g. s with a perpendicular p, or px with py), the (+,+) stabilisation is exactly cancelled by (+,−) — no MO forms (zero overlap). MO wavefunctions are labelled g (gerade — sign unchanged on inversion through the centre) or u (ungerade — sign reversed).

Dipole Moment & Molecular Polarity

dipole momentµ = q × d · 1 D = 1 × 10⁻¹⁸ esu·cm = 3.335 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m · µ is a vector (points + → −)
  • Diatomic: µ = the single bond moment; greater ΔEN ⇒ greater µ. HF (1.78) > HCl (1.07) > HBr (0.79) > HI (0.38) D.
  • Polyatomic: µ = vector resultant of all bond moments. H₂O = 1.84 D, NH₃ = 1.47 D.
  • µ = 0 for symmetric molecules (bond moments cancel): CO₂, CS₂, BeF₂ (linear); BF₃ (trigonal planar); CH₄, CCl₄ (tetrahedral).
★ NH₃ vs NF₃Both are pyramidal with a lone pair on N. In NH₃ the lone-pair dipole and the resultant of the three N–H bond moments point the same way (add) ⇒ µ = 1.47 D. In NF₃ the N→F bond moments oppose the lone-pair dipole (partly cancel) ⇒ µ = only 0.24 D. Hence µ(NH₃) > µ(NF₃).
applications% ionic character = (µobserved / µ100% ionic) × 100 · distinguishes polar/non-polar, shape (symmetry), cis (µ≠0) vs trans (µ=0), o > m > p (para µ=0)

L-10

Hydrogen Bonding & Bond Length

Hydrogen bonding

  • When H is bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (F, O, N), the polarised δ⁺H is attracted to the lone pair of an electronegative atom of a neighbouring molecule — this attraction is the hydrogen bond.
  • Intermolecular (between molecules): HF, H₂O, NH₃, alcohols, p-nitrophenol — raises boiling point & enables water-solubility. Intramolecular (within one molecule): o-nitrophenol, o-hydroxybenzaldehyde.
  • H-bonding makes b.p. of HF > HCl, H₂O ≫ H₂S, NH₃ > PH₃ (all anomalously high). In ice the molecules form an open, cage-like H-bonded lattice ⇒ ice is less dense than water.

Bond length

  • Equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. For a covalent molecule A–B, d = rA + rB (sum of covalent radii); in a homonuclear molecule the covalent radius is half the bond length.
  • Factors: (i) larger atoms ⇒ longer bond (HI > HBr > HCl > HF); (ii) higher bond multiplicity ⇒ shorter bond (C≡C < C=C < C–C); (iii) more s-character ⇒ shorter bond (sp < sp² < sp³ for C–H).

Book Example · L-10

Strong Box · PYQ 102
(JEE Adv 2022, Paper-1) Correct statement(s) about the MOs formed by overlap of two 2p_z orbitals (z = molecular axis).
Two 2p_z orbitals overlap head-on ⇒ only σ2p_z and σ*2p_z form (no π). The bonding σ2p_z has two nodal planes, both perpendicular to the axis ⇒ statement (a). Statements about π/π* do not apply here.
(a) σ orbital has two nodal planes. (2p_z overlap gives only σ/σ*, not π.)
