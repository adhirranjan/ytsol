1What is the SI unit of acceleration? (a) m/s (b) km/h (c) m/s² (d) cm/s²SI unit of accelerationThe unit of acceleration in the SI system is · SI unit of acceleration is🟢 Exactcm/s²
Concept Acceleration measures how fast the velocity itself is changing, so by definition it is a velocity divided by a time. Its unit is therefore built entirely from the units of velocity and time — you never memorise it, you derive it. In SI, length is the metre and time is the second, so velocity is metre-per-second and acceleration is that per second again. The trap options are quantities with the wrong dimensions (a velocity) or the right dimensions in the wrong system (CGS).
Formula a = Δv ÷ Δt ⇒ unit of a = (unit of velocity) ÷ (unit of time). Velocity = displacement ÷ time = m ÷ s = m s⁻¹, so acceleration = m s⁻¹ ÷ s = m s⁻². SI base units needed: length → metre (m), time → second (s). Dimensional formula of acceleration: [a] = [L T⁻²].
Solution
1. Start from the definition: a = change of velocity ÷ time = Δv ÷ Δt.
2. Substitute SI units: (m s⁻¹) ÷ (s) = m s⁻² (metre per second squared).Dividing a "per-second" quantity by seconds again gives "per-second-squared".
3. Test each option for dimension and system: (a) m/s = velocity (L T⁻¹), wrong dimension.
4. (b) km/h = velocity again, and not even an SI unit — doubly wrong.
5. (d) cm/s² has the correct dimension L T⁻² but uses the centimetre — that is the CGS unit, not SI.
6. Only (c) m/s² is L T⁻² expressed in SI base units.

Concept Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. When it is constant, the velocity does not merely change — it changes by the same amount every second. So the velocity gained over a stretch of time is just "how much per second" × "how many seconds," which is added on top of whatever velocity the particle already had. That single idea is the whole of the first equation of motion; no integration is needed once a is constant.
Formula v = u + a·t — v = final velocity (m s⁻¹), u = initial velocity (m s⁻¹), a = constant acceleration (m s⁻²), t = time elapsed (s). Units check: a·t has m s⁻² × s = m s⁻¹, which matches u and v, so the sum is dimensionally legal. Assumption: a is constant over the whole interval and the motion is along a straight line, so all quantities can be treated as signed scalars along one axis.
Solution
1. List the data with units: u = 10 m s⁻¹, a = 2 m s⁻², t = 4 s; unknown v.Both u and a are positive, so the particle speeds up along its direction of motion.
2. Choose the equation linking u, a, t and v — that is v = u + a·t.Displacement is neither given nor asked, so the first equation of motion is the direct route.
3. Compute the velocity gained: a·t = 2 m s⁻² × 4 s = 8 m s⁻¹.Gaining 2 m s⁻¹ every second for 4 s gives 2+2+2+2 = 8 m s⁻¹.
4. Add it to the initial velocity: v = 10 m s⁻¹ + 8 m s⁻¹ = 18 m s⁻¹.
5. Sanity check: v > u as expected for positive acceleration, and 18 m s⁻¹ is a plausible everyday speed.

3A train moving with uniform acceleration: the two ends of the train pass a point on the track with velocities v₁ and v₂ (v₁ = v₂ = 2 m/s). Find the velocity with which the middle point of the train passes the same pointVelocity of the midpoint from end velocitiesA train is moving with uniform acceleration. The two ends of the train pass through a point on t...🟢 Exact2 ⚠
Concept As the train slides past a fixed point, every part of it — front, middle, rear — crosses that point at a different instant and therefore with a different velocity. Because the acceleration is uniform, the neat result is that the velocity of the mid-point of the length is not the ordinary average of the end velocities but the root-mean-square of them. This comes straight from v² = u² + 2as, in which it is v² (not v) that grows linearly with distance, so the "half-distance" value uses squares. Note: as printed the data v₁ = v₂ = 2 m/s secretly forces a = 0, contradicting "uniform acceleration" — a book defect — but the formula still returns a clean value.
Formula v² = u² + 2as applied over half the length gives v_mid = √((v₁² + v₂²) ÷ 2). Over the full length L, v₂² = v₁² + 2aL; over L/2, v_mid² = v₁² + 2a(L/2) = v₁² + (v₂² − v₁²)/2 = (v₁²+v₂²)/2. Data: v₁ = 2 m s⁻¹ (one end), v₂ = 2 m s⁻¹ (other end).
Solution
1. Write v² = u² + 2as for the full train length L: v₂² = v₁² + 2aL.
2. Write it again to the mid-point, distance L/2: v_mid² = v₁² + 2a(L/2) = v₁² + aL.
3. From step 1, aL = (v₂² − v₁²)/2; substitute: v_mid² = v₁² + (v₂² − v₁²)/2 = (v₁² + v₂²)/2.So the mid-point velocity is the RMS of the two end velocities — bigger than the plain average whenever v₁ ≠ v₂.
4. Insert the numbers: v_mid = √((2² + 2²)/2) = √((4 + 4)/2) = √4 = 2 m s⁻¹.
5. Consistency: with v₁ = v₂ the RMS equals the common value, so 2 m/s is forced regardless of the (here zero) acceleration.

  second end         L                     v2 = 2 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick For "velocity at the mid-point of distance" use the RMS √((v₁²+v₂²)/2); for "velocity at the mid-instant of time" use the ordinary average (v₁+v₂)/2. Trap: students blindly average to (2+2)/2 = 2 and get lucky here, but that average is wrong whenever the two end speeds differ — and this very question is internally inconsistent (equal end speeds ⇒ a = 0), a printed-book defect.

4A car starts from rest and moves with constant acceleration. The ratio of the distance covered in the nth second to that covered in `n` seconds is🟢 Exactc2/n − 1/n²
Concept Two quantities that sound alike must not be confused: the distance covered in n seconds is the whole trip from t = 0 to t = n, while the distance covered in the nth second is only the thin slice between t = n−1 and t = n. The first grows like n² (area of a triangle on the v–t graph); the second grows like the odd numbers (the difference of two such triangles). Their ratio is a pure number in n, so no value of a survives — a is common to both and cancels.
Formula Distance in n seconds S = ½ a n² (u = 0). Distance in the nth second Sₙ = u + ½ a(2n − 1) = ½ a(2n − 1). Sₙ = S(n) − S(n−1) = ½a[n² − (n−1)²] = ½a(2n − 1). Data: starts from rest, u = 0, acceleration a constant.
Solution
1. Distance in the full n seconds: S = ½ a n².
2. Distance in the nth second alone: Sₙ = ½ a(2n − 1).
3. Form the ratio Sₙ : S = [½ a(2n − 1)] ÷ [½ a n²] = (2n − 1)/n².The ½ and a cancel top and bottom — the answer cannot depend on how hard the car accelerates.
4. Split the single fraction: (2n − 1)/n² = 2n/n² − 1/n² = 2/n − 1/n².
5. Match to the options: this is exactly choice (c).Watch the order of terms — (a) and (b) put 2/n² first, which is a different expression.

  distance in nth second       ½ a(2n-1)         prop (2n-1)
  ratio (nth sec / n sec)      (2n-1)/n²         2/n - 1/n²   <-- ANSWER
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a ratio of two kinematic distances is asked from rest, expect a to cancel — so plug in a convenient a = 1 (or test n = 1, 2 against the options numerically). At n = 1 the true ratio is (2−1)/1 = 1; only (c) gives 2/1 − 1/1 = 1. Trap: reading "nth second" as "n seconds" — the sliver vs the whole trip.
Answer: (c) 2/n − 1/n².

Concept The motion has two straight-line phases on a velocity–time graph: a rising line of slope α from rest up to a peak velocity v, then a falling line of slope −β back down to rest. The peak is shared by both phases, so v links them. Writing the time of each phase in terms of that same v and adding the two times to the given total t gives one equation in v — a classic "meet in the middle" trick.
Formula Phase 1 (rest → v): v = α t₁ ⇒ t₁ = v/α. Phase 2 (v → rest): 0 = v − β t₂ ⇒ t₂ = v/β. Constraint t₁ + t₂ = t. Both phases start or end at the same peak v, so v is the single unknown tying the accelerations to the total time.
Solution
1. Time to speed up from rest to peak v at rate α: t₁ = v/α.
2. Time to slow from peak v to rest at rate β: t₂ = v/β.
3. Add and set equal to the total: v/α + v/β = t.The whole journey is only these two phases, so their times must sum to t.
4. Factor v: v(1/α + 1/β) = t ⇒ v·(α + β)/(αβ) = t.Common denominator: 1/α + 1/β = (β + α)/(αβ).
5. Solve for the peak: v = αβ t /(α + β).Unit check: (m s⁻² · m s⁻² · s)/(m s⁻²) = m s⁻¹, a velocity ✓.

  slow-down   -β        t2 = v/β     v = β t2
  total       --        t1+t2 = t    v = αβt/(α+β)   <-- ANSWER
⚡ JEE Trick The peak velocity is set by the product-over-sum combination αβ/(α+β) — the same shape that appears in parallel resistors and reduced mass. Trap: options (a)/(b) with (α²±β²)/αβ are dimensionally wrong (they give m s⁻², not m s⁻¹); a five-second unit check kills them before any algebra.

Concept A speed–time graph converts kinematics into geometry. Two readings come straight off the picture: the slope of the line is the acceleration (rise = change in speed, run = change in time), and the area under the line up to any instant is the distance covered in that interval. Here the speed rises by exactly 5 m s⁻¹ every 10 s, so the plot is a straight line — the signature of uniform acceleration — and both readings are easy.
Formula (i) a = Δv ÷ Δt = (v₂ − v₁) ÷ (t₂ − t₁) in m s⁻², the slope. (ii) s = area under the v–t graph = ½ × (u + v) × t in m — the area of a trapezium of parallel sides u and v (m s⁻¹) and width t (s). Units: m s⁻¹ × s = m, so an area on a v–t graph is genuinely a distance. Assumption: the tabulated points lie on one straight line (verified below), so a is constant and the trapezium rule is exact.
Solution
1. Plot/inspect the data — speed 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 m s⁻¹ at times 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 s.Every 10 s step raises the speed by exactly 5 m s⁻¹, so the points lie on a straight line: acceleration is uniform.
2. (i) Acceleration — take the slope over the full run: Δv = 30 m s⁻¹ − 5 m s⁻¹ = 25 m s⁻¹; Δt = 50 s − 0 s = 50 s.
3. a = 25 m s⁻¹ ÷ 50 s = 0.5 m s⁻².Cross-check on one small step: (10 − 5) ÷ (10 − 0) = 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5 m s⁻² — the same, confirming the line.

Second, the car is slowing down, so the acceleration is negative (a retardation); the sign is not decoration — it is what makes the algebra return a positive distance. With u, v and t known, the cleanest route is the average-velocity form, which needs no value of a at all.
Formula Conversion: 1 km h⁻¹ = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 5/18 m s⁻¹. Then s = ½ × (u + v) × t — distance (m) = mean of initial and final velocity (m s⁻¹) × time (s), valid only for constant acceleration. Solution
1. Convert the initial speed: u = 90 km h⁻¹ × (5/18) = 450/18 = 25 m s⁻¹.Multiplying by 5/18 is the same as ×1000 m then ÷3600 s.
2. Convert the final speed: v = 36 km h⁻¹ × (5/18) = 180/18 = 10 m s⁻¹. Time t = 5 s.

4. Use the average-velocity form: s = ½ × (25 m s⁻¹ + 10 m s⁻¹) × 5 s.

Concept "Uniformly accelerated" does not mean the velocity is uniform — it means the acceleration is uniform, i.e. constant in magnitude and direction. Since acceleration is the velocity gained per second, holding it constant forces the velocity to change by the same amount in every equal interval of time: +2 m s⁻¹ in the 1st second, +2 m s⁻¹ in the 2nd, and so on. That equal-changes-in-equal-times signature is the defining behaviour, and it is what makes the v–t graph a straight line.
Formula a = Δv/Δt = constant (m s⁻²) ⟺ Δv = a·Δt is the same for every equal Δt. If a is fixed, then for any two equal time slices Δt the product a·Δt is identical, so the velocity increments are identical. Consequence: v = u + at is linear in t — a straight-line v–t graph of slope a.

2. Rearrange: Δv = a·Δt. With a constant, equal time intervals Δt must give equal velocity changes Δv.e.g. a = 2 m s⁻² gives Δv = 2 m s⁻¹ in every 1 s, without exception.
3. Restated: the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals — exactly option (d).
