1According to Bohr's postulates, an electron in a stationary orbit … (cannot radiate energy while revolving)Stationary orbitsA-POSTULATE🟡 SimilaraCannot radiate energy while revolving
Concept Classical electromagnetism says any accelerating charge must continuously radiate energy. An orbiting electron is accelerating (its direction changes), so classically it should spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻⁸ s and the atom would collapse. Bohr's first postulate rescues atomic stability by asserting that the electron may occupy certain special "stationary" orbits in which it revolves without emitting any radiation, so its energy stays fixed.
Key relation Allowed (stationary) orbits are those obeying mvr = nh/2π; while the electron stays in one, ΔE = 0 so no energy is radiated. Radiation appears only when the electron jumps between two stationary orbits, carrying energy hν = Ehi − Elo.
Solution
1. Classical view: orbiting = accelerating ⇒ radiate ⇒ spiral in ⇒ atom collapses.This contradicts the observed stability of atoms, so a new rule is needed.
2. Bohr's postulate: the electron in a stationary orbit does not radiate energy while revolving.Its energy is constant, so there is nothing to emit until it changes orbit.
3. Hence the correct completion is "cannot radiate energy while revolving" — option (a).
⚡ JEE Trick Read "stationary orbit" as "non-radiating, fixed-energy orbit." Any option claiming the electron radiates, gains, or loses energy while staying in the same orbit is wrong by definition of a stationary state.
Answer: (a) Cannot radiate energy while revolving.

Concept In Bohr's model the allowed orbit radii are not arbitrary — they grow in the fixed proportion 1 : 4 : 9 : 16 …, i.e. as n². The first-orbit radius a₀ (the Bohr radius) is supplied in metres here, so the answer must also come out in metres. The classic trap is scaling linearly (2a₀ for the 2nd orbit) instead of quadratically (4a₀).
Formula rn = a₀ n²/Z radius of the nth stationary orbit of a one-electron species; quantising angular momentum (mvr = nh/2π) and balancing the Coulomb pull against the centripetal need gives r ∝ n²/Z. Data: a₀ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m, n = 2, Z = 1 (hydrogen).
Solution
1. Identify the species and orbit: hydrogen atom (Z = 1), second Bohr orbit (n = 2).With Z = 1 the radius depends only on n², so no charge correction is needed.
2. Write the radius formula for hydrogen: rn = a₀ n² = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ × n² m.
3. Substitute n = 2: r₂ = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ × 2² = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ × 4 = 2.116×10⁻¹⁰ m.Multiplying by 4 shifts 5.29 to 21.16, moving the exponent from 10⁻¹¹ to 10⁻¹⁰.
4. Round to three significant figures: r₂ ≈ 2.12×10⁻¹⁰ m (= 2.12 Å) — option (c).1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m; Bohr orbits for small n are a few ångströms, so the size is sensible.
5. Sanity check the ratio: r₂/r₁ = 4, so the answer must be exactly four Bohr radii — 4 × 5.29 = 21.16, i.e. 2.116×10⁻¹⁰ m ✓.
 n     n²     r = a₀ n² (m)
 1     1      5.29×10⁻¹¹
 2     4      2.116×10⁻¹⁰    <-- answer ≈ 2.12×10⁻¹⁰ m
 3     9      4.76×10⁻¹⁰
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the ladder r₁ : r₂ : r₃ = 1 : 4 : 9 — the 2nd orbit of H is always 4a₀, so the whole sum is 5.29 × 4 = 21.16 done mentally. Trap: the options will include 2a₀ ≈ 1.06×10⁻¹⁰ m for students who scale with n instead of n², and bare a₀ for those who forget to square at all.

3r1(H)=0.529 Å — radius of 3rd orbit of Li²⁺ (Z=3)Radius (H-like ion)watch?v=7q22T98l6nM🟢 Exactb1.587 Å
Concept Li²⁺ has lost two of its three electrons, leaving a single electron around a Z = 3 nucleus — a perfect hydrogen-like ion, so Bohr's radius formula applies directly. Two effects compete: going up to n = 3 stretches the orbit by n² = 9, while the triple nuclear charge contracts every orbit by a factor Z = 3. The net radius is therefore only 9/3 = 3 times the hydrogen first-orbit radius, not 9 times.
Formula rn = r₁(H) × n²/Z the n² comes from angular-momentum quantisation, the 1/Z from the stronger Coulomb attraction of a bigger nuclear charge. Data: r₁(H) = 0.529 Å, n = 3, Z = 3 for Li²⁺.
Solution
1. Check the species is one-electron: Li (Z = 3) minus 2 electrons ⇒ Li²⁺ has exactly 1 electron, so the Bohr formula is valid.The formula fails for multi-electron species — always confirm this first.
2. Form the scaling factor: n²/Z = 3²/3 = 9/3 = 3.n² stretches, Z shrinks; only their ratio matters.
3. Multiply by the base radius: r₃(Li²⁺) = 0.529 × 3 = 1.587 Å.
4. Cross-check in metres: 1.587 Å = 1.587×10⁻¹⁰ m — bigger than a₀ (orbit 3) yet smaller than H's own r₃ = 4.76 Å because Z = 3 pulls the electron in. This matches option (b).
5. Extra check: because n = Z here, the multiplier n²/Z collapses to n = 3, so the radius is exactly three Bohr radii — a pattern worth remembering (whenever n = Z, r = 0.529 × n Å) ✓.
 species   n    Z    n²/Z      r = 0.529 × n²/Z
 H         1    1    1         0.529 Å
 Li²⁺      3    3    9/3 = 3   1.587 Å    <-- answer
 H         3    1    9         4.761 Å  (for contrast)
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever n equals Z (here both 3), the ratio n²/Z collapses to n, so r = 0.529 × 3 instantly. Trap: forgetting the 1/Z and reporting 0.529 × 9 = 4.76 Å — that distractor is nearly always planted among the options for hydrogen-like-ion radius questions.
Answer: (b) 1.587 Å.

Concept The Bohr radius grows as n² but shrinks as the nuclear charge Z pulls the electron in, so it depends on the ratio n²/Z. A highly charged ion in a high orbit can therefore have exactly the same radius as hydrogen in some lower orbit. Setting the two radii equal turns the problem into a simple algebraic equation for the unknown n of hydrogen.
Formula rn = 0.529 × n²/Z Å (Z = nuclear charge, n = orbit number). For hydrogen Z = 1, so rn(H) = 0.529 n². The 0.529 Å factor cancels when we equate two such radii, leaving just n²/Z terms.
Solution
1. Radius of the 4th orbit of Be³⁺ (Z = 4): r₄ = 0.529 × 4²/4 = 0.529 × 16/4 = 0.529 × 4 = 2.116 Å.n² = 16 and dividing by Z = 4 leaves a factor of 4.
2. Radius of the nth orbit of H (Z = 1): rₙ = 0.529 × n².
3. Set them equal: 0.529 n² = 2.116 = 0.529 × 4.Both sides share the 0.529 factor, so it divides out.
4. Cancel 0.529: n² = 4 ⇒ n = 2.

5H absorbs energy n=2→n=5, then returns directly to n=2 — nature of emitted radiationEnergy transitionswatch?v=zpx5m5IVp1w🟡 Similarb(ii) & (iii) — falls in visible region & single wavelength
Concept When an electron is excited from n = 2 to n = 5 it absorbs one fixed quantum of energy. If it then falls directly back from n = 5 to n = 2 in a single jump, it re-emits that whole quantum as one photon of a single wavelength. Because the transition ends on n = 2 it belongs to the Balmer series, which lies in the visible region.
Key relation ΔE = E₅ − E₂ and λ = hc/ΔE; a direct 5→2 drop is one transition ⇒ one wavelength. Series ending at n = 2 (Balmer) = visible light; number of distinct wavelengths = number of downward jumps taken.
Solution
1. Excitation n = 2 → 5 absorbs energy ΔE = E₅ − E₂ (a single fixed amount).
2. Direct return 5 → 2 is one jump, so exactly one photon of one wavelength is emitted.A single transition can produce only a single spectral line.
3. The emission terminates at n = 2 ⇒ Balmer series ⇒ visible region.Balmer (nfinal = 2) lines fall in visible light; Lyman (n = 1) is UV.
4. So the radiation is (ii) in the visible region and (iii) of a single wavelength ⇒ option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Number of spectral lines = number of downward transitions; a direct return is always one line. Landing on n = 2 ⇒ Balmer ⇒ visible; landing on n = 1 ⇒ Lyman ⇒ UV. Trap: "returns directly" rules out the multi-line answer that a step-by-step cascade would give.
Answer: (b) (ii) & (iii) — falls in visible region & single wavelength.

1Explain / write the postulates of Bohr's atomic theoryBohr postulatesA-POSTULATE🟡 Similar—
Concept Bohr (1913) grafted the quantum idea onto Rutherford's nuclear atom to explain why atoms are stable and why hydrogen's spectrum is a set of sharp lines. His model rests on a small set of postulates that fix which orbits are allowed and how light is emitted or absorbed.
Postulates
1. The electron revolves around the nucleus only in certain fixed circular orbits ("stationary states / energy levels") of definite energy.
2. In these orbits the electron does not radiate energy, so the atom is stable (breaking the classical prediction).
3. Only orbits for which the angular momentum is quantised are allowed: mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3, …n is the principal quantum number; angular momentum comes only in whole multiples of h/2π.
4. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between two orbits, as a photon of ΔE = E2 − E1 = hν.Down-jump ⇒ emission; up-jump ⇒ absorption; this gives the line spectrum.
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the four in order: fixed orbits → no radiation → mvr = nh/2π → ΔE = hν on jumps. The quantisation of angular momentum (postulate 3) is the one examiners most often ask you to state exactly.
Key point: electrons occupy fixed, non-radiating orbits of quantised angular momentum (mvr = nh/2π), and light is emitted/absorbed only on transitions with ΔE = hν.
2What is Bohr radius?Bohr radiusA-RADIUS🟡 Similar—
Concept The Bohr radius is the radius of the first (n = 1) orbit of the hydrogen atom — the smallest, innermost allowed orbit. It is a fundamental constant of atomic physics and the natural unit of length for all Bohr-model radii, because every other orbit's radius is a simple multiple of it.
Key relation a0 = r1(H) = 0.529 Å = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m, and in general rn = a0 · n²/Z. Set n = 1 and Z = 1 (hydrogen) and rₙ collapses to a₀.
Solution
1. Put n = 1, Z = 1 into rₙ = 0.529 n²/Z ⇒ r₁(H) = 0.529 × 1/1 = 0.529 Å.
2. This value, 0.529 Å = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m = 52.9 pm, is defined as the Bohr radius a₀.
3. All other radii are stated relative to it: rₙ = a₀ n²/Z.e.g. the 2nd orbit of H is 4a₀; the 1st orbit of He⁺ is a₀/2.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise a₀ = 0.529 Å = 5.29×10⁻¹¹ m — it is the anchor for every radius numerical. Trap: the Bohr radius is specifically the hydrogen first orbit (Z = 1); for an ion the first-orbit radius is a₀/Z, not a₀.
Key point: the Bohr radius is the radius of the first orbit of the hydrogen atom, a₀ = 0.529 Å (5.29×10⁻¹¹ m); all Bohr radii follow rₙ = a₀ n²/Z.
3Summarise the results of Bohr's theoryResults of Bohr's theoryA-BOHRMODEL🟡 Similar—
Concept Solving Bohr's postulates for the hydrogen atom yields compact formulas for the size, speed, and energy of each orbit and — most importantly — a successful explanation of the hydrogen line spectrum. These "results" are what made the model famous.
Key results
1. Orbit radius: rn = 0.529 n²/Z Å — radius grows as n², shrinks as Z.
2. Electron energy: En = −13.6 Z²/n² eV — negative (bound) and quantised; least negative (loosest) for large n.
3. Velocity: vn ∝ Z/n — the electron is fastest in the innermost orbit.
4. Spectral lines: a jump n₂ → n₁ emits a photon whose wavenumber obeys the Rydberg relation, correctly reproducing the Lyman/Balmer/… series of hydrogen.This quantitative match with experiment was Bohr's greatest triumph.
⚡ JEE Trick Keep the three scalings on hand: r ∝ n²/Z, E ∝ −Z²/n², v ∝ Z/n. Almost every Bohr numerical is one of these three formulas plugged in. Trap: energy carries a minus sign — a more negative Eₙ means a more tightly bound electron.
Key point: Bohr's theory gives rₙ = 0.529 n²/Z Å, Eₙ = −13.6 Z²/n² eV, vₙ ∝ Z/n, and quantitatively explains the hydrogen line spectrum via ΔE = hν.
4What are the limitations / demerits of Bohr's model?Limitations of BohrA-BOHRLIMIT🟡 Similar—
Concept Bohr's model works beautifully for one-electron (hydrogen-like) systems but fails the moment more electrons or finer detail are involved, because it mixes classical orbits with an ad-hoc quantisation rule and ignores wave nature. Knowing where it breaks down is a standard board/JEE question.
Limitations
1. Applies only to hydrogen / one-electron ions (He⁺, Li²⁺…); it cannot explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms.It ignores electron–electron repulsion.
2. Cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines, nor the Zeeman (magnetic-field) and Stark (electric-field) splitting.
3. Violates Heisenberg's uncertainty principle by assigning the electron a definite orbit (fixed r and v at once).
4. Treats the electron as a particle only, ignoring its wave (dual) nature; gives no account of line intensities or of chemical bonding / orbital shapes.
⚡ JEE Trick One-line summary: Bohr = "one electron only, no fine structure, no uncertainty, no wave nature." Any option claiming it explains multi-electron spectra or orbital shapes is a limitation it does NOT overcome. Trap: it does work for He⁺ and Li²⁺ (they are one-electron), so those are not counter-examples.
Key point: Bohr's model is limited to one-electron systems and fails to explain multi-electron spectra, fine structure, Zeeman/Stark effects, the uncertainty principle, and the electron's wave nature.

2Relationship for angular momentum in Bohr's model (mvr = nh/2π)Angular momentumA-ANGMOM🟡 Similarcmvr = nh/2π
Concept Bohr's quantisation rule is that only orbits with certain angular momenta are allowed — the angular momentum must be a whole-number multiple of h/2π. This single condition is what selects the discrete Bohr orbits out of the classical continuum.
Key relation mvr = n·(h/2π), where m = electron mass, v = speed, r = orbit radius, n = 1, 2, 3… and h = Planck's constant. mvr is the angular momentum of a particle moving in a circle; setting it to n·h/2π quantises it.
Solution
1. Angular momentum of the orbiting electron = m·v·r.
2. Bohr allows only values that are integer multiples of h/2π: m v r = n h/2π.n is the principal quantum number labelling the orbit.
3. This matches option (c): mvr = nh/2π.
⚡ JEE Trick Recognise h/2π (also written ℏ) as the quantum of angular momentum. Options with h alone, h/π, or mvr = nh are distractors. Trap: it is the angular momentum (mvr) that is quantised, not the energy or radius directly.
Answer: (c) mvr = nh/2π.

4The force on the electron in Bohr's orbit is balanced by … (centrifugal)Force balanceA-POSTULATE🟡 SimilarcCentrifugal force
Concept For the electron to stay in a stable circular orbit, the inward electrostatic (Coulomb) attraction from the nucleus must be exactly balanced. In the rotating-frame description used at this level, that inward pull is balanced by the outward centrifugal force arising from the electron's circular motion.
Key relation Coulomb attraction = centrifugal force, i.e. kZe²/r² = mv²/r. The Coulomb term supplies the centripetal requirement; balancing it with the equal, opposite centrifugal term keeps the orbit steady.
Solution
1. The nucleus attracts the electron inward with Coulomb force kZe²/r².
2. A stable circular orbit needs this balanced by the outward centrifugal force mv²/r.Equal magnitude, opposite direction ⇒ the radius stays constant.
3. Hence the balancing force is the centrifugal force — option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick In Bohr numericals the working equation is always "Coulomb = centrifugal" (kZe²/r² = mv²/r); it is the source of the radius and velocity formulas. Trap: gravitational or magnetic forces are negligible here — the balance is purely electrostatic vs centrifugal.
Answer: (c) Centrifugal force.

Concept Bohr's model assumes a single electron orbiting a nucleus with no electron–electron repulsion to complicate things. It therefore succeeds only for hydrogen and hydrogen-like (one-electron) species — H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺ — and fails for any atom or ion with two or more electrons.

Solution
1. The model's equations are derived for one electron around a nucleus of charge +Ze.
2. Species that fit: H (Z=1), He⁺ (Z=2), Li²⁺ (Z=3)… — all one-electron = hydrogen-like.
3. Multi-electron atoms (He, Li, …) are outside its scope, so the answer is hydrogen-like atoms — option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick "Hydrogen-like" = "one electron, any Z." He⁺ and Li²⁺ still obey Bohr; neutral He does not. Trap: don't restrict the model to hydrogen alone — it covers all one-electron ions via the Z in the formulas.
Answer: (c) Hydrogen-like atoms.
