1The geometrical meaning of the derivative at a point is? (slope of the tangent) (Level-1)Derivative = tangent slopeDerivative as slope of a tangent line (Khan) · Derivative: slope of the tangent line🟢 ExactcSlope of the tangent
Concept The derivative has two faces. Analytically it is the instantaneous rate of change of y with respect to x; geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line drawn to the curve at that point. The two are the same number because the tangent is defined as the limiting position of a secant, and the secant's slope is exactly the average rate Δy/Δx. This question asks for the geometric face — and every wrong option (area, normal, the y-value itself) belongs to some other chapter of calculus.
Formula f′(a) = lim(Δx→0) [f(a + Δx) − f(a)]/Δx = tanθ, where θ is the inclination of the tangent at the point (a, f(a)). The bracket is the secant slope between two nearby points; the limit slides the second point into the first, turning the secant into the tangent. Units: (unit of y)/(unit of x) — on an x–t graph the tangent slope is a velocity in m s⁻¹.
Solution
1. Fix the point P(a, f(a)) on the curve and take a neighbour Q(a + Δx, f(a + Δx)).
2. The secant PQ has slope Δy/Δx = [f(a + Δx) − f(a)]/Δx — the average rate over the interval.Rise over run for the chord joining the two points.
3. Shrink Δx → 0: Q slides along the curve into P and the secant pivots into the tangent at P.
4. The limiting slope is the derivative: f′(a) = slope of the tangent = tanθ.This limit is simultaneously the definition of f′(a) and of the tangent's slope — hence the geometric meaning.
5. Eliminate the distractors: the area under the curve is the integral (anti-derivative), the normal has slope −1/f′(a), and the value f(a) is just the height of the point — none is f′(a).
6. Physics reading: on an x–t graph the tangent slope at an instant is the instantaneous velocity; on a v–t graph it is the acceleration.

2If y = x⁵, find dy/dx at x = 2 (80) (Level-2)Power rule + evaluateDerivative & its value at a given x · Power rule made easy🟡 Similar80
Concept A derivative is a function, not a number. Differentiating y = x⁵ gives a new function dy/dx = 5x⁴ that reports the slope of the curve at every x. To get the slope at one particular point you must do two separate operations in the right order: first differentiate, then substitute. Reversing them (putting x = 2 into y first) gives y = 32, a constant, whose derivative is 0 — the classic blunder this question is built to catch.
Formula Power rule: d/dx (xⁿ) = n·x⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ for any real n. Bring the exponent down as a multiplier, then reduce the exponent by one. The value at a point is written (dy/dx)|x=2 — the vertical bar means "evaluated at". Here x and y are pure numbers (no physical units), so dy/dx is dimensionless; if x were a time in s and y a length in m, dy/dx would carry m s⁻¹.
Solution
1. Identify the exponent: y = x⁵, so n = 5.The base is x itself, so the plain power rule applies — no chain rule needed.
2. Apply the power rule — bring 5 down in front and drop the exponent to 5 − 1 = 4:d/dx(x⁵) = 5·x⁽⁵⁻¹⁾.
3. dy/dx = 5x⁴ — this is the slope function, valid at every x.
4. Now substitute x = 2 into the derivative (not into y): dy/dx|x=2 = 5 × (2)⁴.
5. Evaluate the power first: (2)⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.Exponent before multiplication — order of operations.
6. Multiply: 5 × 16 = 80.
7. Interpret: at the point (2, 32) the tangent to y = x⁵ rises 80 units of y per 1 unit of x — a very steep curve.

Substituting the number early turns your function into a constant, and the derivative of any constant is 0 — an answer of 0 on this type of question is the tell-tale sign you swapped the order. Speed tip: for y = xⁿ evaluated at x = a the answer is just n·a⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ — here 5 × 2⁴ = 80 in one line.

3If s = ∛t, then ds/dt is? ((1/3)t^(−2/3)) (Level-3)Fractional-power ruleDerivative of cube root x · Differentiating fractional powers of x🟢 Exact (t for x)b(1/3)t^(−2/3)
Concept A cube root is just a fractional power in disguise: ∛t = t^(1/3). Once it is rewritten that way, the ordinary power rule applies with n = 1/3 — the rule works for every real exponent, not only whole numbers. The entire difficulty of the question is the exponent arithmetic: subtracting 1 from 1/3 gives −2/3, and getting that fraction wrong (or its sign) produces every one of the wrong options.
Formula Power rule: d/dt (tⁿ) = n·t⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ for any real n. Radical conversion: ∛t = t^(1/3), and t⁻ᵏ = 1/tᵏ for rewriting the result. A root is a fractional exponent (index in the denominator), so the power rule can see it. If s were a distance in m and t a time in s, ds/dt would carry m s⁻¹ — here both are pure numbers.
Solution
1. Rewrite the radical as a power: s = ∛t = t^(1/3), so n = 1/3.The power rule cannot act on the ∛ symbol directly — convert first, always.
2. Apply the power rule: ds/dt = (1/3)·t^(1/3 − 1).
3. Do the exponent subtraction carefully: 1/3 − 1 = 1/3 − 3/3 = −2/3.This is where most errors happen — the new exponent is negative.
4. So ds/dt = (1/3)·t^(−2/3) — exactly option (b).
5. Optional tidy form: (1/3)t^(−2/3) = 1/(3t^(2/3)) = 1/(3·∛(t²)).Negative exponent means reciprocal; the 1/3 stays in front.
6. Numerical check at t = 8: exact value (1/3)·8^(−2/3) = (1/3)·(1/4) = 1/12 ≈ 0.0833. Finite difference: [∛8.001 − ∛8]/0.001 = (2.0000833 − 2)/0.001 ≈ 0.0833 ✓ — the formula matches the curve's actual slope.

4If tanθ = dy/dx and dy = 0.4, dx = 0.2, find θ in degrees (63.4°) (Level-4)Slope → angle (m = tanθ)Inclination of a line — m = tan θ · How slope equals tangent of an angle🟡 Similar63.4°
Concept The slope of a line and the angle it makes with the +x axis are two ways of saying the same thing, linked by m = tanθ. This matters constantly in physics: the inclination of a v–t graph is the acceleration, so reading an angle off a graph lets you recover a rate. Here the "rise" dy and the "run" dx are given directly, so we form the slope as their ratio and then invert the tangent to recover θ.
Formula tanθ = dy/dx = rise ÷ run, hence θ = tan⁻¹(dy/dx). tanθ is the ratio of the opposite side (rise) to the adjacent side (run) of the slope triangle, so the arctangent undoes it and returns the inclination. dy and dx carry the same unit here (both are graph increments), so their ratio is dimensionless — as it must be, since you can only take tan⁻¹ of a pure number. θ emerges in degrees with the calculator in DEG mode.
Solution
1. List the given increments: dy = 0.4, dx = 0.2 (same units, so the ratio is a pure number).
2. Form the slope: tanθ = dy ÷ dx = 0.4 ÷ 0.2.Slope is defined as rise over run — divide, never subtract.
3. Divide: 0.4 ÷ 0.2 = 2 (dimensionless).0.4/0.2 = 4/2 = 2; the units of dy and dx cancel.
4. Invert the tangent: θ = tan⁻¹(2).We know tanθ and want θ, so apply the inverse function to both sides.
5. Evaluate with the calculator in degrees: tan⁻¹(2) = 63.43° ≈ 63.4°.
6. Sanity check: tan 45° = 1 and tan 60° = 1.73, so a slope of 2 must give an angle just above 60° — 63.4° fits.tanθ increases with θ, so a larger slope means a larger angle.
7. The slope is positive, so θ lies in the first quadrant (0° < θ < 90°) — the line rises left-to-right.

5If y = √x + x^(3/2) + x⁵, then dy/dx is? (1/(2√x) + (3/2)√x + 5x⁴) (Level-5)Radical / fractional powersDifferentiating fractional powers of x · Derivative of √x🟡 Similarb1/(2√x) + (3/2)√x + 5x⁴
Concept The power rule is not restricted to whole-number exponents — it holds for every real n, including fractions. The only skill needed here is rewriting every radical as a fractional power before differentiating, because √x is invisible to the power rule until you see it as x^(1/2). The sum rule then lets us handle the three terms completely independently and add the results.
Formula Power rule: d/dx (xⁿ) = n·x⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ for any real n. Sum rule: d/dx (u + v + w) = du/dx + dv/dx + dw/dx. Differentiation is linear, so a sum differentiates term by term — no cross terms are ever produced. Radical conversion: √x = x^(1/2) and 1/xᵏ = x⁻ᵏ. All quantities here are pure numbers (no physical units).
Solution
1. Rewrite every term as a pure power of x: y = x^(1/2) + x^(3/2) + x⁵.√x = x^(1/2) by the definition of a fractional exponent; the other two are already powers.
2. Apply the sum rule — differentiate each of the three terms separately.
3. Term 1: d/dx(x^(1/2)) = (1/2)·x^(1/2 − 1) = (1/2)·x^(−1/2).Bring 1/2 down, subtract 1: 1/2 − 2/2 = −1/2.
4. Convert term 1 back to radical form: (1/2)x^(−1/2) = 1/(2·x^(1/2)) = 1/(2√x).A negative exponent means a reciprocal: x^(−1/2) = 1/√x.
5. Term 2: d/dx(x^(3/2)) = (3/2)·x^(3/2 − 1) = (3/2)·x^(1/2) = (3/2)√x.3/2 − 1 = 3/2 − 2/2 = 1/2, and x^(1/2) = √x.
6. Term 3: d/dx(x⁵) = 5·x^(5−1) = 5x⁴.
7. Add the three results: dy/dx = 1/(2√x) + (3/2)√x + 5x⁴.
8. Domain note: the first two terms require x > 0.√x is real only for x ≥ 0, and 1/(2√x) blows up at x = 0 — the tangent there is vertical.

1Which quadrant has a positive slope for a line? (First)Slope sign vs quadrantInclination of a line — m = tan θ · Why tan is ± in different quadrants🟡 SimilaraFirst
Concept The sign of a line's slope is fixed by the quadrant in which its inclination angle θ lies, because slope = tanθ and the tangent function changes sign from quadrant to quadrant. A line whose inclination is in the first quadrant (0° < θ < 90°) rises as you move right, so both its slope and tanθ are positive. This is the graphical basis for reading physics graphs: a positive-slope v–t line means positive acceleration.
Formula slope m = tanθ = Δy/Δx, where θ is measured anticlockwise from the +x axis. Δy and Δx carry the same graph units, so m is dimensionless for same-unit axes; on a v–t graph m = Δv/Δt instead carries m s⁻². The sign of tanθ by quadrant follows the ASTC rule: All positive in Q1, Sine only in Q2, Tangent in Q3, Cosine in Q4.
Solution
1. Write the slope as a ratio of sides: tanθ = Δy/Δx (rise over run).The sign of the ratio is decided by the signs of the rise and the run.
2. First quadrant, 0° < θ < 90°: moving right gives Δx > 0, and the line rises so Δy > 0.
3. Therefore tanθ = (+)/(+) = positive — the slope is positive in the first quadrant.
4. Second quadrant, 90° < θ < 180°: the line falls to the right, so Δy < 0 while Δx > 0 ⇒ tanθ = (−)/(+) = negative.e.g. tan 135° = −1, a line falling at 45°.
5. Third quadrant (180°–270°): both Δy and Δx are negative ⇒ tanθ = (−)/(−) = positive again — but a line's inclination is conventionally reported only over 0°–180°, so Q1 is the intended answer.
6. Fourth quadrant (270°–360°): Δy < 0, Δx > 0 ⇒ tanθ = negative.
7. Conclusion: within the standard 0°–180° range for a line's inclination, only the first quadrant gives a positive slope.

2The secant to a curve intersects it at how many points? (2)Secant definitionSecant lines and tangent lines (Calculus 1)🟢 Exactb2
Concept A secant is, by definition, a straight line that cuts a curve at two distinct points. It is the geometric object behind the average rate of change: the slope of the secant through P and Q is Δy/Δx over that interval. Contrast it with the tangent, which touches at (locally) one point and encodes the instantaneous rate. The whole architecture of differentiation is the passage from the two-point secant to the one-point tangent, so knowing "secant = 2 points" is not vocabulary trivia — it is the starting post of the derivative's definition.
Formula Secant through P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂): slope = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) = Δy/Δx. Two distinct points are needed to fix a line and to form this ratio — that is why the count is exactly 2. Tangent = limit of the secant as Q → P. Units: (unit of y)/(unit of x); on an x–t graph the secant slope is the average velocity in m s⁻¹.
Solution
1. Recall the definition: a secant (from Latin secare, "to cut") is a line that cuts through the curve.
2. Cutting through requires two distinct intersection points P and Q on the curve — that pair defines the line completely.One point cannot fix a unique line; two do.
3. Its slope uses both points: Δy/Δx = [f(x₂) − f(x₁)]/(x₂ − x₁) — the average rate of change between them.
4. Compare the candidates: 1 point describes a tangent (touching, not cutting); 2 points is the secant ✓; 3 or more is not part of the definition.A long secant may happen to re-cross a wiggly curve again, but the definition — and the slope formula — uses exactly two.
5. Connection to calculus: as the two points merge (Δx → 0), the secant becomes the tangent and its slope Δy/Δx becomes dy/dx.
6. Physics reading: on an x–t curve, the secant joining two instants gives the average velocity over the interval; the tangent at one instant gives the instantaneous velocity.

3What does the notation dy/dx represent? (a derivative / rate of change)Derivative notationdy/dx, d/dx and dy/dt — derivative notations🟢 ExactcA derivative or rate of change
Concept dy/dx is Leibniz's notation for the derivative — the instantaneous rate of change of y with respect to x. Although it is written like a fraction, it is not "d times y over d times x": it is a single indivisible symbol meaning "the limit of Δy/Δx as Δx → 0". The fraction-like shape is deliberate and useful (it remembers its own units and behaves well in the chain rule), but the object itself is a limit, i.e. a derivative.
Formula dy/dx = lim(Δx→0) Δy/Δx = lim(Δx→0) [f(x + Δx) − f(x)]/Δx. Δy/Δx is a genuine ratio of finite changes; dy/dx is its limiting value — the instantaneous rate. Units: (unit of y)/(unit of x). If y is position in m and x is time in s, then dy/dx is a velocity in m s⁻¹ — the notation displays its own units, one of its great virtues.
Solution
1. Parse the symbol: "d" marks an infinitesimally small change, so dy/dx reads "the change in y per change in x, in the limit of a vanishing change".
2. That limit is precisely the definition of the derivative: dy/dx = f′(x) — a rate of change, and geometrically the tangent slope.Same quantity, three names: Leibniz dy/dx, Lagrange f′(x), and "instantaneous rate".
3. Rule out "a fraction of two numbers": d is not a factor that can be cancelled; "dy ÷ dx" as ordinary division is meaningless until the limit is taken.You cannot divide two quantities that are each tending to zero — only their ratio has a limit.
4. Rule out "Δy/Δx": that is the average rate over a finite interval (secant slope); dy/dx is its Δx → 0 limit (tangent slope).
5. Worked check: y = x², Δy/Δx = 2x + Δx (finite, average); dy/dx = lim = 2x (derivative) — clearly a rate, not a static number.
6. Physics reading: v = dx/dt, a = dv/dt, i = dq/dt — every instantaneous rate in physics wears this notation.

⚡ JEE Trick Read dy/dx as one word: "the derivative of y with respect to x". Trap: never cancel the d's or treat dy/dx as literal division of two finite numbers — Δ is finite, d is a limit.
Answer: (c) — dy/dx represents a derivative, the instantaneous rate of change of y with respect to x.
4What does Df(x) mean in operator notation? (derivative of f)Operator notationDerivative notations — know the difference · Derivative notations in calculus🟡 Similarcdx of f ⚠
Concept Calculus has several rival notations for the same operation, and exam questions test whether you can read all of them. D is the differential operator: a symbol that acts on a function and hands back its derivative. So Df(x) is nothing more than f′(x) = dy/dx written in Euler's compact operator style — the notation changes, the mathematics does not.
Formula Df(x) ≡ d/dx [f(x)] ≡ f′(x) ≡ dy/dx — four names for one quantity, the rate of change of f with respect to x. D is an operator, not a number: it must always be given a function to act on, so "D" alone or "D × f" is meaningless. Units: if f is a displacement in m and x a time in s, then Df carries m s⁻¹ — the operator always divides the output unit by the input unit.
Solution
1. Read D as the instruction "differentiate what follows with respect to the independent variable".It is a verb, not a quantity — it does something to a function.
2. Apply it to f(x): Df(x) means d/dx of f(x), i.e. the derivative of f.
3. So Df(x) = f′(x) = dy/dx — all identical in meaning and in value at every x.
4. Higher orders stack the operator: D²f(x) = d²f/dx² = f″(x), the second derivative.D² means "apply D twice", not "square the function".
5. Worked check: if f(x) = x³, then Df(x) = 3x² and D²f(x) = 6x.
6. Physics reading: with x(t) as position, Dx = v (velocity, m s⁻¹) and D²x = a (acceleration, m s⁻²).Each application of D divides by one more power of the time unit.

5What happens to a secant line as Δx → 0? (turns into a tangent)Secant → tangent limitThe limit as secant becomes tangent🟢 ExactbIt turns into a tangent
Concept The tangent line is manufactured from secants by a limit. Hold one point P of the curve fixed, let the second point Q sit a horizontal distance Δx away, and watch the secant PQ as Δx shrinks: Q slides along the curve toward P, the secant pivots about P, and in the limit Δx → 0 it settles into a single limiting line — the tangent at P. This is not a metaphor; it is the literal definition of the tangent in calculus, and the slope of that limiting line is the derivative f′(x).
