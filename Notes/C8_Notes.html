★ Core ideaOxidation and reduction are reciprocal — they always occur together. The oxidant is reduced; the reductant is oxidised. LEO says GER: Loss of Electrons = Oxidation, Gain of Electrons = Reduction.

Rules for assigning oxidation number (O.N.)

  • Free element = 0 (H₂, N₂, P₄, O₂, metals).
  • H = +1 (−1 in metal hydrides, NaH). Alkali metals = +1, alkaline-earth = +2, F always −1.
  • O = −2 usually; −1 in peroxides (–O–O–), +2 in OF₂, −(1/2) in superoxides (KO₂).
  • Sum of O.N. in a neutral molecule = 0; in an ion = charge on the ion.
  • O.N. may be integral, fractional (average, e.g. Fe in Fe₃O₄ = +8/3), zero or negative. Max O.N. = group number (F, O are exceptions); min O.N. = group − 8.
  • Special: in CrO₅ (butterfly, 4 peroxo O at −1, one O at −2) Cr = +6; in S₂O₈²⁻ (one peroxide link) S = +6; in Na₂S₂ (persulphide) S = −1.

Electrode potential & electrochemical series

cell & sign conventionE°cell = E°(oxidation, anode) + E°(reduction, cathode) · E°(oxidation) = −E°(reduction) · SHE assigned 0.000 V
  • In the electrochemical series (reduction potentials): more −ve E° = stronger reducing agent, more active metal (top: Li, K, Ca…). More +ve E° = stronger oxidising agent (bottom: F₂, Cl₂, Au…).
  • A metal higher in the series displaces one lower from solution; metals above H₂ liberate H₂ from dilute acid.
★ Golden workflowMost redox numericals reduce to: (1) assign O.N. → find what is oxidised/reduced; (2) write & balance half-reactions (mass and charge); (3) apply M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ or mole-ratio. Always balance in both mass and charge.

Oxidation–Reduction (Classical) & Oxidation Number

Classical definitions

  • Oxidation = addition of oxygen or any electronegative radical, OR removal of hydrogen or any electropositive radical. e.g. C + O₂ → CO₂ ; 2FeCl₂ + Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃ ; H₂S + Br₂ → 2HBr + S ; 2KI + H₂O₂ → 2KOH + I₂.
  • Reduction = addition of hydrogen / electropositive radical, OR removal of oxygen / electronegative radical. e.g. 2HgCl₂ + SnCl₂ → Hg₂Cl₂ + SnCl₄ ; CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O ; 2FeCl₃ + H₂ → 2FeCl₂ + 2HCl.
  • Redox is reciprocal: if one substance is oxidised the other is reduced simultaneously. An oxidant, after oxidising the other, is itself reduced; a reductant is itself oxidised.

Oxidation number & oxidation state

  • Oxidation number = the charge (real or apparent) an atom carries in a species; positive if the atom donates electrons, negative if it accepts. e.g. in FeCl₃, Fe gives up 3e⁻ → O.N. of Fe = +3, each Cl = −1.
  • Oxidation state = oxidation number per atom (in KMnO₄, Mn is in the +7 state).
valency vs oxidation numbervalency = combining capacity (always ≥ 0, usually a fixed integer) ; O.N. = signed charge (may be +, −, zero, integral or fractional)
Rules used to compute O.N. (full list)
(1) free state → 0. (2) H = +1 (−1 in metal hydrides); group-1 metals +1, group-2 +2; O = −2 (−1 peroxide, +2 OF₂, −(1/2) superoxide); F always −1; halogens −1 in halides; S = −2 in sulphides. (3) Σ O.N. in a molecule = 0. (4) Σ O.N. in a polyatomic ion = net charge. (5) O.N. may be a +/− integer or fraction (an element in two different states gives a fractional average). (6) max O.N. = group number (F, O excepted); (7) min O.N. = group − 8 (metals excepted). (8) transition & p-block elements show variable O.N. (9) in amalgams & metal carbonyls the metal O.N. is 0. (10) in FeSO₄·NO (brown-ring), NO is +1 and Fe is +1. (11) in persulphides (Na₂S₂) S = −1. (12) in CrO₅ (butterfly) Cr = +6. (13) in K₃CrO₈ Cr = +5 (tetraperoxo complex).
⚠ TrapFractional O.N. is an average. In Fe₃O₄ the "+8/3" means a mixture of Fe²⁺ and 2 Fe³⁺; in Na₂S₄O₆ the "+2.5" averages two S at 0 and two S at +5.

Electronic Concept, Oxidant/Reductant & Types of Redox

Electronic concept

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons (species becomes more positive): Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ ; Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ ; 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
  • Reduction = gain of electrons (more negative): Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ ; Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺ ; S + 2e⁻ → S²⁻.
  • Oxidising agent accepts electrons (Sn⁴⁺, F₂); reducing agent loses electrons (Hg⁺, Fe²⁺, 2Cl⁻).
TermElectron changeO.N. change
Oxidation / substance oxidised / reducing agentloses e⁻increases
Reduction / substance reduced / oxidising agentgains e⁻decreases
💡 Important agentsCommon oxidants: O₂, O₃, H₂O₂, Cl₂, conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄, KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇. Common reductants: H₂, C, CO, HBr/HI, H₂S, SO₂, SnCl₂, metals. H₂O₂ and SO₂ act as both.

Types of redox reaction

  • Combination — elements/compounds unite with O.N. change: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O ; 3Mg + N₂ → Mg₃N₂ ; H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.
  • Decomposition — reverse of combination; at least one product in elemental form: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ ; 2NaH → 2Na + H₂. (Note: 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ changes O but K is unchanged; not every decomposition is redox, e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ is not.)
  • Displacement — metal displacement (stronger reductant displaces weaker: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu) and non-metal displacement (reactivity F > Cl > Br > I: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂; active metals displace H₂ from water/acid, reactivity Fe < Zn < Mg).
  • Disproportionation — one element in an intermediate state is simultaneously oxidised and reduced (see Memorize card).
⚠ Noble metalsAg and Au do not displace H₂ even from mineral acids (they lie below H in the series).

Balancing Redox Reactions — Oxidation-Number Method

Steps

  • (1) Identify atoms whose O.N. changes; find the increase/decrease per atom × number of atoms. (2) Multiply the oxidant & reductant by integers so total increase = total decrease. (3) Balance all atoms other than H and O. (4) Balance O by adding H₂O; (5) then balance H.
  • Acidic medium: balance O with H₂O on the O-deficient side, then add H⁺ to balance H. Basic medium: add H₂O to the H-deficient side and an equal number of OH⁻ on the other side.
worked (basic): MnO₄⁻ + Br⁻ → MnO₂ + BrO₃⁻Mn: +7→+4 (↓3) ; Br: −1→+5 (↑6). LCM balance → 2MnO₄⁻ + Br⁻ → 2MnO₂ + BrO₃⁻ → add H₂O/OH⁻ → 2MnO₄⁻ + Br⁻ + H₂O → 2MnO₂ + BrO₃⁻ + 2OH⁻
💡 No acid/base presentFor FeS₂ + O₂ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂, O cannot be balanced with H₂O. Use net O.N. change: in FeS₂, Fe(+2→+3, ↑1) and 2S(−1→+4, ↑10) → net ↑11; O₂(0→−2, ↓4). Balance 11 vs 4 → 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂.

Balancing Redox Reactions — Ion-Electron (Half-Reaction) Method

Steps

  • (1) Find atoms whose O.N. changes; split into an oxidation and a reduction half-reaction. (2) Balance atoms other than H & O in each half. (3) Add electrons to balance the O.N. change. (4) Balance charge: add H⁺ (acidic) or OH⁻ (basic). (5) Balance H & O with H₂O. (6) Multiply the halves so electrons cancel; add.

Equivalent Weight, Disproportionation & n-factor

Equivalent weight of oxidants & reductants

  • Eq. wt. = molar mass / (electrons gained or lost) = molar mass / (change in O.N. of the element).
  • MnO₄⁻ (acidic, +7→+2): Eq. wt. of KMnO₄ = M/5. Cr₂O₇²⁻ (+6→+3, 6e⁻ for 2 Cr): Eq. wt. of K₂Cr₂O₇ = M/6. Oxalic acid (C₂O₄²⁻ → 2CO₂ + 2e⁻): Eq. wt. of H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O = M/2.

Disproportionation, comproportionation & n-factor

  • Disproportionation — one species both oxidised & reduced: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ ; 4KClO₃ → 3KClO₄ + KCl ; 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH(hot) → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O.
  • Chlorine (−1 to +7): ClO⁻, ClO₂⁻, ClO₃⁻ disproportionate (3ClO⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ ; 6ClO₂⁻ → 4ClO₃⁻ + 2Cl⁻ ; 4ClO₃⁻ → Cl⁻ + 3ClO₄⁻); ClO₄⁻ cannot (+7, highest).
  • Comproportionation is the reverse: 10Br⁻ + 2BrO₃⁻ + 12H⁺ → 6Br₂ + 6H₂O.
n-factor for a disproportionating speciesn-factor = (n₁ × n₂)/(n₁ + n₂). e.g. 3H₃PO₂ → PH₃ + 2H₃PO₃: n₁ = 2 (loss), n₂ = 4 (gain) → n-factor = 8/6 = 4/3 → Eq. wt. of H₃PO₂ = M/(4/3) = 3M/4

Redox Titrations, Stoichiometry & Electrode Potential

Redox titrations & indicators

  • Permanganate titration (acidic): reductants (FeSO₄, Mohr's salt, H₂O₂, oxalic acid/oxalates) are titrated against KMnO₄, which is its own self-indicator (persistent pink end point). MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O ; 2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O.
  • Dichromate titration (acidic): same reductants against K₂Cr₂O₇ (not a self-indicator → internal indicator diphenylamine, or external K₃[Fe(CN)₆]): 6Fe²⁺ + Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H₂O.
  • Iodimetric/iodometric: I₂ as oxidant with starch indicator (blue with I₂): I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.
⚠ Never use HCl in KMnO₄ titrationsHCl is oxidised to Cl₂ by MnO₄⁻, giving a false (high) reading. Use dilute H₂SO₄.
stoichiometry of redox in solutionmolarity equation M₁V₁/n₁ = M₂V₂/n₂ · normality equation N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ · Strength (g/L) = Normality × Eq. wt.

Electrode potential & electrochemical series

  • A metal dipped in its ion solution sets up an electrode potential. Absolute value is unmeasurable → measured vs the SHE (0.000 V). By IUPAC, tabulated values are reduction potentials; E°(oxidation) = −E°(reduction).
  • E°cell = E°(oxidation, anode) + E°(reduction, cathode). For the Zn|Zn²⁺ ‖ H⁺|H₂ cell, E°cell = 0.76 V → E°ox(Zn) = +0.76 V, so E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V.
  • Electrochemical series (reduction potentials): top (Li −3.05, K −2.93, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn −0.76, Fe −0.44 …) = strong reductants/active metals; bottom (Cu +0.34, I₂, Ag +0.80, Br₂, Cl₂ +1.36, Au, F₂ +2.87) = strong oxidants.
  • Uses: metals above H₂ liberate H₂ from dilute acid; a metal higher in the series displaces one lower; oxidising power of non-metals rises down the series (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂).
