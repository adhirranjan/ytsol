1Two soap bubbles of different radii r1 and r2 are in contact. Determine the radius of the interface. (Level-1)Radius of the common interfaceTwo soap bubbles of radii R and r come in contact. R is... 🟡 SimilarR = r1r2/(r2 − r1)
Concept A soap bubble has two free surfaces, so it carries an excess pressure 4T/r over the outside. When two bubbles touch, they share one common film; the pressure difference between the two bubbles must be supported by the curvature of that shared film. Smaller bubble = higher pressure, so the film bulges into the larger bubble.
Formula Pin − Pout = 4T/r for each bubble; for the common film Psmall − Plarge = 4T/R (the shared film is also a soap film with two surfaces, so 4T, not 2T)
Solution
1. Call the radii rs (smaller bubble) and rl (larger bubble). Atmospheric pressure outside = P0, surface tension T is the same for both.
2. Pressure inside the small bubble: Ps = P0 + 4T/rs. Pressure inside the large bubble: Pl = P0 + 4T/rl.
3. Since rs < rl, we get Ps > Pl. The common film separates a higher pressure (small side) from a lower pressure (large side), so it bulges toward the large bubble: it is concave toward the smaller bubble, with its centre of curvature in the larger bubble.
4. Apply the soap-film relation across the interface of radius R: Ps − Pl = 4T/R.
5. Substitute: 4T/rs − 4T/rl = 4T/R. The 4T cancels: 1/R = 1/rs − 1/rl = (rl − rs)/(rsrl).
6. Invert: R = rsrl/(rl − rs). With the problem's labels (r1 the smaller, r2 the larger, matching the printed answer) this is R = r1r2/(r2 − r1).
7. Check: if r1 → r2 the interface becomes flat (R → ∞); for equal bubbles there is no pressure difference, as expected. Numerical check with 3 cm and 4 cm: R = 12/1 = 12 cm.
⚡ JEE Trick Treat the interface exactly like a bubble of its own: 1/R = 1/(small) − 1/(large); the 4T always cancels, so the answer never depends on T or P0.
Answer: R = r1r2/(r2 − r1) (r1 smaller, r2 larger); the film is concave toward the smaller bubble.
2Two soap bubbles have radii R1 and R2 in vacuum. If they coalesce under isothermal conditions, what is the new radius of the bubble, if T is the surface tension of the soap solution? (Level-2)Coalescence of bubbles in vacuumTwo spherical soap bubbles of radii r_1 and r_2 in vacuume coalesce under isothermal condition. 🟡 SimilarR = √(R12 + R22)
Concept In vacuum the outside pressure is zero, so the gas pressure inside a soap bubble is purely the surface-tension excess 4T/R. Isothermal coalescence conserves the gas (Boyle's law applied to the total pV), so the sum of pV before equals pV after.
Formula p = 4T/R (since p0 = 0)   p1V1 + p2V2 = pV (same amount of gas, same temperature, so total pV is conserved)
Solution
Given two soap bubbles of radii R₁ and R₂ in vacuum coalesce under isothermal conditions; surface tension T
Find the new radius of the bubble
1. In vacuum there is no outside pressure, so the pressures are p1 = 4T/R1, p2 = 4T/R2 and, for the new bubble of radius R, p = 4T/R.
2. The volumes are V = (4/3)πR3 for each bubble.
3. The gas is the same before and after and the temperature is constant, so nRT is unchanged: p1V1 + p2V2 = pV.
4. Substitute: (4T/R1)(4/3)πR13 + (4T/R2)(4/3)πR23 = (4T/R)(4/3)πR3.
5. Simplify each term: (16/3)πT R12 + (16/3)πT R22 = (16/3)πT R2.
6. Cancel (16/3)πT: R2 = R12 + R22, so R = √(R12 + R22).
7. Interpretation: with p0 = 0, pV ∝ R2 is proportional to the surface area, so the total surface area is conserved. Check: 3 cm and 4 cm give R = 5 cm.
⚡ JEE Trick In vacuum, pV ∝ R2, so isothermal coalescence conserves surface area: R2 = R12 + R22. In air (p0 ≠ 0) this is NOT true, so do not carry it over.
Answer: R = √(R12 + R22)

Concept A soap bubble is a thin liquid film with an inner and an outer surface, both of tension T. The excess pressure needed to balance both surfaces is 4T/R, twice that of a single-surface liquid drop (2T/R).
Formula ΔP = 4T/R (two surfaces; use R = D/2, a frequent slip is plugging in the diameter)
Solution
Given soap bubble of diameter 10 mm; surface tension 0.04 N/m
Find the excess pressure inside the bubble
1. Given: diameter D = 10 mm, so R = D/2 = 5 mm = 5×10−3 m. Surface tension T = 0.04 N/m.
2. A soap bubble has two free surfaces, so the force balance on a hemisphere gives ΔP·πR2 = 2·(2πR T), i.e. ΔP = 4T/R.
3. Substitute: ΔP = 4 × 0.04 / (5×10−3) = 0.16 / (5×10−3).
4. Divide: 0.16 / 0.005 = 32.
5. Units: (N/m)/m = N/m2 = Pa, so ΔP = 32 N/m2.
6. Sanity check: a single-surface drop would give 2T/R = 16 N/m2; the soap bubble gives double, 32, as it should.
7. Atmospheric pressure is about 105 N/m2, so this excess (32) is tiny: the bubble's inside pressure is practically atmospheric.
⚡ JEE Trick Soap bubble = 4T/R (two surfaces); liquid drop or air bubble inside liquid = 2T/R. Halve the diameter before substituting.

Concept For a large bubble, surface-tension excess pressure is negligible, so the gas pressure equals the surrounding liquid pressure. The temperature is constant, so Boyle's law applies between bottom and surface. Doubling the radius multiplies the volume by 8.
Formula P1V1 = P2V2   Pbottom = P0 + ρgd   V ∝ r3 (radius doubles means volume × 8, not × 2)
Solution
Given large bubble rising from the bottom of a lake to the surface; its radius doubles; atmospheric pressure equals a water column of height H
Find the depth of the lake
1. Atmospheric pressure equals a water column of height H: P0 = ρgH.
2. At the surface the bubble pressure is P2 = P0 = ρgH. At the bottom (depth d) it is P1 = P0 + ρgd = ρg(H + d). Surface-tension excess is neglected for a large bubble.
3. Volume ratio: V2/V1 = (r2/r1)3 = 23 = 8, so V2 = 8V1.
4. Temperature is the same, so P1V1 = P2V2: ρg(H + d)V1 = ρgH × 8V1.
5. Cancel ρg V1: H + d = 8H.
6. Solve: d = 8H − H = 7H.
7. Check: pressure at the bottom is 8 times atmospheric, i.e. 1 atm + 7 atm of water, and 7 atm of water is 7H deep. Consistent.
⚡ JEE Trick Radius × n means volume × n3; the pressure ratio is then n3, so depth = (n3 − 1)H. Here (8 − 1)H = 7H.
Answer: Depth of the lake = 7H
5Two soap bubbles ‘A’ and ‘B’ are blown and connected through a tube system. What will happen if the stop cocks S2, S3 are opened, keeping the stop cock S1 closed? (Level-5)Two connected soap bubblesIf two soap bubbles of different radii are in connected with each other (1) Air flows from larger... 🟡 SimilarThe smaller bubble shrinks and the larger grows (air flows small → large)
Concept Excess pressure in a soap bubble is 4T/r, inversely proportional to radius. The smaller bubble is at the higher pressure. When the two are connected, air moves from high to low pressure, so it flows from the smaller bubble into the larger one.
Formula ΔP = 4T/r   rsmall < rlarge ⇒ Psmall > Plarge (a smaller radius means a larger pressure, the opposite of the intuition that a bigger bubble holds more pressure)
Solution
1. Both bubbles are blown from the same soap solution, so T is the same. Outside pressure P0 is the same for both.
2. Inside pressures: PA = P0 + 4T/rA and PB = P0 + 4T/rB. The bubble with the smaller radius has the larger internal pressure.
3. Stopcock S1 stays closed, so there is no path to the outside. Opening S2 and S3 joins the interiors of A and B through the tube system.
4. Air flows from the higher-pressure region to the lower-pressure one, i.e. from the smaller bubble into the larger bubble.
5. As the smaller bubble loses air its radius decreases, so its pressure 4T/r rises further. The larger bubble gains air, its radius grows and its pressure falls. The pressure difference increases, so the process runs away rather than equalising.
6. This continues until the smaller bubble collapses to a nearly flat film on the tube end (radius of curvature becomes that of the opening), while the larger bubble ends up bigger.
7. Result: the smaller bubble shrinks and the larger bubble grows; the two never settle at equal sizes (equal size would be an unstable equilibrium).
⚡ JEE Trick Soap-bubble sizes are unstable: the pressure curve 4T/r means the small one always loses. 'Air flows to equalise the sizes' is the standard wrong option.
Answer: The smaller bubble shrinks and the larger bubble grows (air flows from the small bubble to the large one).

Concept In a double bubble the shared film separates two gas regions. The smaller bubble has the higher pressure, so the common film bulges into the larger bubble. Its curvature is fixed by the pressure difference across it, and the same constant (2T for an air bubble, one surface) appears on every term, so it cancels.

1. Let r1 = 3 cm (smaller), r2 = 4 cm (larger). Pressures: P1 = P0 + 2T/r1, P2 = P0 + 2T/r2.

3. For a film of radius R the same difference is 2T/R. Equate: 1/R = 1/r1 − 1/r2 = (r2 − r1)/(r1r2).

Answer: (c) 12 cm, concave towards the 3 cm bubble.

Concept Air streaming into the bubble is brought to rest by the film, so its kinetic energy per unit volume is converted into a dynamic (stagnation) pressure on the wall. The bubble stays attached while the film's Laplace excess pressure can still resist this push; it separates when the two are equal.
Formula Dynamic pressure of the stopped air: pdyn = (1/2)ρv2
Excess pressure of a single-surface bubble: Δp = 2T/R (one liquid surface here, so 2T/R, not 4T/R as for a soap film)
Solution
Given air bubble of radius R formed in a narrow tube of radius b (R ≫ b); air of density ρ blown with velocity v; bubble surface tension T
Find the radius at which the bubble separates from the tube (MCQ)
1. Take a small area dA of the bubble wall. Air of density ρ hits it at speed v and stops, so momentum is destroyed at a rate that gives pressure pdyn = (1/2)ρv2 (the stagnation-pressure idea).
2. Surface tension resists this by creating an excess pressure inside a bubble of radius R: Δp = 2T/R. As R grows (R >> b), Δp falls.
3. The bubble can no longer hold on to the narrow tube mouth (radius b << R) when the push of the air equals the hold of the surface: (1/2)ρv2 = 2T/R.
4. Solve for R: R = 2T × 2/(ρv2) = 4T/(ρv2).
5. Dimension check: T/(ρv2) = (N/m)/(kg m-3 m2 s-2) = m, a length, as needed.
6. This matches option (a).

Answer: (a) R = 4T/(ρv2)

Concept In a tube open at both ends, the water column hangs between two menisci. Each meniscus makes a pressure jump of 2T/R across it, with the higher pressure on the concave side. The column can stay put only if these jumps are consistent with its weight (hydrostatics).
Formula Pressure in water at a concave-up meniscus: p = p0 − 2T/R
Column equilibrium: pbottom − ptop = ρgh (inside the water, pressure at the bottom exceeds that at the top by ρgh)
Solution
1. Both ends touch air at p0. Water wets glass, so a meniscus with its concave side facing the air has water pressure p0 − 2T/R beside it.
2. At the top meniscus the water pressure is ptop = p0 − 2T/R1. At the bottom meniscus the water pressure is pbot = p0 + 2T/R2 if it bulges outward (convex downward).
3. Hydrostatics inside the water needs pbot − ptop = ρgh > 0, so 2T/R1 + 2T/R2 = ρgh: the top must be concave and the bottom convex, with radii obeying this sum.
4. Arrangements where both menisci curve the same way (so pbot ≤ ptop), or a flat/convex top, cannot give pbot − ptop = +ρgh, so no equilibrium exists.
5. By this test the shapes in figures (a), (b) and (c) fail the balance and cannot occur; only figure (d) is admissible.
6. So the shapes that may NOT be taken are (a), (b), (c).

Answer: (a), (b), (c) cannot be taken
