Key Facts & Formula Sheet

Quantity / IdeaResult
Surface tensionT = F/L — force per unit length acting along (tangential to) the free surface, perpendicular to the line. Unit N/m (= J/m²), dimensions [M¹L⁰T⁻²]. It is a property of the liquid–air interface, caused by unbalanced cohesive pull on surface molecules.
Surface energyWork to create new surface W = T · ΔA. Surface energy per unit area equals T numerically. For a soap film / bubble there are TWO surfaces, so ΔA counts both faces.
Molecular theoryCohesion = attraction between like molecules; adhesion = between unlike (liquid–solid). Molecular range ≈ 10⁻⁹ m; the sphere of influence is the small sphere (radius = molecular range) within which a molecule feels its neighbours. A surface molecule has no liquid above it ⇒ net inward pull ⇒ the surface behaves like a stretched elastic skin.
Angle of contact θAngle between the solid surface and the tangent to the liquid meniscus, measured inside the liquid. Acute (θ < 90°) → wetting, adhesion > cohesion, meniscus concave, liquid rises. Obtuse (θ > 90°) → non-wetting (e.g. mercury–glass ≈ 135°–140°), cohesion > adhesion, meniscus convex, liquid depressed. Depends on the liquid–solid pair, impurities and temperature.
Excess pressure — liquid dropΔP = 2T/R (one surface). Pressure is higher on the concave side.
Excess pressure — soap bubble (in air)ΔP = 4T/R (two surfaces, inner + outer)
Excess pressure — air bubble in a liquidΔP = 2T/R (one surface). Cylindrical surface: ΔP = T/R.
Capillary rise (Jurin's law)h = 2T cosθ / (rρg), r = tube radius, ρ = liquid density. So h ∝ 1/r (narrower tube ⇒ higher rise). With meniscus-weight correction (θ = 0): (h + r/3) rρg = 2T.
Capillary rise between two parallel platesh = 2T cosθ / (ρg d), d = plate separation
Tube of insufficient lengthIf the tube length L < h, the liquid does NOT overflow. It rises to the top and the meniscus flattens so its radius of curvature R′ grows to satisfy h R = L R′ (hr = const at fixed T), keeping the vertical surface-tension pull balanced.
Shape of meniscus & wettingWetting liquid (water–glass, θ ≈ 0) → concave meniscus, rises. Non-wetting (mercury–glass) → convex meniscus, falls. Drop on a surface spreads if adhesion dominates, beads up if cohesion dominates.
Factors affecting TTemperature: T decreases as temperature rises, becoming zero at the critical temperature. Impurities / surfactants: soap, detergent, oil (surface-active agents) lower T; highly soluble ionic salts (e.g. NaCl) can slightly raise it. Dust and grease lower it.
Work to blow a bubble (radius r)W = T · 2(4πr²) = 8πr²T (soap bubble, two surfaces). To grow r₁→r₂: W = 8πT(r₂² − r₁²). A single liquid drop has one surface: W = 4πr²T.
Splitting one drop into n dropletsVolume fixed: R = n¹⁄³ r. Surface area ↑, so energy released to be supplied ΔE = 4πT(n r² − R²) = 4πTR²(n¹⁄³ − 1). This comes from the liquid's internal energy, so the temperature drops by Δθ = 3T(1/r − 1/R)/(ρs) (s = specific heat).
Core definitionsT = F/L = surface energy per unit area [N/m = J/m²]
Excess pressure (memorise the 2 vs 4)drop 2T/R · soap bubble 4T/R · air bubble in liquid 2T/R · cylinder T/R
Capillarityh = 2T cosθ / (r ρ g) (Jurin's law, h ∝ 1/r)
★ Core ideaSurface tension is one quantity (T) seen three ways: as a force per length on the surface (F/L), as the energy per area of that surface (W = TΔA), and as the cause of an excess pressure across any curved interface (≈ 2T/R). Every drop, bubble and capillary problem is one of these three in disguise.
💡 Method that never failsFor bubbles/drops count the surfaces first: a liquid drop and an air bubble in liquid have one (2T/R); a soap bubble/film has two (4T/R, and work uses 2·4πr²). For capillary questions write h = 2Tcosθ/(rρg) and take ratios — T, ρ, g usually cancel, leaving a clean h₁/h₂ = (cosθ₁/cosθ₂)(r₂/r₁).
⚠ Three traps that cost marks(a) Soap bubble is 4T/R, a drop or an air bubble in liquid is 2T/R — mixing them is the commonest slip. (b) In a short tube the liquid never squirts out; the meniscus flattens (hr = const). (c) Surfactants (soap) lower surface tension — soap water does not have higher T than pure water.

What this lecture covers (from the formula sheet)

  • Surface tension as a tangential force per unit length on the free surface: T = F/L, unit N/m, equivalently surface energy per unit area J/m².
  • Surface energy: work done to increase surface area, W = T·ΔA; a soap film exposes two surfaces so its area change is counted twice.
  • Molecular origin: unbalanced cohesive forces on surface molecules (no liquid above) give a net inward pull, making the surface act like a stretched membrane.
  • Key microscopic lengths: molecular range (≈ 10⁻⁹ m) and the sphere of influence around each molecule.

What this lecture covers (from the formula sheet)

  • Angle of contact θ: acute (<90°) for wetting liquids with a concave meniscus; obtuse (>90°) for non-wetting liquids like mercury with a convex meniscus.
  • Excess pressure across a curved surface is always higher on the concave side: drop 2T/R, soap bubble 4T/R, air bubble in liquid 2T/R, cylindrical surface T/R.
  • Two bubbles in contact: common-film radius R = r₁r₂/(r₂ − r₁) (curves toward the larger bubble).

What this lecture covers (from the formula sheet)

L-4

Capillary Action & Rise of Liquid in a Capillary Tube (Ascent Formula)

Capillary Action

  • Capillary action is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to the combination of surface tension and the adhesive / cohesive forces between the liquid and the tube walls. It is named after capillaries, the tiny blood vessels that behave similarly.
  • When a glass capillary tube of very small diameter is dipped vertically into a liquid, the liquid either rises or is depressed relative to the outside level, depending on the liquid and the tube.
  • Water (wetting liquid): rises above the outside level because the contact angle with glass is less than 90° — adhesive forces (liquid–glass) exceed the cohesive forces within the water, pulling it up the walls.
  • Mercury (non-wetting liquid): is depressed below the outside level because its contact angle with glass is greater than 90° — cohesion dominates, so the liquid is pulled down.
  • Capillarity is not limited to glass tubes; it occurs in any narrow tube or porous material (plant roots, paper towels). The rise/fall depends on the tube diameter, the surface tension and the contact angle.
  • Applications: capillary tubes in medical instruments, ink pens, wicking in diapers and paper towels, groundwater movement in soil, and transport of fluids in plants.

Rise of Liquid in a Capillary Tube — Ascent Formula

  • In water the meniscus inside the tube is concave (curves downward); adhesive forces pull the water upward.
  • The pressure just below the meniscus inside the tube is lower than just above it, by 2T/R, where T is the surface tension and R is the radius of curvature of the meniscus. So just below the meniscus the pressure is p₀ − 2T/R.
  • Outside the tube the surface is plane, so the pressure just below it is the atmospheric p₀. Since pressure at the same level in a connected fluid must be equal, water flows in and rises until the weight of the raised column supplies the pressure difference.
  • At equilibrium the column of height h gives hρg = 2T/R.
  • The radius of curvature R of the meniscus relates to the tube radius r by R = r/cosθ, where θ is the contact angle.
Equilibrium of the columnh ρ g = 2T/R ⇒ h = 2T/(R ρ g)
Ascent formula (R = r/cosθ)h = 2T cos θ / (r ρ g) and h R = 2T/(ρ g)
  • Notable features: (i) if θ > 90° then cosθ is negative, h is negative — the liquid is depressed instead of raised. (ii) When θ = 0 the meniscus is hemispherical and a small correction for the weight of liquid in the meniscus (volume (1/3)πr³) may be added: (h + r/3)rρg = 2T. (iii) The rise is larger for narrower tubes, since h ∝ 1/r.
💡 Why water rises / mercury falls (one line)The curved meniscus creates a pressure jump 2T/R. For a concave (wetting) meniscus the pressure just under it is lower than atmospheric, so outside liquid is pushed up until hρg = 2T/R. For a convex (mercury) meniscus the pressure under it is higher, so the liquid is pushed down — a depression.

Capillary Rise Between Two Parallel Plates

  • Two parallel plates of width b (= l) separated by a distance d are dipped in a liquid. The upward surface-tension force along both liquid–air interfaces is F_up = 2bT cosθ (the factor 2 because there are two interfaces, one on each side).
  • The downward weight of the raised column of cross-section b·d and height h is F_down = ρg h b d.
  • At equilibrium 2bT cosθ = ρg h b d, giving the rise between plates.
Parallel platesh = 2T cos θ / (ρ g d) ( h ∝ 1/d )
★ RememberTube uses r in the denominator (2Tcosθ/rρg); parallel plates use the gap d (2Tcosθ/ρg d). Same numerator, just r → d — don't carry an extra factor of 2 or a radius into the plate formula.

Practical applications of capillarity

  • Rise of oil in a lamp wick, continuous flow of ink in a pen nib, wetting of a towel hung over a bucket, and absorption of ink by blotting paper.

Book Example 1 · L-4
A capillary tube of radius 0.20 mm is dipped vertically in water. Find the height of the water column raised. Surface tension of water = 0.075 N/m, density of water = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s². (θ = 0 for glass–water.)
Ascent formula with cos0° = 1: h = 2T/(rρg).
Substitute: h = (2 × 0.075) / (0.20×10⁻³ × 1000 × 10) = 0.15 / (2.0) = 0.075 m.
h = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm
⚡ JEE trick: with θ = 0, g = 10 and ρ = 1000, the denominator is just r×10⁴, so h = 2T/(r×10⁴) — one division.
Book Example 2 · L-4
A glass tube of radius 0.4 mm is dipped vertically in water. Find to what height the water rises. If the tube is inclined at 60° to the vertical, how much length of the capillary is occupied by water? Surface tension = 7.0×10⁻² N/m, density = 10³ kg/m³ (θ = 0, g = 9.8 m/s²).
Vertical rise: h = 2T cos0°/(rρg) = (2 × 7.0×10⁻²) / (0.4×10⁻³ × 10³ × 9.8).
= 0.14 / 3.92 = 3.57×10⁻² m = 3.57 cm.
The vertical height h is fixed by the balance; tilting the tube does not change h, it only lengthens the liquid thread along the tube.
Length along the tube: l = h / cos60° = 3.57 / (1/2) = 7.14 cm.
Vertical rise h = 3.57 cm; length occupied along the tilted tube l = 7.14 cm
⚡ JEE trick: capillary rise is measured as a vertical height — on tilting, the column length is h/cosφ, so it grows but the top stays at the same height above the reservoir.
Book Example 3 · L-4
A capillary tube of radius 5×10⁻⁴ m is immersed in mercury. The mercury level inside the tube is found to be 8×10⁻³ m below the reservoir level. Determine the angle of contact between mercury and glass. Surface tension of mercury = 0.465 N/m, density = 13.6×10³ kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s².
Ascent formula rearranged: cosθ = h r ρ g / (2T), with h negative (depression).
Numerator: h r ρ g = (−8×10⁻³)(5×10⁻⁴)(13.6×10³)(9.8).
= (−8×10⁻³)(5×10⁻⁴) = −4×10⁻⁶ ; ×13.6×10³ = −5.44×10⁻² ; ×9.8 = −0.5331.
cosθ = −0.5331 / (2 × 0.465) = −0.5331 / 0.930 = −0.5732.
θ = cos⁻¹(−0.5732) = 124°58′ ≈ 125° (obtuse — mercury does not wet glass).
θ ≈ 124°58′ (≈ 125°)
⚡ JEE trick: a negative h (depression) forces cosθ negative, i.e. an obtuse contact angle — the maths automatically flags a non-wetting liquid.
Book Example 4 · L-4
Mercury has a contact angle of 120° with glass. A narrow tube of radius 1.0 mm made of this glass is dipped in a trough of mercury. By what amount does the mercury dip relative to the outside level? Surface tension of mercury = 0.5 N/m, density = 13.6×10³ kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s².
h = 2T cosθ/(r ρ g), cos120° = −0.5.
h = (2 × 0.5 × (−0.5)) / (10⁻³ × 13.6×10³ × 9.8) = (−0.5) / (133.28).
= −3.75×10⁻³ m.
h = −3.75×10⁻³ m = −3.75 mm (a depression of 3.75 mm)
⚡ JEE trick: the minus sign is the whole answer — report its magnitude as the depth of dip, 3.75 mm.
Book Example 5 · L-4
Two parallel plates 20 cm long with a 0.1 mm separation are slightly dipped in water (surface tension 75 dyne/cm). Calculate the rise of water between the plates. (θ = 0, ρ = 1 g/cm³, g = 981 cm/s².)
Parallel-plate rise: h = 2T cosθ/(ρ g d), all quantities in CGS.
h = (2 × 75 × 1) / (1 × 981 × 0.01) = 150 / 9.81.
= 15.29 cm.
h = 15.29 cm
⚡ JEE trick: 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm — keep CGS consistent (dyne/cm, g/cm³, cm/s²) and the plate gap d, not a radius, sits in the denominator.

Strong Box · PYQ 1 · JEE Advanced 2024 Paper 2
A spherical soap bubble inside an air chamber at pressure P₀ = 10⁵ Pa has a radius such that the excess pressure inside it is ΔP = 144 Pa. The chamber pressure is reduced to 8P₀/27 (temperature unchanged). Assuming the excess pressure is always ≪ chamber pressure, the new excess pressure ΔP′ (in Pa) is: (a) 96 (b) 95 (c) 91 (d) 89.
Excess pressure of a soap bubble: ΔP = 4T/r, so ΔP ∝ 1/r.
The trapped air obeys isothermal Boyle's law: P_in V = const, with V ∝ r³ and P_in ≈ P_chamber (since ΔP ≪ chamber pressure).
So P₀ r₁³ = (8P₀/27) r₂³ ⇒ r₂³/r₁³ = 27/8 ⇒ r₂/r₁ = 3/2.
ΔP′ = 4T/r₂ = (4T/r₁)(r₁/r₂) = 144 × (2/3).
ΔP′ = 96 Pa.
(a) 96 Pa
⚡ JEE trick: pressure ratio (27/8) ⇒ radius ratio (3/2) ⇒ excess-pressure ratio (2/3). Chain the cube root and reciprocal in one step.
Strong Box · PYQ 2 · JEE Advanced 2023 Paper 2
An incompressible liquid is in a container with a weightless piston having a hole; a capillary of inner radius 0.1 mm is dipped vertically through the hole. The air in the container is compressed isothermally from V₀ to (100/101)V₀. Find the height h (cm) of the liquid column in the capillary above the liquid level. T = 0.075 N/m, P₀ = 10⁵ N/m², g = 10 m/s², ρ = 10³ kg/m³, contact angle = 0. (a) 23 (b) 25 (c) 27 (d) 21.
Ordinary capillary rise: h₀ = 2T/(rρg) = (2 × 0.075)/(10⁻⁴ × 10³ × 10) = 0.15/1 = 0.15 m = 15 cm.
Isothermal compression of the trapped air: P₀V₀ = P₂(100/101)V₀ ⇒ P₂ = (101/100)P₀ = 1.01×10⁵ Pa.
Extra pressure on the liquid surface ΔP = P₂ − P₀ = 10³ Pa, which pushes liquid further up.
Extra rise Δh = ΔP/(ρg) = 10³/(10³ × 10) = 0.1 m = 10 cm.
Total column h = 15 + 10 = 25 cm.
(b) 25 cm
⚡ JEE trick: superpose the two drivers — the capillary pull (2T/rρg) and the compressed-air overpressure (ΔP/ρg) — and simply add the heights.
Strong Box · PYQ 3 · JEE Advanced 2020 Paper 1
Water is filled above the rim of a glass; the bulge is modelled as a disc of thickness h with semicircular edges. The water surface breaks when the pressure at the bottom of the disc exceeds what the surface tension can hold. With ρ = 10³ kg/m³, T = 0.07 N/m, g = 10 m/s², the value of h (in mm) is: (a) 3.74 (b) 3.70 (c) 4.25 (d) 3.90.
The semicircular edge has radius of curvature R = h/2 (the edge spans the thickness h).
Excess pressure the curved edge can withstand (cylindrical-type surface): ΔP = T/R = 2T/h.
Hydrostatic pressure at the bottom of the disc of height h: p = ρgh.
At the breaking point ρgh = 2T/h ⇒ h² = 2T/(ρg).
h² = (2 × 0.07)/(10³ × 10) = 0.14/10⁴ = 1.4×10⁻⁵ ⇒ h = 3.74×10⁻³ m.
(a) 3.74 mm
⚡ JEE trick: edge radius = h/2 turns T/R into 2T/h, and balancing against ρgh gives the neat h = √(2T/ρg).
Strong Box · PYQ 4 · JEE Advanced 2017 Paper 1
A drop of radius R = 10⁻² m with surface tension S = 0.1/4π N/m divides into K identical drops; the total change in surface energy is ΔU = 10⁻³ J. If K = 10^α, find α. (a) 6 (b) 7 (c) 5 (d) 4.
Volume conserved: K·(4/3)πr³ = (4/3)πR³ ⇒ r = R/K^(1/3).
New area − old area = K·4πr² − 4πR² = 4πR²(K^(1/3) − 1).
ΔU = S·ΔA = (0.1/4π)·4πR²(K^(1/3) − 1) = 0.1 R²(K^(1/3) − 1).
10⁻³ = 0.1 × (10⁻²)² × (K^(1/3) − 1) = 10⁻⁵ (K^(1/3) − 1).
K^(1/3) − 1 = 100 ⇒ K^(1/3) ≈ 100 = 10² ⇒ K = 10⁶ ⇒ α = 6.
(a) α = 6
⚡ JEE trick: ΔU = 4πSR²(K^(1/3) − 1) is the master line for fragmenting a drop; the chosen S = 0.1/4π cancels the 4π deliberately.
Strong Box · PYQ 5 · JEE Advanced 2010 Paper 2
[Dropper passage] Liquid medicine of density ρ forms a spherical drop at a dropper opening of radius r; it detaches when the surface-tension force can no longer hold its weight. With the data of PYQ 6 (r = 5×10⁻⁴ m, T = 0.11 N/m, giving detachment radius R ≈ 1.4×10⁻³ m), the surface energy of the drop just after it detaches is: (a) 1.4×10⁻⁶ J (b) 2.7×10⁻⁶ J (c) 5.4×10⁻⁶ J (d) 8.1×10⁻⁹ J.
Surface energy of a (single-surface) liquid drop: E = T × 4πR².
E = 0.11 × 4π × (1.4×10⁻³)² = 0.11 × 4π × 1.96×10⁻⁶.
4π × 1.96×10⁻⁶ = 2.463×10⁻⁵ ; × 0.11 = 2.71×10⁻⁶ J.
(b) 2.7×10⁻⁶ J
⚡ JEE trick: a free drop has ONE surface — use 4πR²T, not 8πR²T (that would be a soap bubble).
Strong Box · PYQ 6 · JEE Advanced 2010 Paper 2
[Dropper passage] With r = 5×10⁻⁴ m, ρ = 10³ kg/m³, g = 10 m/s², T = 0.11 N/m, the radius of the drop when it detaches from the dropper is approximately: (a) 1.4×10⁻³ m (b) 3.3×10⁻³ m (c) 2.0×10⁻³ m (d) 4.1×10⁻³ m.
Detachment condition: surface-tension hold = weight. Vertical surface-tension force = 2πr²T/R (from PYQ 7).
2πr²T/R = (4/3)πR³ρg ⇒ 2r²T = (4/3)R⁴ρg ⇒ R⁴ = 3r²T/(2ρg).
R⁴ = 3(5×10⁻⁴)²(0.11)/(2 × 10³ × 10) = 3 × 25×10⁻⁸ × 0.11 / (2×10⁴) = 8.25×10⁻⁸/2×10⁴ = 4.125×10⁻¹².
R = (4.125×10⁻¹²)^(1/4) = 1.425×10⁻³ m ≈ 1.4×10⁻³ m.
(a) 1.4×10⁻³ m
⚡ JEE trick: equate the surface-tension hold (2πr²T/R) to the drop weight and the R⁴ pops out cleanly as 3r²T/2ρg.
Strong Box · PYQ 7 · JEE Advanced 2010 Paper 2
[Dropper passage] If the radius of the dropper opening is r, the vertical force on the drop of radius R due to surface tension (assuming r ≪ R) is: (a) 2πrT (b) 2πRT (c) 2πr²T/R (d) 2πR²T/r.
Surface tension acts along the rim of the opening, a circle of circumference 2πr, giving a total pull T·2πr directed along the spherical surface.
Only the vertical component holds the drop. The rim of radius r sits on the sphere of radius R, so the surface there makes an angle φ with sinφ = r/R.
Vertical component = (2πrT)·sinφ = 2πrT·(r/R) = 2πr²T/R.
(c) 2πr²T/R
⚡ JEE trick: the extra factor r/R is the geometric projection sinφ of the rim onto the vertical — it is what makes the force fall off as the drop grows.
Strong Box · PYQ 8 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 2
A soap bubble of surface tension 0.04 N/m is blown to a diameter of 7 cm. If (15000 − x) μJ of work is done in blowing it further to a diameter of 14 cm, find x. (π = 22/7).
Soap bubble has two surfaces: W = T × 2(4πr₂² − 4πr₁²) = 8πT(r₂² − r₁²).
r₁ = 3.5 cm = 0.035 m, r₂ = 7 cm = 0.07 m ⇒ r₂² − r₁² = 0.0049 − 0.001225 = 0.003675 m².
W = 8 × (22/7) × 0.04 × 0.003675 = (8×22×0.04×0.003675)/7 = 0.025872/7 = 0.003696 J.
W = 3696 μJ. So (15000 − x) = 3696 ⇒ x = 15000 − 3696.
x = 11304 (work done = 3696 μJ)
⚡ JEE trick: doubling the diameter quadruples r², so r₂² − r₁² = 3r₁²; keep the factor 8π (two surfaces) and the μJ unit.
Strong Box · PYQ 9 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 2
Surface tensions of two liquids of the same density, T₁ and T₂, are measured by capillary rise using tubes of inner radii r₁ and r₂ with r₁ > r₂; the measured heights are h₁ and h₂. Which relation do they satisfy? (a) h₁ > h₂ and T₁ = T₂ (b) h₁ = h₂ and T₁ = T₂ (c) h₁ > h₂ and T₁ < T₂ (d) h₁ < h₂ and T₁ = T₂.
Jurin's law (θ = 0, same ρ): h = 2T/(rρg), so for equal surface tension h ∝ 1/r.
Test each option for self-consistency. If T₁ = T₂ then h₁/h₂ = r₂/r₁ < 1 (since r₁ > r₂), so h₁ < h₂ — matches (d), contradicts (a) and (b).
For (c): h₁ > h₂ needs T₁/r₁ > T₂/r₂, i.e. T₁/T₂ > r₁/r₂ > 1, impossible if T₁ < T₂. Inconsistent.
Only option (d) is internally consistent.
(d) h₁ < h₂ and T₁ = T₂
⚡ JEE trick: with equal T and ρ, the wider tube (r₁) always gives the smaller rise — h ∝ 1/r settles it immediately.
Strong Box · PYQ 10 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 2
When part of a straight capillary is placed vertically in a liquid, the liquid rises to height h. If the inner radius, the density and the surface tension each decrease by 1%, the height changes by what %? (a) +1 (b) −3 (c) +3 (d) −1.
h = 2T cosθ/(r ρ g) ⇒ h ∝ T/(r ρ) (θ, g fixed).
Fractional change: Δh/h = ΔT/T − Δr/r − Δρ/ρ.
Each quantity decreases by 1%: ΔT/T = −1%, Δr/r = −1%, Δρ/ρ = −1%.
Δh/h = (−1) − (−1) − (−1) = −1 + 1 + 1 = +1%.
(a) +1%
⚡ JEE trick: add signed percentage changes with the sign of each exponent in h ∝ T¹ r⁻¹ ρ⁻¹ — the two −1 exponents flip their decreases into increases.
Strong Box · PYQ 11 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 1
Statement I: Pressure of a fluid is exerted only on a solid surface in contact, as fluid pressure does not exist everywhere in a still fluid. Statement II: Excess potential energy of molecules on the surface of a liquid (compared to the interior) results in surface tension. Choose the correct option. (a) Statement I false, Statement II true (b) Statement I true, Statement II false (c) Both false (d) Both true.
Statement I: FALSE — pressure exists at every point in a still fluid (Pascal), not only where it touches a solid; it acts in all directions.
Statement II: TRUE — surface molecules have higher potential energy (fewer neighbours), and the tendency to minimise this energy manifests as surface tension.
(a) Statement I is false but Statement II is true
⚡ JEE trick: "pressure only on contact surfaces" is a classic wrong statement — fluid pressure is a field defined throughout the bulk.
Strong Box · PYQ 12 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 1
Two soap bubbles of radii 2 cm and 4 cm are in contact with each other. The radius of curvature of the common surface, in cm, is ____.
For two bubbles in contact, the common film curves toward the larger bubble; its radius is R = r₁r₂/(r₂ − r₁).
Here r₁ = 2 cm, r₂ = 4 cm ⇒ R = (2 × 4)/(4 − 2) = 8/2.
R = 4 cm.
R = 4 cm
⚡ JEE trick: common-surface radius = r₁r₂/(r₂ − r₁); the excess pressures 4T/r₁ and 4T/r₂ differ, and the film bulges into the lower-pressure (bigger) bubble.
Strong Box · PYQ 13 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 2
An air bubble of radius 1.0 mm is at a depth of 20 cm below the free surface of a liquid of surface tension 0.095 J/m² and density 10³ kg/m³. The difference between the pressure inside the bubble and the atmospheric pressure is ____ N/m². (g = 10 m/s²).
Inside pressure = atmospheric + hydrostatic (at depth) + excess pressure of the single bubble surface (2T/r).
P_in − P_atm = ρgh + 2T/r.
ρgh = 10³ × 10 × 0.20 = 2000 N/m²; 2T/r = (2 × 0.095)/(1.0×10⁻³) = 190 N/m².
P_in − P_atm = 2000 + 190 = 2190 N/m².
2190 N/m²
⚡ JEE trick: an air bubble in liquid has ONE surface (2T/r), and remember to add the depth term ρgh to the surface-tension term.
Strong Box · PYQ 14 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 1
An air bubble of radius 0.1 cm lies at a depth of 20 cm below the free surface of a liquid of density 1000 kg/m³. If the pressure inside the bubble is 2100 N/m² greater than the atmospheric pressure, the surface tension of the liquid (SI) is (g = 10 m/s²): (a) 0.1 (b) 0.05 (c) 0.02 (d) 0.25.
P_in − P_atm = ρgh + 2T/r = 2100 N/m².
ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 0.20 = 2000 N/m².
2T/r = 2100 − 2000 = 100 ⇒ T = 100 × r/2 = 100 × (0.1×10⁻²)/2 = 100 × 10⁻³/2.
T = 0.05 N/m.
(b) 0.05 N/m
⚡ JEE trick: subtract the depth term ρgh first; whatever is left (100 Pa) is the pure surface-tension excess 2T/r. Watch 0.1 cm = 10⁻³ m.
Strong Box · PYQ 15 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 1
Statement I: Hot water flows faster than cold water. Statement II: Soap water has higher surface tension compared to fresh water. Choose the correct option. (a) Statement I true, Statement II false (b) Statement I false, Statement II true (c) Both false (d) Both true.
Statement I: TRUE — hot water has lower viscosity, so for the same conditions it flows faster.
Statement II: FALSE — soap is a surfactant and LOWERS surface tension; soap water has lower surface tension than fresh water.
(a) Statement I is true but Statement II is false
⚡ JEE trick: surfactants always reduce T — that single fact kills Statement II at sight.
Strong Box · PYQ 16 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 1
A liquid column of height 0.04 cm balances the excess pressure of a soap bubble of a certain radius. If the liquid density is 8×10³ kg/m³ and the surface tension of the soap solution is 0.28 N/m, the diameter of the soap bubble is ____ cm (g = 10 m/s²).
Soap bubble excess pressure (two surfaces) = 4T/r, balanced by the liquid column ρgh.
ρgh = 4T/r ⇒ r = 4T/(ρgh).
r = (4 × 0.28)/(8×10³ × 10 × 0.04×10⁻²) = 1.12/(8×10³ × 10 × 4×10⁻⁴) = 1.12/32.
r = 0.035 m = 3.5 cm ⇒ diameter = 7 cm.
Diameter = 7 cm
⚡ JEE trick: use 4T/r (soap bubble, not 2T/r); keep 0.04 cm = 4×10⁻⁴ m before dividing.
Strong Box · PYQ 17 · JEE Advanced 2019 Paper 1 (multiple correct)
A cylindrical capillary of radius 0.2 mm is made by joining two capillaries T₁ (water contact angle 0°) and T₂ (contact angle 60°). It is dipped vertically in water in two configurations — Case I (T₁ at the bottom, T₂ on top) and Case II (T₂ at the bottom, T₁ on top). Which options are correct? (T = 0.075 N/m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s². Reference rises: 7.5 cm for θ = 0, 3.75 cm for θ = 60°.) (a) For Case II, with the joint 5 cm above the water, the column = 3.75 cm (b) For Case I, with the joint 8 cm above the water, the column = 7.5 cm (c) The meniscus-weight correction differs for the two cases (d) For Case I, with the joint 5 cm above the water, the column > 8.75 cm.
Reference rises: h(θ=0) = 2T/(rρg) = (2×0.075)/(0.2×10⁻³×1000×10) = 7.5 cm; h(θ=60°) = 7.5×cos60° = 3.75 cm.
(a) Case II: bottom tube is T₂ (θ=60°), whose equilibrium rise 3.75 cm is below the 5 cm joint, so water stays in T₂ and rises to 3.75 cm. TRUE.
(b) Case I: bottom tube is T₁ (θ=0°), equilibrium rise 7.5 cm, below the 8 cm joint, so water stays in T₁ and rises to 7.5 cm. TRUE.
(c) The top meniscus differs between the cases (θ=0° hemispherical vs θ=60° shallower), so the weight of liquid held in the meniscus — and hence its height correction — differs. TRUE.
(d) Case I, joint at 5 cm: T₁ would reach 7.5 cm but hits the joint at 5 cm; entering T₂ it cannot support more than 3.75 cm, so the meniscus pins at the junction and the column settles at 5 cm (an intermediate curvature, cosφ ≈ 0.67, is allowed). It does NOT exceed 8.75 cm. FALSE.
Correct: (a), (b), (c). [(d) is false — the column pins at the 5 cm junction, not above 8.75 cm.]
⚡ JEE trick: the top meniscus controls the rise. While water is in the lower tube that tube's contact angle sets the height; at a junction the meniscus pins and can hold any height between the two tubes' limits (3.75–7.5 cm).
Strong Box · PYQ 18 · JEE Advanced 2018 Paper 1 (multiple correct)
A uniform capillary of inner radius r is dipped vertically in water; the water rises to height h. Surface tension σ, contact angle θ, mass of water in the meniscus ignored. Which statements are true? (a) For a given tube material, h decreases as r increases (b) For a given tube material, h is independent of σ (c) If the experiment is done in a lift accelerating upward at constant a, h decreases (d) h is proportional to the contact angle θ.
Ascent formula: h = 2σ cosθ/(r ρ g).
(a) h ∝ 1/r, so increasing r decreases h. TRUE.
(b) h ∝ σ, so it is NOT independent of σ. FALSE.
(c) In a lift accelerating up, effective gravity becomes g+a, so h = 2σcosθ/(r ρ (g+a)) decreases. TRUE.
(d) h ∝ cosθ, not θ itself. FALSE.
Correct: (a), (c)
⚡ JEE trick: read the ascent formula as h ∝ σ·cosθ/(r·g_eff); every option is just one variable in it — and "cosθ" is not "θ".
