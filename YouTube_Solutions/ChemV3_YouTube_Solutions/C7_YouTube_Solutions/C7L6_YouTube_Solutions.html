1 (i)Fill in the blank: Equilibrium vapour density can be used to estimate the ______ of dissociation for gaseous substances.Vapour density and dissociationRelation between degree of dissociation and vapour density | Chemical Equilibrium class 11🟡 Similardegree
Concept The whole lecture rests on one measurement: when a gas dissociates, the number of molecules rises, so the mixture becomes lighter per unit volume. Measuring how much lighter tells you the degree of dissociation.
Key relation V.D. ∝ 1/(number of moles) ⇒ D/d = neq/ninitial ⇒ α = (D−d)/[(n−1)d] Every numerical in this lecture is one of these three statements rearranged.
Solution
1. Start from PV = nRT written as PV = (w/M)RT, so M = wRT/(PV) = ρRT/P.
2. Vapour density is defined as V.D. = M/2, so V.D. = ρRT/(2P).
3. Substituting P = nRT/V gives V.D. = ρV/(2n) — and at equilibrium in a closed vessel both ρ and V are fixed.
4. So V.D. is inversely proportional to the number of moles in the vessel, which is the whole basis of the method.
5. Dissociation increases the number of moles, so it lowers the vapour density; the size of the drop measures how far the dissociation has gone.
6. That fraction is the degree of dissociation, written α. The blank is filled with degree.
7. Note the word choice: “extent” and “rate” are both wrong here — the first is vague and the second is kinetics, not equilibrium.

 V.D. = M/2         ->  V.D. = rho V / (2n)

 rho, V fixed  =>  V.D. proportional to 1/n

 dissociation raises n  ->  V.D. falls
 size of the fall = DEGREE of dissociation

1 (v)Fill in the blank: The equilibrium vapour density of a substance is proportional to its ______ mass.V.D. and molar massVapour Density | Physical Chemistry | NEET JEE | Anushka Mam🟡 Similarmolar (molecular)
Concept Vapour density is defined as half the molar mass, so the two are directly proportional. That single proportionality is why a molar-mass measurement and a vapour-density measurement are interchangeable.
Key relation V.D. = M/2, so V.D. ∝ M The constant of proportionality is exactly ½, coming from the mass of one mole of H2 being 2 g.
Solution
1. Vapour density is the ratio of the mass of a volume of the gas to the mass of an equal volume of hydrogen at the same temperature and pressure.
2. Equal volumes contain equal numbers of moles, so that ratio is M/M(H2) = M/2.
3. Hence V.D. = M/2, and the equilibrium vapour density is proportional to the molar (molecular) mass of the mixture.
4. The book states this pair explicitly as DT = MT/2 and DO = MO/2, for the theoretical and the observed values.
5. That is why the two forms of the α formula are interchangeable: α = (MT−MO)/[MO(n−1)] and α = (DT−DO)/[DO(n−1)].
6. The factors of ½ cancel top and bottom, leaving the same number whichever pair of quantities the question supplies.
7. Item 3(E) of this Quick Recap makes the same point: the proportional relationship is between equilibrium vapour density and molar mass.
 V.D. = mass of gas / mass of equal vol. of H2

 alpha = (M(T)-M(O)) / (M(O)(n-1))
       = (D(T)-D(O)) / (D(O)(n-1))   [same]

Concept α is defined as a fraction of the starting amount, so it cannot exceed the whole. Its range is 0 to 1 inclusive, and the statement is true.
Key relation α = (moles dissociated)/(moles taken) ≤ 1 The numerator can at most equal the denominator, which is the case of complete dissociation.
Solution
1. Take C moles of reactant; the amount that dissociates is at most C, because nothing more is present to dissociate.
2. So α = (amount dissociated)/C ≤ C/C = 1.
3. The lower limit is α = 0, meaning no dissociation at all, so the whole range is 0 ≤ α ≤ 1. The statement is true.
4. This is also a useful check on any numerical answer: an α of 1.4 or −0.2 signals an algebra error, not an unusual reaction.
5. Percentages are the same statement scaled: 0% to 100%, never more.
6. Watch for one genuine exception in appearance only — association problems, where the observed molar mass rises instead of falling. The formula there carries (1/n − 1) in the denominator and still delivers α ≤ 1.
7. Level-4 Q7 of this lecture is exactly that case, with acetic acid dimerising in benzene.
8. Note the one construction that can appear to break the rule: a question that quotes an α above 1 is almost always describing association, where the formula carries (1/n − 1) in the denominator rather than (n − 1). That denominator is negative, and so is the numerator, so the quotient still lands inside the permitted range. Solution
1. The derivation begins with PV = nRT and ends at V.D. = ρV/(2n). Every step assumes a gas.
2. A pure solid has a fixed density unrelated to the number of moles present, and it does not appear in K at all.
3. So a solid’s “vapour density” is not a measurable quantity of the kind the method needs. The statement is false.

2 (iii)True or false: The degree of dissociation and equilibrium vapour density are inversely proportional.α against V.D.Vapor Density And Degree Of Dissociation- Chemical Equilibirium🟡 SimilarTrue as α rises the equilibrium vapour density falls
Concept Dissociation multiplies the number of moles, and vapour density varies as 1/n. So a larger α always means a smaller observed vapour density — an inverse relationship, and the statement is true.
Key relation d = D/[1 + (n−1)α] The denominator grows with α, so d falls monotonically as α rises.
Solution
1. Start with 1 mole of A dissociating into n moles of products, with degree α.
2. Total moles at equilibrium = (1−α) + nα = 1 + (n−1)α.
3. Since V.D. ∝ 1/ntotal, the observed value is d = D/[1 + (n−1)α].
4. As α increases from 0 to 1, the denominator increases from 1 to n, so d decreases from D to D/n. The relation is inverse, and the statement is true.
5. Read it as a claim about direction rather than strict proportionality: d is not literally k/α, but it does fall steadily as α rises, which is what “inversely” means in this context.
6. The two end points confirm it: α = 0 leaves d at its largest value D; α = 1 drives it to its smallest value D/n.
7. Every numerical in Levels 1–3 of this lecture is one instance of this single curve.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the one relation d = D/[1 + (n−1)α]. Solved for α it becomes α = (D−d)/[(n−1)d], so the two formulae the paper uses are a single line apart.

Concept Partial dissociation is the case where some but not all of the reactant has broken up, so α sits strictly between 0 and 1. The statement is true.
Key relation α = 0 no dissociation; 0 < α < 1 partial; α = 1 complete The three cases exhaust the possibilities, and “partial” names the middle one.
Solution
1. If α = 0 nothing has dissociated, so the description “partially dissociated” does not apply.
2. If α = 1 everything has dissociated, which is complete rather than partial dissociation.
3. Every remaining case has 0 < α < 1, so a partially dissociated substance has α strictly between 0 and 1. The statement is true.
4. Item 3(D) of this Quick Recap puts it in words: partial dissociation describes a reactant that has not completely broken down into products.
5. Physically this is the normal situation for a reversible reaction — both reactant and products are present at equilibrium, which is what makes K finite and non-zero.
6. The two extremes correspond to K = 0 and K → ∞, neither of which describes a true equilibrium mixture.
7. Every worked numerical in this lecture returns an α in this middle band, from 0.09 up to about 0.88.

2 (v)True or false: Changes in temperature can affect both equilibrium vapour density and the degree of dissociation.Effect of temperatureDEGREE OF DISSOCIATION, AVERAGE MOLECULAR WEIGHT OF EQUILIBRIUM MIXTURE, Concept of VAPOUR DENSITY🟡 SimilarTrue temperature changes K, hence α, hence the vapour density
Concept Temperature is the one variable that changes K itself. A different K means a different α, and a different α means a different observed vapour density. The statement is true.
Key relation T → K → α → d, a single chain Every link is a genuine dependence, so a change at one end propagates to the other.
Solution
1. By the van’t Hoff equation, K depends on temperature: it rises with T for an endothermic reaction and falls for an exothermic one.
2. Dissociation is almost always endothermic, since bonds are being broken, so heating raises K.
3. A larger K at the same pressure means a larger α — more of the reactant has broken up.
4. A larger α means more moles in the vessel, so by d = D/[1 + (n−1)α] the observed vapour density falls.
5. So a temperature change affects both quantities, and the statement is true.
6. Level-5 Q1 of this lecture is a direct demonstration: heating PCl5 to 230 °C drops its vapour density from 104.16 to 62, corresponding to 68% dissociation.
7. Note the contrast with a catalyst, which changes neither K, nor α, nor the vapour density — only the time taken to reach them.
8. Note the direction for the rarer exothermic dissociation. There cooling would raise K and hence α, and the vapour density would fall on cooling rather than on heating — the chain still holds, only the sign of the first link changes.

Concept Two equilibria sharing Cl2. Adding CO pushes the second one backward, which consumes Cl2; the first equilibrium then feels a drop in a product and moves forward, using up PCl5.
Key relation Cl2 is common to both; its concentration is set by both equilibria simultaneously A change in one system reaches the other only through the shared species.
Solution
1. Adding CO to COCl2 ⇌ CO + Cl2 raises Q above K for that system.
2. It therefore shifts backward, combining CO with Cl2 to re-form COCl2. So [Cl2] falls.
3. Cl2 is also a product of PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2. Removing a product lowers Q below K for that system.
4. That equilibrium therefore shifts forward: more PCl5 dissociates.
5. Consequently [PCl5] decreases and [PCl3] increases. So (c) is correct and (a) and (b) are both wrong.
6. (d) claims [Cl2] is greater; the first shift removed Cl2, and the second shift only partly restores it, so the net concentration is lower. Reject.
7. The partial restoration is Le Chatelier's usual behaviour — the system opposes the change without cancelling it, so Cl2 ends up between its old value and the value it would have had with no second equilibrium present.
