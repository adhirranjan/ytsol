1The moment of inertia of a non uniform ring of mass m and radius R will be : (a) greater than mR² (b) less than mR² (c) may be less than or greater than mR² (d) equal to mR² — (Level-1)MOI of a ring · mass distributionRotational Motion 04 — Moment of Inertia: Introduction🟡 Similardequal to mR²
Concept Moment of inertia is a weighted sum, I = Σmiri², where the weight is the square of each element's perpendicular distance from the axis. A ring is special: every particle of it sits at exactly the same distance R from the central axis, no matter how the mass is smeared around the circumference. So the factor r² comes out of the sum as the common constant R², and what is left is just the total mass. Non-uniformity redistributes mass along the ring, never away from radius R.
Formula I = ∫r² dm, with r = R for every element of a ring. The integral only cares about distance from the axis, and a ring has just one such distance.
Solution
1. Cut the ring into elements dm1, dm2, … of unequal size — that is what "non uniform" permits.
2. Each element lies on the circle, so its perpendicular distance from the central axis is r = R identically.
3. I = ∫r² dm = ∫R² dm = R²∫dm.R² is a constant and pulls straight out of the integral.
4. ∫dm = m, the total mass, so I = mR² — identical to the uniform ring.
5. Sanity check with an extreme case: put the whole mass m at a single point on the rim. Then I = mR² again, so even the most lopsided ring gives the same value.
6. Decoding the options: (a) and (b) would need some mass at r > R or r < R, impossible for a ring; (c) is the "it depends" trap that (a)/(b) invite; (d) is correct.
 axis
   |        all dm at r = R
   |     .--o--o--.
   |   o           o     I = R^2 * (sum dm)
   |    `--o---o--'        = m R^2
   |
 uniform or not: same I
⚡ JEE Trick The word "non uniform" is bait — students reflexively assume the answer must change. It changes for a rod or a disc (where elements sit at many different r) but never for a ring or a hollow cylinder, where geometry fixes r = R. Ask first: does non-uniformity move mass to a different radius? If not, I is untouched.
Answer: (d) equal to mR².
2A thin ring has mass 2 kg and radius 0.5 m. What is its moment of inertia about its central axis? — (Level-2)MOI of a ring about the central axisMoment of Inertia Derivation (Ring, Rod, Disk, Cylinder)🟡 Similar0.5 kg m²
Concept "Central axis" of a ring means the axis through the centre and perpendicular to the plane of the ring — the axis it spins about like a wheel. Every mass element of a thin ring sits at the same perpendicular distance R from that axis, so the integral ∫r²dm collapses to R²∫dm. This is the cleanest moment of inertia in the whole chapter and the benchmark against which all others are quoted (a disc of the same m and R has only half as much).
Formula I = mR² for a thin ring about its central perpendicular axis. Derived directly from I = ∫R²dm = R²m, since r is the constant R.
Solution
1. Identify the axis: through the centre O, perpendicular to the plane of the ring.
2. List the data in SI units already: m = 2 kg, R = 0.5 m.
3. I = mR² = (2)(0.5)² = (2)(0.25) = 0.5 kg m².Squaring the radius first avoids the common slip of computing 2 × 0.5 = 1.
4. Check the units: kg × m² = kg m², correct for moment of inertia.
5. Radius of gyration k = √(I/m) = √(0.5/2) = 0.5 m = R, exactly as expected for a ring.For a ring the radius of gyration equals the geometric radius — a fast self-check.
6. Contrast: a disc of the same mass and radius would give ½mR² = 0.25 kg m², so quoting 0.25 here means the ring was mistaken for a disc.
 m = 2 kg, R = 0.5 m
 I = m R^2
   = 2 * (0.5)^2
   = 2 * 0.25
   = 0.5 kg m^2
 k = sqrt(I/m) = 0.5 m = R  (ring check)
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is the half. Ring → mR², disc → ½mR², and the phrase "thin ring" is the only word separating 0.5 from 0.25 here. A second trap is the axis: about a diameter the same ring gives ½mR² = 0.25 kg m² by the perpendicular axis theorem, so read the axis before reaching for a formula.
Answer: 0.5 kg m².
3Find the moment of inertia of a non uniform rod AB of length ℓ about an axis perpendicular to the rod and passing through point A. The mass per unit length of the rod is given by λ = (λ0/ℓ)(ax + b), where x is the distance of a point on the rod from end A, and λ0, a & b are constants. — (Level-3)MOI by integration · rod of variable λMOI of a thin rod of linear density λ about an axis at its end🟡 Similar(λ0ℓ²/12)(3aℓ + 4b)
Concept When the mass is not spread evenly, no standard formula applies and the definition I = ∫r²dm must be used from scratch. The rod is one-dimensional, so the natural element is a slice of length dx sitting at distance x from the axis at A. Its mass is dm = λ(x)dx, and because the axis is perpendicular to the rod at A, the perpendicular distance of that slice from the axis is simply x. Integrating x²λ(x)dx from 0 to ℓ sweeps the whole rod.
Formula dm = λ(x) dx and I = ∫0ℓ x² λ(x) dx. λ is mass per unit length, so length × λ gives mass; x is the lever arm since the axis is perpendicular at A.
Solution
1. Take an element at distance x from A of width dx; its mass is dm = λdx = (λ0/ℓ)(ax + b)dx.
2. Its contribution is dI = x²dm = (λ0/ℓ)(ax³ + bx²)dx.Multiplying x² into the bracket first turns the integral into two elementary power integrals.
3. I = (λ0/ℓ)∫0ℓ(ax³ + bx²)dx = (λ0/ℓ)[aℓ⁴/4 + bℓ³/3].
4. Cancel one power of ℓ: I = λ0[aℓ³/4 + bℓ²/3] = λ0ℓ²[aℓ/4 + b/3].Pulling out ℓ² exposes the common denominator 12.
5. Put the bracket over 12: aℓ/4 + b/3 = (3aℓ + 4b)/12, so I = (λ0ℓ²/12)(3aℓ + 4b).
6. Uniform-rod check: set a = 0, then λ = λ0b/ℓ is constant, m = λ0b and I = λ0ℓ²(4b)/12 = mℓ²/3 — the textbook rod-about-its-end result.Any variable-density answer must reduce to the standard one when the variation is switched off.
 A |----|====|--------| B     axis _|_ at A
   0    x   x+dx      l
 dm = (L0/l)(a x + b) dx
 dI = x^2 dm
 I  = (L0/l)[a l^4/4 + b l^3/3]
    = (L0 l^2 /12)(3 a l + 4 b)
 check a=0 -> m l^2/3
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps here. First, cancelling ℓ wrongly: the prefactor is λ0/ℓ, not λ0, and forgetting it leaves an answer one power of ℓ too big. Second, mixing up 3 and 4: the cubic term (from ax³) integrates to /4 and the quadratic term (from bx²) to /3, so after clearing denominators the a-term carries the 3 and the b-term the 4 — exactly the crossover students reverse.
Answer: I = (λ0ℓ²/12)(3aℓ + 4b).
4Find the moment of inertia of the given system about axis AB. The figure shows two rods, each of mass m and length ℓ, hinged at the top point A and making 30° on either side of the vertical axis AB, and a ring of mass 2m and radius R at the bottom whose own axis coincides with AB. — (Level-4)Composite body · rod inclined to the axis + ringRigid Body & Rotation | Moment of Inertia | Class 11 JEE🟡 Similar2mR² + mℓ²/6
Concept Moment of inertia is additive: compute I for each piece about the same axis AB and add. For the ring the axis AB is its own central axis, so it contributes MR² directly. For a rod inclined at angle θ to the axis, only the component of each element's position perpendicular to the axis counts — an element at distance s along the rod sits at perpendicular distance s·sinθ, so every lever arm is scaled by sinθ and I is scaled by sin²θ.
Formula Ring about its own axis: I = MR². Rod of mass m, length ℓ, hinged on the axis at one end at angle θ: I = (mℓ²/3)sin²θ. ∫(s sinθ)²(m/ℓ)ds from 0 to ℓ gives (mℓ²/3)sin²θ.
Solution
1. Ring: mass 2m, radius R, its axis is AB itself, so Iring = (2m)R² = 2mR².
2. Each rod starts on the axis at A, so no parallel-axis shift is needed — only the sin²θ tilt factor.
3. With θ = 30°, sin²30° = (1/2)² = 1/4.
4. One rod: I = (mℓ²/3)sin²30° = (mℓ²/3)(1/4) = mℓ²/12.The tilt cuts the perpendicular-rod value mℓ²/3 down by a factor of four.
5. Both rods are identical and symmetric about AB, so together they give 2 × mℓ²/12 = mℓ²/6.
6. Add the pieces: IAB = 2mR² + mℓ²/6.The 60° between the two rods never enters — each rod's tilt is measured from the axis, not from its partner.

      /   |   \
   --(====|====)--  ring 2m, R : I = 2m R^2

⚡ JEE Trick The tempting error is to use mℓ²/3 per rod and get 2mR² + 2mℓ²/3, forgetting that mℓ²/3 is only for a rod perpendicular to the axis. The second temptation is to use 60° (the angle between the rods) instead of 30°; that would give sin²60° = 3/4 and an answer three times too large. Always measure θ from the rod to the rotation axis.
Answer: IAB = 2mR² + mℓ²/6.
5Find the moment of inertia of a triangular lamina of mass M about the axis of rotation AA' shown in the figure. The lamina is a right triangle whose vertical leg of length b lies along AA' and whose horizontal leg of length h runs perpendicular to AA' from the top vertex. — (Level-5)MOI of a triangular lamina about one legRotational Mechanics 15 — MOI of a Triangular Plate about its base🟡 Similarbh²/6 ⚠
Concept The axis AA' contains one leg of the triangle, so distance from the axis is measured horizontally. Slice the lamina into thin strips parallel to the axis is the wrong choice here; instead slice into strips perpendicular to the axis, each a little rod of length x(y) with one end on the axis. Such a rod has I = (dm)x²/3 about an axis through its end, and the strip width x shrinks linearly from h at the top to 0 at the bottom vertex.
Formula Surface density σ = M/(½bh)= 2M/(bh); strip element dI = (dm)x²/3 with dm = σx dy. Each horizontal strip is a rod rotating about an axis through its own end, hence the 1/3.
Solution
1. Put the right-angle corner at the top on the axis; measure y downward from it, 0 ≤ y ≤ b. The strip half-width is x = h(1 − y/b).
2. Strip mass dm = σx dy = (2M/bh)·h(1 − y/b)dy.
3. Strip contribution dI = dm·x²/3 = (σ/3)x³dy = (σh³/3)(1 − y/b)³dy.Cubing the linear taper is what makes the whole integral elementary.
4. I = (σh³/3)∫0b(1 − y/b)³dy = (σh³/3)(b/4) = σbh³/12.
5. Substitute σ = 2M/(bh): I = (2M/bh)(bh³/12) = Mh²/6.The base length b cancels completely — only the reach h away from the axis matters.
6. Key check: the printed key reads bh²/6. That expression carries no mass and has dimensions of length³, so it cannot be a moment of inertia; the working gives Mh²/6, so the printed b is a typo for M. ⚠

1Moment of inertia comes into play: (a) in translatory motion (b) in rotatory motion (c) in vibratory motion (d) when the body is permanently at rest — (Level-1)Meaning of moment of inertiaRotational Motion 04 — Moment of Inertia: Introduction🟡 Similarbin rotatory motion
Concept Every equation of linear motion has a rotational twin, and in the twin the role of mass is taken by the moment of inertia. Newton's F = ma becomes τ = Iα; the kinetic energy ½mv² becomes ½Iω²; the momentum mv becomes Iω. All of those appear only when a body turns about an axis. In pure translation every particle has the same velocity and no axis exists, so I never enters the description.
Formula τ = Iα, L = Iω, KErot = ½Iω². All three contain an angular quantity, so all three are meaningless without rotation.
Solution
1. Write the definition: I = Σmiri², where ri is distance from an axis of rotation.
2. Without a rotation axis, ri is undefined, so I itself is undefined.This single observation kills options (a) and (d) immediately.
3. Option (a), translatory motion: resistance to change is measured by mass m alone, F = ma; I plays no role.
4. Option (c), vibratory motion: a linear oscillator (spring–mass) uses m; only a torsional or physical pendulum uses I, and that is rotation in disguise, not vibration in general.
5. Option (d), a body permanently at rest: I can still be computed as a geometric property, but it "comes into play" — that is, produces observable dynamics — only when the body turns.
6. Option (b) is the one situation where I is indispensable: τ = Iα governs the motion. Hence (b).
 linear            rotational
 -------           ----------
 m                 I = sum m r^2
 v                 w
 F = m a           tau = I alpha
 KE = m v^2/2      KE = I w^2/2
 I appears only in the right column
⚡ JEE Trick Students pick (c) because a torsional pendulum vibrates and does use I. But that oscillation is rotational; ordinary vibration (a mass on a spring) needs only m. Keep the mapping m ↔ I in mind and the answer is instant. The distractor (d) plays on the fact that I is a property of the body even at rest — true, but it does not act until something rotates.
Answer: (b) in rotatory motion.
2Moment of inertia plays the same role in rotatory motion as in translatory motion is played by (a) velocity (b) acceleration (c) mass (d) force — (Level-1)Linear–rotational analogyRotational Motion 04 — Moment of Inertia: Introduction🟡 Similarcmass
Concept Mass is the measure of a body's reluctance to change its state of linear motion — its inertia. Moment of inertia is the measure of a body's reluctance to change its state of rotational motion. The parallel is not loose analogy but exact substitution: write any rotational law and it is the linear law with m replaced by I, v by ω, F by τ, and a by α. Unlike mass, however, I also depends on where the axis is, because it weights each particle by r².
Formula F = ma maps to τ = Iα; p = mv maps to L = Iω. The quantity standing where m stood is I, so I is the rotational mass.
Solution
1. Compare F = ma with τ = Iα: torque is the analogue of force, angular acceleration of linear acceleration, and I sits exactly where m sits.
2. Compare p = mv with L = Iω: again I occupies the slot held by m.
3. Compare ½mv² with ½Iω²: the same substitution once more.Three independent laws all agreeing is decisive, not a coincidence of notation.
4. Option (a) velocity: v maps to ω, angular velocity, not to I.
5. Option (b) acceleration maps to α and option (d) force maps to torque τ, so neither is the analogue of I.Each distractor is the correct analogue of some other rotational quantity, which is why they look plausible.
6. Therefore the translational quantity whose role I takes over is mass, option (c).
  translation      rotation
  -----------      --------
  m         <-->   I
  v         <-->   w
  a         <-->   alpha
  F         <-->   tau
  p = m v   <-->   L = I w
⚡ JEE Trick The one place the analogy breaks: m is a fixed number for a body, while I changes when you move the axis. A skater pulling in her arms cannot change her mass but she does change her I, and ω rises to keep L = Iω fixed. Examiners love that follow-up, so learn the analogy and its single exception.
Answer: (c) mass.
3The moment of inertia of a body does not depend on (a) the mass of the body (b) the angular velocity of the body (c) the axis of rotation of the body (d) the distribution of the mass in the body — (Level-1)What I depends onMoment of Inertia of a Rigid Body — Rotational Motion🟡 Similarbthe angular velocity of the body
Concept Read the definition I = Σmiri² and list what actually appears in it: the masses of the elements, and their perpendicular distances from a chosen axis. Nothing about how fast the body is turning is written anywhere in that expression. Moment of inertia is therefore a purely geometric and inertial property of body-plus-axis, fixed before the motion even starts — exactly as mass is fixed independent of speed.
Formula I = Σmiri² = ∫r²dm. The only inputs are m and r; ω never enters, which settles the question by inspection.
Solution
1. (a) Mass: I is directly proportional to total mass for a fixed shape — double every mi and I doubles. So I does depend on mass.
2. (c) Axis: ri is measured from the axis, so shifting the axis changes every ri. A rod gives mℓ²/12 about its centre but mℓ²/3 about its end — a factor of four.This is precisely what the parallel axis theorem quantifies.
3. (d) Distribution: a ring gives mR² while a disc of the same m and R gives ½mR², purely because the mass sits differently. So I does depend on distribution.
4. (b) Angular velocity: ω does not appear in I = ∫r²dm at all.
5. Confirm with dynamics: in L = Iω and KE = ½Iω², I is the constant coefficient and ω the variable — a fixed I is what makes those relations linear and quadratic in ω.If I depended on ω these standard formulas would be wrong.
6. Hence the property I is independent of is the angular velocity: option (b).

4The moment of inertia of a ring of mass m and radius r about an axis passing through the centre and perpendicular to the plane of the ring, is: (a) (1/2)mr² (b) mr² (c) (1/4)mr² (d) (3/4)mr² — (Level-1)Standard MOI · ringMoment of Inertia Derivation (Ring, Rod, Disk, Cylinder)🟡 Similarbmr²
Concept The central perpendicular axis of a ring is the unique axis for which every particle of the ring is equidistant from it. That makes the defining integral trivial: the factor r² is a constant and comes outside, leaving only the total mass. It is the largest moment of inertia any body of mass m can have with all its material inside radius r, because no mass sits closer in to reduce the average of r².
Formula I = ∫r²dm = r²∫dm = mr². Valid only because r is the same constant for every element of a thin ring.
Solution
1. Take an element dm anywhere on the ring; its perpendicular distance from the central axis is r for all such elements.
2. dI = r²dm, and integrating: I = ∫r²dm = r²∫dm = r²m = mr².No integration technique is needed — the constant simply factors out.
3. Radius of gyration k = √(I/m) = r, i.e. the entire mass may be imagined concentrated on the rim, which is literally true here.
4. Option (a) ½mr² is the disc about the same axis, or the ring about a diameter; both are different geometries or axes.
5. Option (c) ¼mr² is the disc about a diameter, from the perpendicular axis theorem Iz = Ix + Iy with Ix = Iy.Every distractor here is a genuine standard result attached to the wrong body or axis.
6. Option (d) (3/4)mr² is the disc about a tangent in its plane (¼ + 1 would be 5/4; 3/4 is simply a decoy). Hence (b).

5The SI unit of angular momentum is: (a) kg·m² (b) kg·m²/s (c) N·m (d) m/s² — (Level-1)Units · angular momentumRotational Motion in One Shot — All Concepts & PYQs🟡 Similarbkg·m²/s
Concept Units are read straight off a defining equation. Angular momentum has two equivalent definitions — L = Iω for a rigid body about an axis, and L = r × p for a particle — and both must give the same unit, which is a useful cross-check. Because ω carries a per-second and I carries kg·m², the product carries kg·m²/s. Note that radians are dimensionless, so they contribute nothing.
Formula L = Iω and equivalently L = mvr. Two routes to the same unit; agreement between them confirms the answer.
Solution
1. Route 1: [I] = kg·m² and [ω] = rad/s = s⁻¹, so [L] = kg·m²·s⁻¹ = kg·m²/s.
2. Route 2: [L] = [m][v][r] = kg × (m/s) × m = kg·m²/s — the same result.Independent derivations agreeing is the strongest check available in a units question.
3. Option (a) kg·m² is the unit of moment of inertia I itself, missing the per-second from ω.
4. Option (c) N·m = kg·m²/s² is torque (and also energy); it differs from L by one extra power of 1/s, since τ = dL/dt.The relation τ = dL/dt is exactly why their units differ by a single second.
5. Option (d) m/s² is linear acceleration — no mass in it at all, so it cannot be a momentum of any kind.
6. An equivalent form worth recognising is J·s (joule-second), the unit of Planck's constant, since J·s = kg·m²/s²·s = kg·m²/s. Hence (b).

⚡ JEE Trick The favourite trap is kg·m², which is I, not L — the option is placed first precisely to catch a fast reader. Remember that torque and angular momentum are one time-derivative apart, so their units are one second apart; if you can write τ = dL/dt you can never confuse N·m with kg·m²/s. Answer: (b) kg·m²/s.

2What is the ratio of moment of inertia of a thin rod about an axis through midpoint and perpendicular to its length and that about an axis through one end and perpendicular to its length? (a) 1/4 (b) 1/3 (c) 1/2 (d) 1 — (Level-2)Rod · centre vs end axisRotational Inertia for a Long Slender Rod🟡 Similara1/4
Concept Both moments of inertia come from the same integral ∫x²dm; only the limits differ. About the midpoint the rod runs from −ℓ/2 to +ℓ/2, so no element is further than ℓ/2 from the axis. 5Find out the moment of inertia of a semi-circular disc about an axis passing through its centre of mass and perpendicular to the plane. — (Level-6)Semicircular disc · shifting to the centre of massMOI of a semi-circular ring and disc about the axis through the centre of mass🟢 ExactIcm = MR²/2 − M(4R/3π)²
Concept The easy axis for a semicircular disc is the one through the centre O of the parent circle, where the sector rule gives ½MR² regardless of the opening angle. But the centre of mass is not at O — a half disc is lopsided, and its centroid sits at 4R/3π from the flat edge along the symmetry line. The parallel axis theorem then runs backwards: instead of adding Md² to reach a shifted axis, we subtract it to descend from O to the centre of mass, which is always the minimum.
Formula IO = Icm + Md² with IO = MR²/2 and d = 4R/3π. The theorem is always anchored at the centre of mass, so O is the shifted axis and Icm the unknown.
Solution
1. Start at O: for any sector of a uniform disc, IO = ½MR² about the central axis perpendicular to the plane.The angular extent cancels between mass and inertia, as established in Level-5 question 6.
2. Locate the centroid: ycm = 4R/(3π) ≈ 0.4244R, measured from O along the axis of symmetry.
3. Rearrange the theorem: Icm = IO − Mycm² = MR²/2 − M(4R/3π)².
4. Evaluate the bracket: (4/3π)² = 16/9π² = 0.18006, so Icm = MR²(0.5 − 0.18006) = 0.31994MR².Closed form: Icm = MR²(9π² − 32)/(18π²), useful if the paper wants an exact surd-free expression.
5. Direction check: Icm < IO, as it must be, since the centre-of-mass axis minimises the moment of inertia over all parallel axes.A result larger than ½MR² would mean the sign of the shift was flipped.
6. Compare the semicircular wire: IO = MR² and d = 2R/π, giving Icm = MR²(1 − 4/π²) = 0.5947MR² — same method, different two constants.
