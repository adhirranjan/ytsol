★ Core ideaFriction is not a fixed number — it is a constraint. Static friction takes whatever value keeps the surfaces from sliding, up to a ceiling μsN. Once that ceiling is breached the contact switches to a fixed μkN. Almost every friction question is really the single question: "has the ceiling been breached yet?" Answer that first, then write Newton's second law.
💡 Method that never fails1. Assume no slipping and find the common acceleration. 2. Work out the friction that assumption requires. 3. Compare it with μsN. If required ≤ available, the assumption holds. If not, the surfaces slip — put in f = μkN and re-solve each body separately. (This is exactly what L-4's examples do.)
L-1

Nature, Origin, Types & Laws of Friction

  • Friction is the resistance that opposes relative motion (or the tendency to relative motion) between two surfaces in contact. It is a contact force and it acts along the surface.
  • It always opposes relative motion — not necessarily the motion you see. That distinction is the whole of the "direction of friction" section below.

Why friction exists

  • Interlocking: no surface is truly smooth. Under a microscope even polished glass shows ridges and valleys, and the ridges of one surface lock into the valleys of the other. Rougher surface ⇒ more irregularities ⇒ larger friction.
  • Intermolecular force: the real area of contact is far smaller than the apparent area. At those few contact points the molecules are close enough to bond; sliding breaks and re-forms those bonds continuously, which is also why friction generates heat and vibration.
💡 Why area doesn't appear in the formulaPressing harder flattens the asperities and raises the real contact area in proportion to N — which is exactly why f ∝ N and not f ∝ (apparent area). Turning a book on its edge changes the apparent area, not the real one.

The three types

  • Static friction — surfaces at rest relative to each other. Ranges from 0 up to fL = μsN. Walking is only possible because of it.
  • Kinetic (sliding) friction — surfaces sliding over each other, fk = μkN. Weaker than limiting static friction, which is why furniture is easier to keep moving than to start moving.
  • Rolling friction — a body rolls instead of sliding, fr = μrN. Much the weakest of the three; the entire reason wheels exist.

Direction of friction — the part people get wrong

  • Kinetic: friction on body A slipping over body B is opposite to the velocity of A relative to B, not opposite to A's velocity in the ground frame.
  • Book's example: a small block slips backwards off a long wheeled box moving right. To the ground observer both move right — yet the block's velocity relative to the box is leftward, so friction on the block acts rightward, and by Newton's third law friction on the box acts leftward.
  • Static: the direction is whatever keeps the bodies from sliding. Find it from Newton's first/second law, or by the trick: imagine the surfaces smooth, see which way the body would then slip, and point the friction the other way.
  • Book's example: A sits on B, B is pulled right by F. With small F nothing moves, so the net force on A is zero and friction on A is zero. Increase F until both accelerate right — now A needs a rightward net force, and the only horizontal force on it is friction, so friction on A is rightward.
⚠ Trap"Friction opposes motion" is a half-truth. Friction is what accelerates A in the example above, and it is what pushes you forward when you walk. It opposes relative sliding at the contact, and can perfectly well be the driving force on a body.

The five laws of dry friction

  • (a) fk = μkN, proportional to the normal reaction — Amonton's first law. No slipping requires fs ≤ μsN.
  • (b) Independent of the area and shape of the contacting surfaces, as long as N is the same — Amonton's second law.
  • (c) At moderate speeds, independent of the relative velocity — Coulomb's law of friction.
  • (d) Kinetic friction is opposite to the velocity of the body relative to the body exerting it.
  • (e) It depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact.
  • Dry (un-lubricated) friction — one dry surface sliding on another.
  • Film friction — a thick layer of oil separates the surfaces, so one oil layer slides on another with no direct solid contact. Friction is greatly reduced.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example 1 · L-1
A block slides down an incline of angle 30° with an acceleration g/4. Find the coefficient of kinetic friction.
Sliding down ⇒ friction is kinetic and acts up the incline. Along the incline: mg sin30° − f = ma with a = g/4.
mg/2 − f = mg/4 ⇒ f = mg/4.
Perpendicular: N = mg cos30° = (√3/2)mg.
μk = f/N = (mg/4) / ((√3/2)mg) = 2/(4√3).
μk = 1/(2√3) ≈ 0.289
Book Example 2 · L-1
A 2.5 kg block on a rough horizontal surface does not slide below a horizontal force of 15 N. With 15 N applied and a gentle push to start it, it covers the first 10 m in 5 s. Find μs and μk. (g = 10)
It is on the verge of sliding at 15 N ⇒ that force equals limiting friction: μsN = F ⇒ μs = F/Mg = 15/(2.5×10) = 0.6.
Once moving, from rest: x = ½at² ⇒ 10 = ½a(5)² ⇒ a = 20/25 = 0.8 m s⁻².
Newton's second law while sliding: F − μkMg = Ma ⇒ 15 − μk(25) = 2.5(0.8) = 2 ⇒ μk(25) = 13.
μs = 0.6  ·  μk = 13/25 = 0.52  (note μk < μs, as it must be)
Book Example 3 · L-1
A 2 kg body lies on a rough incline of 30°, μs = 0.2. Find the force parallel to the incline needed to make it move (a) up the incline, (b) down the incline.
(a) Pushing it up ⇒ friction acts down the incline at its limiting value: F = mg sinθ + μmg cosθ = mg(sinθ + μcosθ).
= 20(sin30° + 0.2 cos30°) = 20(0.5 + 0.1732) = 20(0.6732).
(b) Moving down ⇒ friction acts up. Limiting friction f = μmg cosθ = (0.2)(20)cos30° = 2√3 ≈ 3.46 N.
Gravity's component along the incline is mg sin30° = 10 N, which already exceeds 3.46 N — the block is sliding down on its own.
(a) F = 13.46 N  ·  (b) F = 0 N (it is already in motion)
Book Example 4 · L-1
Two 1.0 kg blocks on a rough table (μ = 0.2 under each) are joined by a string; the leading one runs over a pulley at the table edge to a hanging 0.50 kg block. Find (a) the acceleration, (b) the tension between the 1.0 kg blocks, (c) the tension attached to the 0.50 kg block. (g = 10)
Let m₁ = 0.5 kg (hanging), m₂ = m₃ = 1 kg. Hanging block: m₁g − T₁ = m₁a ⇒ 5 − T₁ = 0.5a … (i)
Front table block: T₁ − T₂ − μm₂g = m₂a ⇒ T₁ − T₂ − 2 = a ⇒ T₁ − T₂ = a + 2 … (ii)
Rear table block: T₂ − μm₃g = m₃a ⇒ T₂ − 2 = a ⇒ T₂ = a + 2 … (iii)
(iii) into (ii): T₁ = 2a + 4 … (iv). Put into (i): 5 − 2a − 4 = 0.5a ⇒ 1 = 2.5a.
a = 0.4 m s⁻² ⇒ T₁ = 2(0.4) + 4 = 4.8 N, T₂ = 0.4 + 2 = 2.4 N.
a = 0.4 m s⁻²  ·  tension between the 1 kg blocks T₂ = 2.4 N  ·  tension on the 0.5 kg block T₁ = 4.8 N
L-2

Static vs Kinetic Friction · Magnitudes & the f–F Graph

Magnitude of static friction

  • "Static" means not moving: the contact surfaces do not slide relative to one another. Static friction is self-adjusting — it matches whatever force is trying to cause sliding.
  • It has a ceiling. The limiting friction is the largest static friction a pair of surfaces can supply, measured as the force needed just before motion starts.
static friction range0 ≤ fs ≤ fmax = μsN   (fmax is independent of the contact area when N is fixed)

Magnitude of kinetic friction

  • Once sliding starts the friction drops to a fixed value fk = μkN and stays there.
  • Three rules: independent of relative velocity at conventional speeds; independent of contact area; proportional to N and to the roughness/stickiness of the pair.
  • A perfectly frictionless surface has μk = 0.

⚠ Book wordingThe module states the speed-independence of kinetic friction as "provided the velocity is less than 10 m/s". There is no such universal cut-off — the correct statement is Coulomb's law (c): independent of speed at moderate speeds. Treat the 10 m/s as an illustration, not a formula.

Graph of friction against applied force

  • Push a packet on a table with a slowly increasing force F. While it does not move, f = F exactly — the graph is a straight line of slope 1 (45°) through the origin.
  • At F = fL the packet breaks away. Friction then drops to the constant kinetic value and stays flat however hard you push.
  • So the curve is: a 45° line up to the peak fL, a small sudden drop, then a horizontal line at fk.
💡 Reading the graph in an examThe peak gives μs = fL/N and the flat part gives μk = fk/N. The drop at the peak is the visual statement of μk < μs.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example 1 · L-2
A 30 kg boy sits on a horse that accelerates at 2.0 m s⁻². (a) If he does not slide back, what friction does the horse exert on him? (b) If he does slide back, what can be said about μs? (g = 10)
(a) He is not sliding, so friction is the only horizontal force on him and it supplies his acceleration: fs = Ma = (30)(2) = 60 N, directed forward.
(b) The most the contact can supply is fmax = μsMg = μs(300) = 300μs.
Sliding back means the required 60 N exceeds that ceiling: 300μs < 60.
(a) fs = 60 N forward  ·  (b) μs < 0.2
Book Example 2 · L-2
A 2 kg block on a table (μs = 0.2) is joined by a light string over a smooth pulley to a hanging mass m. What is the largest m for which nothing moves? (g = 10)
System in equilibrium: hanging mass gives T = mg … (i); table block gives T = f … (ii).
At the largest m the friction sits at its cap: f = μsN = (0.2)(2)(10) = 4 N.
Equating: mg = μsN ⇒ m = μsN/g = (0.2)(20)/10.
mmax = 0.4 kg
Book Example 3 · L-2
A 20 kg box is pulled along a horizontal surface with μk = 0.25. Find the friction the surface exerts on it. (g = 10)
Horizontal pull ⇒ N = Mg = (20)(10) = 200 N.
fk = μkN = (0.25)(200).
fk = 50 N, opposite to the motion
Book Example 4 · L-2
A rope of length L and mass M is pulled along a rough floor by a horizontal force F = Mg applied at one end. μk = 1/2. Find the tension at the midpoint of the rope.
Whole rope: F − μMg = Ma ⇒ Mg − Mg/2 = Ma ⇒ a = g/2.
Now take the rear half (mass M/2, normal reaction Mg/2). Its friction is f′ = μ(Mg/2) = Mg/4.
Newton's second law on that half: T − f′ = (M/2)a ⇒ T − Mg/4 = (M/2)(g/2) = Mg/4.
T = Mg/2  (exactly half the applied force, because mass and friction both scale with length)
Book Example 5 · L-2
A block on a rough floor is pushed with a horizontal force F. The friction-versus-F graph rises at angle θ and breaks away at F = 100 N. If θ = 15°, find the maximum retarding force.
Reading the line's slope: tanθ = fk/F ⇒ fk = F tanθ.
= 100 tan15° = 100(0.2679).
The maximum retarding force is larger than this, because the peak of the graph (limiting static friction) sits above the kinetic plateau.
fk = 26.79 N; maximum retarding force > 26.79 N (it is the static peak, not the kinetic value)
Book Example 6 · L-2
Same arrangement as Example 5, but θ = 25°. Find the kinetic friction.
fk = F tanθ = 100 tan25° = 100(0.4663).
fk = 46.63 N

These two examples only work as arithmetic on a hypothetical line. L-3

Angle of Friction & Angle of Repose

Angle of friction λ (also written φ)

  • A rough surface exerts two forces on a body: the normal reaction N and the friction μN. Their resultant is the total contact force R.
  • When the body is about to slip, the angle this resultant makes with the normal is the largest it can be — that maximum angle is the angle of friction.
angle of frictiontanλ = μN / N = μ  ⇒  λ = tan⁻¹μ

Angle of repose α

  • Put a block on an incline and raise the angle slowly. At a critical angle it just begins to slide — that is the angle of repose.
  • As θ grows, N = mg cosθ falls (so limiting friction fL = μmg cosθ falls) while the driving force mg sinθ rises. They must cross:
at θ = αfL = F  ⇒  μmg cosα = mg sinα  ⇒  tanα = μ  ⇒  α = tan⁻¹μ
★ RememberAngle of repose = angle of friction, numerically. Both are tan⁻¹μ. One is measured on an incline, the other in the contact-force triangle — same number.
  • θ < α → F < fL, block stationary; friction is static and equals mg sinθ, not μmg cosθ.
  • θ = α → F = fL, block on the verge of sliding.
  • θ > α → F > fL, block slides with a = g(sinθ − μcosθ).
⚠ The single most common mistakeWriting f = μmg cosθ for a block that is sitting still on an incline. That formula is only the ceiling. Below the angle of repose the actual friction is mg sinθ.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example 1 · L-3
Find the angle of friction λ for a body on a horizontal surface about to slip, if μ = √3.
λ = tan⁻¹μ = tan⁻¹√3.
λ = 60°
⚠ Book misprint: the module's last line reads "μ = 60°". It is the angle that is 60°; μ stays √3.
Book Example 2 · L-3
A block of mass m rests on a rough horizontal surface whose angle of friction is φ. It is pulled by a force F at angle θ above the horizontal. Find the minimum F needed to move it.
Angle of friction φ ⇒ μ = tanφ. The upward component F sinθ lightens the block: N = mg − F sinθ.
On the verge of moving: F cosθ = f = μN = μ(mg − F sinθ).
F cosθ + μF sinθ = μmg ⇒ F = μmg / (cosθ + μ sinθ).
Substitute μ = sinφ/cosφ and multiply numerator and denominator by cosφ: F = mg sinφ / (cosθ cosφ + sinθ sinφ).
Fmin = mg sinφ / cos(θ − φ)
⚡ JEE trick: the denominator is largest when θ = φ, so the overall smallest pull is F = mg sinφ, applied at the angle of friction itself. Pull at the angle of friction and you work least hard — a standard one-mark result.
⚠ Book misprints: the module writes "μ = tanθ" where it means tanφ, prints "N + mg − F sinθ" for "N = mg − F sinθ", and labels the final answer "f =" instead of "F =". The result quoted above is correct.
Book Example 3 · L-3
A 1 kg particle rests on a plane inclined at 30° and is just about to slip. Find μ.
"Just about to slip" means the inclination has reached the angle of repose, so α = 30°.
μ = tanα = tan30°.
μ = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577  (the mass is irrelevant — m cancels)
Book Example 4 · L-3
The angle of repose is 45°. Find the friction force, net force and acceleration of a block on the incline when (a) θ = 30°, (b) θ = 45°, (c) θ = 60°.
μ = tanα = tan45° = 1.
(a) θ = 30° < α: limiting friction would be μmg cos30° = (√3/2)mg, but the driving force is only mg sin30° = mg/2. Since mg/2 < (√3/2)mg the block stays put, so friction is static and equals the driving force.
⇒ f = mg/2, net force = 0, a = 0.
(b) θ = 45° = α: on the verge of sliding, still in equilibrium. f = mg sin45° = mg/√2 (which is also μmg cos45° — the two coincide exactly at θ = α), net force = 0, a = 0.
(c) θ = 60° > α: it slides, so friction is kinetic: fk = μmg cos60° = (1)(mg)(1/2) = mg/2.
a = g(sin60° − μcos60°) = g(√3/2 − 1/2) = ((√3 − 1)/2)g, and net force = ma = ((√3 − 1)/2)mg down the incline.
(a) f = mg/2, a = 0  ·  (b) f = mg/√2, a = 0  ·  (c) f = mg/2, a = ((√3−1)/2)g ≈ 0.37g down the incline
L-4

Two-Block (Stacked) Problems

  • Two blocks in contact either move together (one rigid body; the friction between them is static and internal) or slip (the friction between them is kinetic, capped at μsN). Deciding which is the entire problem.
💡 The three-step test1. Assume they move together and find a = Fnet/(m₁+m₂). 2. Isolate the block that is not pushed and find the friction f that would give it that a. 3. If f ≤ μsN the assumption is right; if f > μsN they slip, and you re-solve each block with f = μkN fixed.
smooth floor, force on the lower blockThey move together while a ≤ μsg  ⇒  Fmax = μsg (mtop + mbottom)
⚠ TrapThe normal reaction at the floor carries the weight of both blocks, Nfloor = (m₁+m₂)g — but the normal reaction between the blocks carries only the top one, N = mtopg. Mixing these two up is the commonest slip in this lecture.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example 1 · L-4
A 4 kg block sits on a 5 kg block on the ground; μ between them is 0.5 and μ between the 5 kg block and the ground is 0.3. A force F = 40 N acts on the 4 kg block. Find the acceleration of each. (g = 10)
Step 1 — assume they move together. Ground friction f₁ = μ₁(m₁+m₂)g = (0.3)(9)(10) = 27 N ⇒ a = (F − f₁)/(m₁+m₂) = (40 − 27)/9 = 13/9 ≈ 1.44 m s⁻².
Step 2 — the friction that requires. On the top 4 kg block: F − f₂ = m₂a ⇒ 40 − f₂ = 4(13/9) = 52/9 ⇒ f₂ = 308/9 = 34.22 N.
Step 3 — compare. That interface can supply at most f₂,max = μ₂m₂g = (0.5)(4)(10) = 20 N. Since 34.22 > 20, they slip.
Top block with kinetic friction fixed at 20 N: a₂ = (40 − 20)/4 = 5 m s⁻².
Bottom block is driven forward by only that 20 N, while the ground can hold back up to 27 N ⇒ it does not move.
4 kg block: a = 5 m s⁻²  ·  5 kg block: a = 0 (stationary)
Book Example 2 · L-4
A 5 kg block sits on an 8 kg block, μ between them = 0.4. F is applied to the 8 kg block. Find the maximum F for which both move together if (a) the floor is frictionless, (b) the floor has μ = 0.3. (g = 10)
(a) Together: a = F/(5+8) = F/13. The only horizontal force on the top 5 kg block is friction, so f = m₁a = 5F/13 … (i)
That friction caps at f = μ₁m₁g = (0.4)(5)(10) = 20 N … (ii)
Equate: 5F/13 = 20 ⇒ F = 52 N.
(b) Floor friction now opposes: f₂ = μ₂(m₁+m₂)g = (0.3)(13)(10) = 39 N ⇒ a = (F − 39)/13.
The top block still needs f₁ = m₁a ≤ 20 N: 20 = 5(F − 39)/13 ⇒ 52 = F − 39.
(a) Fmax = 52 N  ·  (b) Fmax = 91 N
⚠ Book misprint: the module writes "f = μm₁a = (0.4)(5)(10)". The arithmetic is right but the symbol should be g, not a: f = μm₁g.
Book Example 3 · L-4
Block A (weight 100 N) rests on block B (weight 250 N), which rests on a smooth surface; μs between A and B is 0.4. F is applied horizontally to A. Find each acceleration for (a) F = 30 N, (b) F = 250 N. (g = 10)
Masses: m₁ = 100/10 = 10 kg (A), m₂ = 250/10 = 25 kg (B). Limiting friction between them: fL = μm₁g = (0.4)(100) = 40 N.
(a) F = 30 N < 40 N ⇒ static friction can hold; the pair moves as one on the smooth floor.
a = F/(m₁+m₂) = 30/35 = 6/7 ≈ 0.86 m s⁻² for both.
(b) F = 250 N > 40 N ⇒ they slip; friction is kinetic and fixed at 40 N.
Block A: F − f = m₁a₁ ⇒ 250 − 40 = 10a₁ ⇒ a₁ = 21 m s⁻².
Block B: the only horizontal force on it is that same 40 N (the floor is smooth) ⇒ a₂ = 40/25 = 1.6 m s⁻².
(a) both at 6/7 ≈ 0.86 m s⁻²  ·  (b) A at 21 m s⁻², B at 1.6 m s⁻²

Worked examples from the module
Strong Box · PYQ 1 (JEE Main 2026, 24 Jan Shift 1)
Blocks A, B and C weigh 4 kg, 6 kg and 8 kg. μ between any two surfaces is 0.5. A rests on B, B rests on C, C rests on the ground; a string runs from B over a pulley fixed to the wall and back to C. Find F needed to slide C at constant speed. (g = 10)
The string reverses direction at the pulley, so as C moves left, B moves right by the same amount — they slide over each other, and the same tension T acts on both.
A rides on B with no relative slipping and no acceleration ⇒ friction on A is zero (but A's weight still presses on the B–C interface).
B–C interface: N = (4+6)(10) = 100 N ⇒ fBC = 0.5(100) = 50 N.
C–ground: N = (4+6+8)(10) = 180 N ⇒ fground = 0.5(180) = 90 N.
Block B at constant speed: T = fBC = 50 N.
Block C at constant speed: F = T + fBC + fground = 50 + 50 + 90.
F = 190 N
Strong Box · PYQ 2 (JEE Main 2026, 28 Jan Shift 1)
A 5 kg block is on a 30° incline with μ = √3/2. What force must be applied so that it moves down without acceleration?
Driving force down the incline: mg sin30° = 50(0.5) = 25 N.
N = mg cos30° = 50(√3/2) = 25√3 N ⇒ f = μN = (√3/2)(25√3) = 37.5 N, acting up the incline as it slides down.
Friction (37.5) exceeds gravity's pull (25), so the block will not slide on its own — a push is needed down the incline.
Constant velocity ⇒ F + mg sin30° = f ⇒ F = 37.5 − 25.
F = 12.5 N down the incline → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 3 (JEE Main 2024)
An object takes n times as long to slide down a rough 45° incline as down an identical smooth 45° incline. Find μk.
Smooth: as = g sin45°. Rough: ar = g(sin45° − μ cos45°) = g sin45°(1 − μ), since sin45° = cos45°.
Same distance from rest: s = ½at² ⇒ t ∝ 1/√a, so n = tr/ts = √(as/ar).
n² = as/ar = 1/(1 − μ) ⇒ 1 − μ = 1/n².
μ = 1 − 1/n² → option (c)
Strong Box · PYQ 4 (JEE Main 2024)
A block sits on a surface whose vertical cross-section is y = x²/4. With μ = 0.5, what is the greatest height above the ground at which it can rest without slipping?
The local incline angle satisfies tanθ = dy/dx = x/2.
It rests as long as tanθ ≤ μ (angle of repose). Highest point: x/2 = 0.5 ⇒ x = 1.
y = x²/4 = 1/4.
hmax = 1/4 m → option (a)
Strong Box · PYQ 5 (JEE Main 2024)
On a doubly-inclined plane (53° and 37°) two blocks M and m are joined by a string over an ideal pulley; μ = 0.25 on both faces. Find m if M = 10 kg moves down with a = 2 m s⁻². (g = 10, tan37° = 3/4)
sin53° = 0.8, cos53° = 0.6, sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8.
M on the 53° face, moving down (friction acts up): Mg sin53° − T − μMg cos53° = Ma.
80 − T − 0.25(100)(0.6) = 20 ⇒ 80 − T − 15 = 20 ⇒ T = 45 N.
m on the 37° face, moving up (friction acts down): T − mg sin37° − μmg cos37° = ma.
45 − 6m − 2m = 2m ⇒ 45 = 10m.
m = 4.5 kg → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 6 (JEE Main 2024)
A 5 kg block rests on a 30° incline, μ = 0.1. F₁ just moves it up, F₂ just prevents it sliding down. Find F₁ − F₂. (g = 10)
Going up, friction acts down: F₁ = mg sinθ + μmg cosθ.
Holding it, friction acts up: F₂ = mg sinθ − μmg cosθ.
The gravity terms cancel on subtraction: F₁ − F₂ = 2μmg cosθ = 2(0.1)(50)(√3/2).
F₁ − F₂ = 5√3 N → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 7 (JEE Main 2024)
A 1 kg block is pushed 10 m up a 60° incline by a 10 N force parallel to the surface, μs = 0.1. Find the work done against friction. (g = 10)
N = mg cos60° = (1)(10)(0.5) = 5 N.
f = μN = (0.1)(5) = 0.5 N, acting down the incline as the block moves up.
Wagainst friction = f × d = (0.5)(10).
W = 5 J → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 8 (JEE Main 2023)
A 5 kg block at rest on a rough table is pushed by a 30 N horizontal force and slides 50 m in 10 s. Find μk. (g = 10)
From rest: s = ½at² ⇒ 50 = ½a(100) ⇒ a = 1 m s⁻².
F − μmg = ma ⇒ 30 − μ(50) = 5(1) ⇒ μ(50) = 25.
μk = 0.50 → option (c)
Strong Box · PYQ 9 (JEE Main 2023)
A 10 kg block on a horizontal surface is pulled by F at 30° above the horizontal. With μs = 0.25 it just starts to move. Find F. (g = 10)
The upward component lightens the block: N = mg − F sin30° = 100 − 0.5F.
Just about to move: F cos30° = μsN ⇒ 0.866F = 0.25(100 − 0.5F).
0.866F = 25 − 0.125F ⇒ 0.991F = 25.
F ≈ 25.2 N → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 10 (JEE Main 2023)
On a 45° incline, the force needed to just push a block up is 2 times the force needed to just stop it sliding down. Find μ.
Up: Fup = mg(sin45° + μ cos45°). Hold: Fhold = mg(sin45° − μ cos45°).
At 45° the sine and cosine are equal, so Fup = 2Fhold becomes 1 + μ = 2(1 − μ).
1 + μ = 2 − 2μ ⇒ 3μ = 1.
μ = 1/3 ≈ 0.33 → option (a)
Strong Box · PYQ 11 (JEE Main 2023)
A 0.1 kg bullet at 400 m s⁻¹ embeds in a 3.9 kg block on a rough floor; the pair travels 20 m before stopping. Find μ. (g = 10)
Momentum conservation in the embedding: (0.1)(400) = (0.1 + 3.9)v ⇒ 40 = 4v ⇒ v = 10 m s⁻¹.
Then friction alone decelerates it: v² = 2(μg)s ⇒ 100 = 2μ(10)(20) = 400μ.
μ = 0.25 → option (d)
Strong Box · PYQ 12 (JEE Main 2023) ⚠ out of chapter
A 600 pF capacitor is charged by a 200 V supply, disconnected, then connected to an uncharged 600 pF capacitor. Find the electrostatic energy lost.
⚠ Book defect: this is an electrostatics question printed inside the Friction Strong Box. It has nothing to do with P-7; it is solved here only for completeness — do not revise it as friction.
Initial energy Ui = ½CV² = ½(600 pF)(200 V)² = 12 μJ.
Charge is conserved: Q = CV = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ C, now spread over 1200 pF ⇒ V′ = Q/2C = 100 V.
Uf = ½(1200 pF)(100 V)² = 6 μJ.
Energy lost = 12 − 6 = 6 μJ
Strong Box · PYQ 13 (JEE Main 2023)
A block slides down a 30° incline with acceleration g/4. Find μk.
a = g(sinθ − μcosθ) ⇒ g/4 = g(sin30° − μ cos30°).
1/4 = 0.5 − μ(√3/2) ⇒ μ(√3/2) = 1/4.
μ = 2/(4√3) = 1/(2√3).
μk = 1/(2√3) → option (b)  (same physics as L-1 Example 1)
Strong Box · PYQ 14 (JEE Main 2022)
A uniform chain of length 6 m rests on a table with part of it hanging over the edge; μs = 0.5. Find the maximum length that can hang.
Let l hang and (6 − l) lie on the table; λ = mass per unit length.
The hanging weight is held by the limiting friction on the part still on the table: λlg = μ λ(6 − l)g.
l = 0.5(6 − l) ⇒ 1.5l = 3.
l = 2 m  (general result: hanging fraction = μ/(1+μ) = 0.5/1.5 = 1/3 of 6 m)
Strong Box · PYQ 15 (JEE Main 2022)
m = 2 kg rests on M = 8 kg on a smooth table; μs between the blocks is 0.5. Find the maximum horizontal F on M for which they move together. (g = 9.8)
Together: a = F/(m + M) = F/10.
The top block is accelerated by friction alone: f = ma ≤ μsmg ⇒ a ≤ μsg = 0.5(9.8) = 4.9 m s⁻².
Fmax = 10amax = 10(4.9).
Fmax = 49 N → option (c)  (note m cancels out of a ≤ μg)
Strong Box · PYQ 16 (JEE Main 2022)
A bag is gently dropped on a belt running at 2 m s⁻¹; μ = 0.4. Find the distance the bag travels during the slipping phase. (g = 10)
While it slips, friction drives the bag forward: a = μg = (0.4)(10) = 4 m s⁻².
Slipping ends when the bag reaches belt speed: t = v/a = 2/4 = 0.5 s.
Bag's displacement: s = ½at² = ½(4)(0.25) = 0.5 m. (The belt moves 2 × 0.5 = 1 m, so the relative slip is also 0.5 m — both readings give the same option.)
0.5 m → option (b)
Strong Box · PYQ 17 (JEE Advanced 2011, Paper-1)
On a 45° incline the force needed to just push a block up is 3 times the force needed to just prevent it sliding down. If N = 10μ, find N.
mg(sin45° + μ cos45°) = 3 mg(sin45° − μ cos45°). At 45° the trig terms are equal, so 1 + μ = 3(1 − μ).
1 + μ = 3 − 3μ ⇒ 4μ = 2 ⇒ μ = 0.5.
N = 10μ = 10(0.5).
N = 5
Strong Box · PYQ 18 (JEE Advanced 2010)
A block on an incline θ with friction μ (tanθ > μ) is held by a force P parallel to the plane, up-the-plane taken positive. As P goes from P₁ = mg(sinθ − μcosθ) to P₂ = mg(sinθ + μcosθ), what does the f-versus-P graph look like?
At P = P₁ the block is on the verge of sliding down, so friction is at its maximum up the plane: f = +μmg cosθ.
At P = P₂ it is on the verge of sliding up, so friction is at its maximum down the plane: f = −μmg cosθ.
Between them the block is in equilibrium: P + f = mg sinθ ⇒ f = mg sinθ − P — a straight line of slope −1.
So the graph falls linearly from +μmg cosθ at P₁, crosses zero at P = mg sinθ, and reaches −μmg cosθ at P₂.
Option (a) — the straight line with negative slope crossing the P-axis
Strong Box · PYQ 19 (JEE Advanced 2009, Paper-1)
A block of base 10 cm × 10 cm and height 15 cm sits on an incline with μ = √3. The inclination is raised gradually from 0°. What happens?
It would slide when tanθ > μ = √3, i.e. θ > 60°.
It would topple when the vertical through the centre of mass leaves the base, i.e. tanθ > (base/height) = 10/15 = 2/3, i.e. θ > 33.7°.
33.7° < 60°, so the toppling threshold is reached first — the block tips over before it ever slides.
Option (b) — it stays at rest up to a certain θ and then topples
⚡ JEE trick: for every "slide or topple" question just compare μ with b/h. Whichever is smaller happens first: μ < b/h ⇒ slides first; μ > b/h ⇒ topples first.

High-Yield Traps & Quick Recall

  • Static friction is not μsN. That is only its ceiling. A stationary block on a gentle incline has f = mg sinθ. Use μsN only at the verge of slipping.
  • Pull vs push at an angle. Pulling at θ above the horizontal reduces N to mg − F sinθ (easier); pushing at θ below increases it to mg + F sinθ (harder). Same magnitude of force, very different answer.
  • Angle of repose = angle of friction = tan⁻¹μ. One number, two names.
  • Stopping distance v²/(2μg) does not contain m. A loaded truck and an empty one stop in the same distance on the same tyres — a favourite one-liner.
  • The normal reaction is not always mg. On an incline it is mg cosθ; in a lift it is m(g ± a); against a vertical wall it is the horizontal push, and the friction there balances the weight.
  • Stacked blocks: the floor's N carries both masses, the interface N carries only the top one.
  • μk < μs always, which is why the f–F graph has a drop at break-away, and why ABS brakes (rolling, near-static contact) beat locked wheels (kinetic).
  • Direction first, magnitude second. Decide which way the surfaces would slide if they were smooth, point the friction against that, and only then put in a number.
  • "Slide or topple" is settled by comparing μ against base/height, nothing else.
