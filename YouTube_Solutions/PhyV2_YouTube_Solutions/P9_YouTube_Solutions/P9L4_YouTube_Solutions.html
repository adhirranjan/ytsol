1A ball is dropped on the ground from a height of 1 metre. What is the height to which the ball would rebound? Given: coefficient of restitution e = 0.6. — (Suggested, Level-1)Coefficient of restitution, rebound heightA ball is dropped on the ground from a height of 1m. The coefficient of restitution is 0.6...🟢 Exact0.36 m
Concept Coefficient of restitution e compares speeds, not heights directly, at the instant of impact: e = (speed of separation)/(speed of approach). But since a free fall/rise converts height to speed by the same formula v=√(2gh) on both sides of the bounce, the height ratio becomes e², not e — this squaring is the one fact this whole question tests.
Formula Speed just before impact: u=√(2gH). Speed just after impact: v=e·u. Rebound height: h=v²/(2g). Combining: h=e²H. m/s² is dimensionally consistent throughout; e itself is a pure ratio of speeds so it carries no units.
Solution
1. Height of fall: H = 1 m. The ground is fixed, so it contributes nothing to the impact speed.
2. Speed just before striking the ground: u = √(2gH) = √(2×9.8×1) ≈ 4.43 m/s (g = 9.8 taken since the book gives no numeric g need — the g cancels out anyway, see step 5).Writing this out is optional once you see g will cancel, but it fixes the physical picture.
3. Rebound speed: v = e·u = 0.6 × 4.43 = 2.658 m/s, by the very definition of e for a collision with a fixed, immovable floor (floor's own speed of approach/separation is zero).
4. Rebound height from v²=2gh: h = v²/(2g) = (0.6u)²/(2g) = 0.36u²/(2g).
5. Substitute u² = 2gH: h = 0.36×2gH/(2g) = 0.36H = 0.36×1 = 0.36 m.g cancelled exactly — the rebound height depends only on e² and the drop height, never on g itself.

   u = √(2gH)                   v = e·u
        │                            │
        ▾                            │
   ═══════════●═══════════════════════   ground (e = 0.6)

   h/H = (v/u)² = e² = 0.36   ⇒   h = 0.36 × 1 m = 0.36 m
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise h = e²H directly — never re-derive it mid-exam. For n successive bounces off a fixed floor, height after the n-th bounce is e^(2n)H, a geometric shrink used constantly in "total distance travelled" problems (see Level-5 Q10 of this lecture). Trap: writing h = eH (forgetting to square) gives 0.6 m, the most common wrong-option distractor in this exact question.
Answer: rebound height = e²H = 0.36 m.
2A sphere of mass m moving with a velocity u hits another stationary sphere of same mass. If e is the coefficient of restitution, what is the ratio of the velocities of the two spheres after the collision? — (Suggested, Level-2)Equal-mass restitution collision, ratio of velocitiesA sphere of mass m moving with a constant velocity u hits stationary sphere of the same mass.🟢 Exact(1−e) / (1+e)
Concept With two unknowns (the two post-collision velocities) you need exactly two equations: momentum conservation (always true) and Newton's restitution law (the collision-specific extra fact). Because the masses are equal here, both equations simplify beautifully and the answer falls out as a clean function of e alone — no m or u survives in the final ratio.
Formula Momentum: mu + 0 = mv₁ + mv₂ ⇒ v₁+v₂=u. Restitution: e = (v₂−v₁)/(u−0) ⇒ v₂−v₁=eu. Both in m/s, e dimensionless. kg cancels immediately from the momentum equation since both masses are m; restitution is already a pure ratio of relative speeds.
Solution
1. Let v₁ = velocity of the originally-moving sphere after impact, v₂ = velocity of the originally-stationary sphere after impact (both along the original line of motion, taken positive forward).
2. Momentum conservation: mu = mv₁+mv₂ ⇒ v₁+v₂ = u. — (i)
3. Newton's restitution law (separation speed = e × approach speed, and the approach speed here is simply u since the target starts at rest): v₂−v₁ = eu. — (ii)
4. Add (i) and (ii): 2v₂ = u(1+e) ⇒ v₂ = u(1+e)/2. Subtract (ii) from (i): 2v₁ = u(1−e) ⇒ v₁ = u(1−e)/2.Notice v₂ > v₁ always (for 0≤e≤1) — the struck sphere can never end up slower than the striker, matching intuition.
5. Form the ratio, and the common factor u/2 cancels exactly: v₁/v₂ = [u(1−e)/2] / [u(1+e)/2] = (1−e)/(1+e).
   before:   u ●──▸           ○ (at rest)
             mass m          mass m

   after:    v₁ ──▸          v₂ ──▸       (v₂ > v₁)

   momentum:   v₁ + v₂ = u
   restitution: v₂ − v₁ = e u
   ⇒ v₁ = u(1−e)/2,  v₂ = u(1+e)/2,   v₁/v₂ = (1−e)/(1+e)
⚡ JEE Trick For equal masses this pair of equations is the single most-recycled result in the chapter — memorise v₁ = u(1−e)/2 and v₂ = u(1+e)/2 outright, they reappear (with role-swaps) in Level-3 Q2 and Level-4 Q9/10 of this very lecture. Sanity check both limits: e=1 (elastic) gives v₁=0, v₂=u — the classic "velocities exchange" result for equal masses; e=0 (perfectly inelastic) gives v₁=v₂=u/2 — they move together, as expected.
Answer: v₁ : v₂ = (1−e) : (1+e).
3A 10 kg ball and a 20 kg ball approach each other with velocities 20 m/s and 10 m/s respectively. What are their velocities after collision if the collision is perfectly elastic? — (Suggested, Level-3)Elastic collision, unequal masses, opposite directionsA 10kg ball and a 20kg ball approach each other with velocities 20m/s and 10m/s respectively...🟢 Exact10 kg ball: 20 m/s reversed  ·  20 kg ball: 10 m/s reversed
Concept "Approach each other" means the two velocities must be given opposite signs in one common direction before anything else is done — the single most common slip in this problem is plugging both speeds in as positive. Once signs are fixed, this is a routine elastic-collision-with-unequal-masses computation using the standard 1-D elastic formulas.
Formula For masses m₁,m₂ with initial velocities u₁,u₂: v₁=[(m₁−m₂)u₁+2m₂u₂]/(m₁+m₂), v₂=[(m₂−m₁)u₂+2m₁u₁]/(m₁+m₂) — all masses in kg, velocities in m/s. Every term has units kg·(m/s)/kg = m/s ✓, and these formulas are just momentum + KE conservation solved simultaneously.
Solution
1. Take the 10 kg ball's direction as positive: m₁=10 kg, u₁=+20 m/s; m₂=20 kg, u₂=−10 m/s (opposite direction, since they approach each other).
2. Check: total momentum p = m₁u₁+m₂u₂ = 10(20)+20(−10) = 200−200 = 0 kg·m/s.A zero total momentum is a useful hint — whatever the balls do afterward, their momenta must still cancel.
3. v₁ = [(10−20)(20)+2(20)(−10)]/(10+20) = [−200−400]/30 = −600/30 = −20 m/s.
4. v₂ = [(20−10)(−10)+2(10)(20)]/(10+20) = [−100+400]/30 = 300/30 = +10 m/s.
5. Check momentum again: 10(−20)+20(10) = −200+200 = 0 ✓, matching step 2. Check KE: before = ½(10)(400)+½(20)(100)=2000+1000=3000 J; after = ½(10)(400)+½(20)(100)=3000 J ✓ elastic confirmed.Both balls simply reverse direction, each keeping its original speed — a signature of equal-and-opposite momentum elastic collisions.
   before:  20 m/s ◂── 10kg      20kg ──▸ 10 m/s
            (leftward +)          (rightward, call −)

   p_total = 10(20) − 20(10) = 0

   after:   10kg ──▸ 20 m/s      20kg ◂── 10 m/s
            (fully reversed)     (fully reversed)
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever total momentum happens to be zero, an elastic collision simply reverses each velocity while keeping each speed unchanged — check this shortcut result against the full formula once, then use it directly in future p_total=0 problems. Trap: forgetting the sign on the approaching ball's velocity is the single most common error, and it silently produces a completely different (wrong) pair of answers.
Answer: 10 kg ball reverses to 20 m/s; 20 kg ball reverses to 10 m/s (both simply turn around).
4A body of mass 5 kg moving along the x-axis with a velocity 2 ms⁻¹. Another body of mass 10 kg is moving along the y-axis with a velocity √3 ms⁻¹. They collide at the origin and stick together. The final velocity of the combined mass is — (Suggested, Level-4) (a) √3 ms⁻¹ (b) (√3+1) ms⁻¹ (c) 4/3 ms⁻¹ (d) None of these2-D perfectly inelastic collision, vector momentumMomentum Problem #1: Objects Collide and Stick Together (solve for final velocity)🟡 Similarc4/3 ms⁻¹
Concept In two dimensions, momentum conservation must be applied component-by-component — the x-momentum before must equal the x-momentum after, completely independently of the y-momentum equation. "Stick together" is the cue for a perfectly inelastic collision, so both bodies share one common final velocity vector once they merge.
Formula p_x: m₁u₁ₓ = (m₁+m₂)Vₓ, p_y: m₂u₂ᵧ = (m₁+m₂)Vᵧ — masses in kg, velocities in m/s. Resultant speed V=√(Vₓ²+Vᵧ²). kg·(m/s) ÷ kg = m/s throughout, and Pythagoras is valid because Vₓ ⊥ Vᵧ.
Solution
1. x-momentum before (only the 5 kg body has any): pₓ = 5 kg × 2 m/s = 10 kg·m/s. y-momentum before (only the 10 kg body has any): pᵧ = 10 kg × √3 m/s = 10√3 kg·m/s.
2. Combined mass after sticking: M = 5+10 = 15 kg.
3. Final x-velocity: Vₓ = pₓ/M = 10/15 = 2/3 m/s. Final y-velocity: Vᵧ = pᵧ/M = 10√3/15 = 2√3/3 m/s.Each axis is solved as if it were a separate 1-D inelastic collision — this decoupling is exactly why the x-axis body contributes nothing to Vᵧ and vice versa.
4. Resultant speed: V = √(Vₓ²+Vᵧ²) = √[(2/3)² + (2√3/3)²] = √[4/9 + 12/9] = √(16/9).
5. V = 4/3 m/s ≈ 1.33 m/s. This matches option (c) exactly.
           y ▲
             │ 10 kg, √3 m/s
             │  ↓
   ──────────●──────────▸ x     5 kg, 2 m/s ▸
         (origin, collision point)

   pₓ = 5×2 = 10,  pᵧ = 10×√3 = 10√3   (kg·m/s)
   M = 15 kg  ⇒  Vₓ=2/3, Vᵧ=2√3/3
   V = √(Vₓ²+Vᵧ²) = √(16/9) = 4/3 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick Compute pₓ and pᵧ as plain numbers first, divide both by the same combined mass, and only take the square root at the very last step — this keeps the arithmetic in whole/simple-surd numbers (10, 10√3, 15) instead of messy decimals throughout. Trap: option (a) √3 comes from forgetting to include the x-momentum entirely; option (b) comes from wrongly adding the two velocities as scalars instead of combining momenta as vectors.
Answer: (c) 4/3 ms⁻¹.
5A ball of mass 'm' strikes the fixed inclined plane after falling through a height 'h'. If it rebounds elastically, then impulse imparted on the ball. — (Suggested, Level-5) [figure: ball falls vertically height h onto a smooth fixed incline of angle θ]Oblique elastic bounce off a fixed smooth inclineA ball of mass m strikes the fixed inclined plane after falling through a height h. if it rebounds...🟢 Exact2m cos θ √(2gh)
Concept "Smooth" incline means the surface can exert no force along its own face — only normal to it. So the velocity component along the incline is completely unaffected by the bounce, while the component perpendicular to the incline reverses exactly (elastic rebound off a fixed, immovable surface). The whole problem reduces to resolving one vertical velocity into these two directions.
Formula Impact speed: u=√(2gh) (m/s), vertically down. Impulse = change in momentum, J=m·Δv (kg·m/s = N·s). Because the tangential component is unchanged, Δv is entirely along the normal to the incline. kg·(m/s) = N·s ✓ — impulse and momentum share units, which is exactly why J=Δp works.
Solution
1. Speed just before impact (free fall through h): u = √(2gh), directed straight down.
2. The incline makes angle θ with the horizontal, so its normal makes angle θ with the vertical. Resolve u along this normal and along the incline surface: normal component = u cos θ (into the surface), tangential component = u sin θ (along the surface).This resolution is the crux of the whole problem — draw the incline, drop the normal, and read the angle θ off the geometry rather than guessing which trig function applies.
3. Tangential component is smooth ⇒ no force acts along it ⇒ it survives unchanged: still u sin θ, same direction, after impact.
4. Normal component undergoes an elastic bounce off a fixed (infinite-mass) surface: it simply reverses sign, magnitude unchanged: from u cos θ (inward) to u cos θ (outward).
5. Since only the normal component changes, the impulse is entirely along the outward normal, with magnitude equal to the full swing: J = m×[u cos θ − (−u cos θ)] = 2mu cos θ.
6. Substitute u = √(2gh): J = 2m cos θ √(2gh).Sanity check: at θ=0° (flat floor) this reduces to J = 2m√(2gh), the familiar "straight bounce" result — a useful limit to test any inclined-collision formula against.
        u = √(2gh) ↓
              │
              ▾
            ╲  θ (from vertical = incline angle)
             ╲ normal
     ─────────╲───────────  smooth fixed incline (angle θ to horizontal)
                    θ

   normal (⊥):  u cosθ  →  reverses to  u cosθ   (impulse here)
   tangent (∥): u sinθ  →  unchanged (smooth ⇒ no tangential force)
   J = m·(2u cosθ) = 2m cosθ √(2gh)
⚡ JEE Trick For any smooth-surface bounce problem, immediately split the impact velocity into normal + tangential components — tangential never changes (smooth), normal reverses (elastic, fixed surface), and impulse is always along the normal only. Trap: option-style distractors 2m√(2gh)sinθ or 2m√(2gh)/cosθ come from resolving along the wrong axis or inverting sin/cos — always confirm θ is measured from the vertical (normal) to the incoming velocity, not from the incline surface.
Answer: Impulse = 2m cos θ √(2gh), directed along the outward normal to the incline.

1During inelastic collision of two particles: (a) (KE)final=(KE)initial (b) (KE)final must be greater than (KE)initial (c) (KE)final must be less than (KE)initial (d) (KE)final may be greater or less than (KE)initial — (Level-1)Definition of inelastic collisionIn an inelastic collision, the final kinetic energy is always less than the initial kinetic energy🟢 Exactc(KE)final must be less than (KE)initial
Concept The defining feature of an inelastic collision (as opposed to elastic) is that some kinetic energy is irreversibly converted into heat, sound and deformation energy during impact. This conversion is a one-way street for ordinary collisions — friction, plastic deformation and sound generation always drain KE, never add to it, so the final KE is pinned below the initial KE, not merely "different from" it.
Formula Momentum is always conserved: Σp_i = Σp_f. Energy bookkeeping: KE_i = KE_f + Q, where Q ≥ 0 is the energy dissipated as heat/sound/deformation (J). Q cannot be negative for a passive (non-explosive) collision — there is no physical mechanism here to inject outside energy into KE.
Solution
1. Option (a) describes an elastic collision (KE strictly conserved) — by definition this is the opposite of "inelastic", so (a) is rejected outright.
2. Option (b), KE increasing, would require an external energy source feeding into the collision (e.g. an explosive separation) — that is not what "collision" ordinarily means in this chapter, and is never classified as inelastic.
3. Every real inelastic collision loses energy to deformation, heat and sound at the point of contact — this is exactly why perfectly inelastic collisions (objects sticking together) show the largest possible KE loss for given masses and speeds.
4. Therefore KE_f is always strictly less than KE_i for any collision correctly labelled "inelastic" — option (d)'s "may be greater or less" over-generalises and admits case (b), which does not belong here.
5. Cross-check with momentum: momentum is conserved regardless of elasticity, so momentum alone never tells you about KE — you must invoke the definition of "inelastic" itself, which is precisely a statement about KE decreasing.
   ELASTIC:            KE_i ══════════════ KE_f      (equal)

   INELASTIC:          KE_i ──────▸ KE_f + Q          (Q > 0, lost as
                                                        heat/sound/deform.)

   PERFECTLY INELASTIC: KE_i ──────▸ KE_f(min) + Q(max)

   In every inelastic case:  KE_f  <  KE_i   always.
⚡ JEE Trick Read "inelastic" as a promise that KE strictly decreases — treat it as interchangeable with "KE is lost" whenever it appears in an option-based question. Trap: option (d) sounds appropriately cautious and "balanced", which is exactly why it tempts students who forget that inelastic collisions are, by the syllabus definition used here, never energy-gaining.
Answer: (c) (KE)final must be less than (KE)initial.
2Two balls at the same temperature collide, which is conserved? (a) Temperature (b) Velocity (c) Kinetic energy (d) Momentum — (Level-1)Universal conservation law in collisionsTwo balls at the same temperature collide inelastically. Which of the following is not conserved?🟢 ExactdMomentum
Concept "Same temperature" is a decoy — it tells you the balls won't spontaneously exchange heat before the collision, but it has no bearing on collision mechanics. The question is really testing which quantity is conserved in every collision, elastic or not, and the syllabus-wide answer to that is always momentum, because no external horizontal force acts during the brief, violent contact.
Formula Σp_before = Σp_after (impulse–momentum theorem: net external impulse ≈ 0 over the short collision time). Contrast: KE_before ≥ KE_after (equality only for elastic collisions). kg·m/s on both sides of the momentum equation — dimensionally, and physically, momentum survives because F_ext·Δt→0 as Δt→0 for an impulsive, brief collision.
Solution
1. Option (a) Temperature: collisions typically raise temperature slightly at the contact zone (some KE → internal/thermal energy) unless perfectly elastic — not generally conserved.
2. Option (b) Velocity: each ball's individual velocity almost always changes during a collision — that is the whole point of colliding. Not conserved.
3. Option (c) Kinetic energy: conserved only in the special case of a perfectly elastic collision; the question does not say the collision is elastic, so KE cannot be assumed conserved in general.
4. Option (d) Momentum: during the collision, the mutual (Newton's third law) forces between the balls are internal to the two-ball system and cancel in pairs; any external force (gravity, air drag) is finite while the collision time is extremely short, so its impulse ≈ 0.This is why momentum conservation is treated as exact for essentially every textbook collision problem, however messy the deformation.
5. Momentum is therefore the one quantity guaranteed conserved regardless of the balls' temperature, elasticity, or material.
     ball A ──▸ ●            ●◂── ball B      (same temperature, T)

   during contact:  F_AB = −F_BA   (Newton's third law, internal pair)
                    F_external·Δt ≈ 0   (Δt tiny)
   ────────────────────────────────────────────
   ⇒ Σp is conserved,  regardless of elastic / inelastic / temperature
⚡ JEE Trick Any "which is conserved in a collision" question with no explicit "elastic" keyword defaults to momentum — it is the one universal invariant. Trap: option (c) tempts students who reflexively associate "collision" with "energy conservation" from earlier mechanics chapters, forgetting KE conservation is a special (elastic-only) case, not a general law.
Answer: (d) Momentum.
3A ball strikes against the floor and returns with double the velocity; in which type of collision is it possible? (a) perfectly elastic (b) Inelastic (c) Perfectly inelastic (d) It is not possible — (Level-1)Energy conservation as an upper bound on rebound speedThe coefficient of restitution e for a perfectly elastic collision is (1) 1 (2) 0 (3) ∞ (4) −1🟡 SimilardIt is not possible
Concept The coefficient of restitution e = (separation speed)/(approach speed) is bounded 0 ≤ e ≤ 1 for any passive, non-explosive collision — e=0 is perfectly inelastic (sticks), e=1 is perfectly elastic (bounces back at the same speed). A ball leaving the floor at double its impact speed would need e=2, which violates energy conservation: the floor is stationary and inert, so it cannot pump extra kinetic energy into the ball.
Formula e = v_rebound / u_impact (dimensionless). Energy check: KE_after ≤ KE_before ⇒ v_rebound ≤ u_impact ⇒ e ≤ 1. Both v_rebound and u_impact are in m/s so e is a pure number; the inequality directly encodes "the floor cannot create energy".
Solution
1. Test (a) perfectly elastic: by definition e=1, so v_rebound = u_impact exactly — same speed, not double. Rejected.
2. Test (b) inelastic (general, 0<e<1): v_rebound = e×u_impact < u_impact — always a decrease, never an increase, let alone doubling. Rejected.
3. Test (c) perfectly inelastic: e=0, the ball would stay on the floor (v_rebound=0) — the opposite extreme of doubling. Rejected.
4. For v_rebound = 2×u_impact, e would have to equal 2, which is outside the physically allowed range [0,1] for a passive collision with an inert floor.e>1 ("superelastic") only occurs when energy is actively injected — e.g. a spring-loaded or explosive mechanism in the floor — never in an ordinary elastic/inelastic bounce.
5. Since none of (a), (b) or (c) can produce a doubled rebound speed, and no ordinary collision type allows e>1, the scenario itself is physically impossible.
   e = v_rebound / u_impact         allowed range:  0 ≤ e ≤ 1

   e=0 (perf. inelastic) ── e=0.6 (typical) ── e=1 (perf. elastic) ── e=2 ✗

⚡ JEE Trick Any option describing a rebound faster than the impact (without stating an active/explosive mechanism) is an automatic "not possible" — check e's upper bound of 1 before doing any other algebra. Trap: students sometimes reach for "perfectly elastic" reflexively as the "best-case" collision, forgetting perfectly elastic only preserves speed, it never amplifies it.
Answer: (d) It is not possible — no passive collision can give e > 1.
4In an inelastic collision, which quantity is not conserved? (a) Momentum (b) Total energy (c) Kinetic energy (d) Mass — (Level-1)Momentum vs. KE conservationIn an inelastic collision- (1) momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not (2) momentum is no...🟢 ExactcKinetic energy
Concept Three of the four options are conserved in every collision, elastic or not — the question is designed to isolate the one exception. Momentum is conserved because internal collision forces cancel in pairs; mass is conserved by simple bookkeeping (nothing physically vanishes); total energy is conserved because energy is never destroyed, only converted between forms (KE → heat + sound + deformation). Kinetic energy alone is singled out because it is only one form of energy, and inelastic collisions by definition convert some of it away.
Formula Momentum: Σp_i = Σp_f. Mass: Σm_i = Σm_f. Total energy: KE_i + (internal energy)_i = KE_f + (internal energy)_f. Kinetic energy alone: KE_i ≠ KE_f for inelastic collisions (KE_f < KE_i). Every equation balances in its own unit — kg·m/s, kg, and J respectively — the mismatch is only in the KE-alone bookkeeping, since KE is not the whole energy story.
Solution
1. Momentum: no external horizontal impulse acts during the brief contact ⇒ Σp is conserved in any collision, elastic or inelastic. Not the answer.
2. Mass: colliding bodies (short of nuclear reactions, irrelevant here) don't lose or gain mass ⇒ conserved. Not the answer.
3. Total energy: the first law of thermodynamics guarantees energy is never destroyed — the "lost" KE simply reappears as heat, sound, and permanent deformation energy, so the grand total (KE + all other forms) is unchanged. Not the answer.
4. Kinetic energy specifically: this is exactly the energy tied up in bulk motion, and it is precisely this form that inelastic collisions convert away into the other forms named in step 3.This is the crucial distinction between "energy" (always conserved) and "kinetic energy" (conserved only when the collision is elastic).
5. Therefore the quantity singled out as NOT conserved in an inelastic collision is kinetic energy, option (c).
   CONSERVED in ANY collision:        NOT conserved in INELASTIC collision:
     • Momentum   Σp                    • Kinetic Energy   KE
     • Mass       Σm                      (converts to heat + sound +
     • Total Energy (KE + heat+sound+deform.)                deformation energy)
⚡ JEE Trick Draw a clean line between "total energy" (always conserved, first law) and "kinetic energy" (conserved only if elastic) every time this style of question appears — examiners frequently plant "total energy" as a distractor precisely because students conflate it with KE. Trap: option (b) is the classic wrong pick for students who reason "energy is never destroyed, so nothing about energy is unconserved here" — true for total energy, false for KE alone.
Answer: (c) Kinetic energy.
5What happens to the kinetic energy in a perfectly inelastic collision? (a) It increases (b) It remains constant (c) It is completely lost (d) It partially converts to other forms — (Level-1)KE loss in a perfectly inelastic collision (not total loss)Derivation → loss in kinetic energy during perfectly inelastic collision🟡 SimilardIt partially converts to other forms
Concept "Perfectly inelastic" means the bodies stick together and move with one common final velocity — it maximises KE loss for the given masses and initial speeds, but it does not mean all the KE vanishes. Whatever momentum the system carried before collision, the combined mass must still carry after collision (momentum is always conserved), so the combined mass is generally still moving, and a moving mass has non-zero KE.
Formula Common velocity: v' = (m₁u₁+m₂u₂)/(m₁+m₂) (m/s). Loss formula: K_i−K_f = m₁m₂(u₁−u₂)²/[2(m₁+m₂)] (J) — the standard result derived on this very lecture's theory page. kg·(m/s)² ÷ kg = kg·m²/s² = J ✓, and the loss formula is manifestly non-negative but is not, in general, equal to the entire K_i.
Solution
1. Option (a) increase: impossible for a passive collision — nothing pumps energy in. Rejected.
2. Option (b) remains constant: contradicts the very definition of "inelastic" (KE is not conserved unless the collision is elastic). Rejected.
3. Option (c) completely lost: this would require v'=0, i.e. the combined mass ends up at rest. But v'=(m₁u₁+m₂u₂)/(m₁+m₂), which is zero only in the special case m₁u₁ = −m₂u₂ (equal and opposite momenta). 5. Average speed = total distance / total time = 47.63/19 ≈ 2.507 m/s.

7. Sanity check the formula structure: as e→1 (perfectly elastic), both D and T diverge to infinity together, and their ratio stays finite — consistent with a ball that would (ideally) bounce forever at a steady average pace.
   h=5m ↓        h·e² ↑↓        h·e⁴ ↑↓ ...  (infinite bounces)

   t₀=1s          2e·t₀           2e²·t₀ ...

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise BOTH closed-form series — D uses (1+e²)/(1−e²), T uses (1+e)/(1−e) — and always derive e from whatever height-ratio or velocity-ratio the question gives BEFORE plugging in. Concept This is a purely 1D-in, 2D-out collision: everything starts moving along one line, but the two split fragments fly off symmetrically at ±45° to that line. Conserve momentum in the ALONG-line (x) direction to find the fragment speed — the perpendicular (y) components of the two fragments are automatically equal and opposite by the given symmetry, so the y-equation is trivially satisfied and adds no new information.
Formula x-momentum conservation: m(2v) + 2m(v) = 0 + 2×[m·u·cos45°] (kg·m/s throughout, u = speed of each fragment). The first mass contributes 0 to the final momentum since it is "stopped completely" — that phrase is doing real work in the problem, removing one term entirely.
Solution
1. Total initial momentum along the line of motion: p_i = m(2v) + (2m)(v) = 2mv + 2mv = 4mv.
2. After collision, the first particle (mass m) is stopped — it contributes zero momentum. The second particle's mass 2m has split into two pieces of mass m each, symmetric about the original direction at ±45°.
3.                      ●───▶  (fragment 2, mass m, mirrored −45°)

Concept For any head-on ELASTIC collision with the target initially at rest, there is a compact ready-made formula for the fraction of KE handed over to the target, purely in terms of the mass ratio — no need to separately solve for both final velocities from scratch every time.
