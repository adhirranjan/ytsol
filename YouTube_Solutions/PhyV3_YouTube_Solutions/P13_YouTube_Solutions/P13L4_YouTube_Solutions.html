1What is the gravitational potential at a point on the axis of a uniform ring of mass M and radius a, at a distance z from the center? (a) GM/(z2+a2) (b) −GM/√(z2−a2) (c) −GM/(z2+a2) (d) −GM/√(z2+a2) — (Level-1)potential on axis of ringGravitation: Lecture 19: Gravitational potential due to uniform ring at a point on its axis🟡 Similard−GM/√(z2+a2)
Concept Potential is a scalar, so the ring is simply chopped into tiny masses dm and their potentials are added with no direction bookkeeping. Every element of the ring sits at the same distance √(z²+a²) from a point on the axis, so the sum collapses to one term. The traps are a positive sign (potential of an attracting mass is always negative) and writing the distance squared without the root.
Formula dV = −G·dm/r,  r = √(z² + a²) Point-mass potential applied to each element; r is fixed for all elements by Pythagoras (radius a, axial distance z).
Solution
1. Take an element dm on the ring. Its distance to the axial point P is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs a and z ⇒ r = √(z² + a²).
2. Potential of the element at P: dV = −G·dm/√(z² + a²).Scalar, so no components cancel; every dm contributes the same sign.
3. r is identical for all elements, so it comes out of the integral: V = −G/√(z²+a²) · ∫dm.
4. ∫dm = M ⇒ V = −GM/√(z² + a²).
5. Check z = 0 (centre): V = −GM/a, finite and negative, as expected since all mass is at distance a.
6. Check z ≫ a: V → −GM/z, the point-mass result. Option (d) passes both limits.
 element dm, distance r = sqrt(z^2 + a^2)  (same for all)
 dV = -G dm / r
 V  = -(G/r) * (sum of dm) = -GM / sqrt(z^2 + a^2)
 z = 0   : V = -GM/a      (finite, ok)
 z >> a  : V = -GM/z      (point mass, ok)
 -----------------------------------------
 V = -GM / sqrt(z^2 + a^2)          <-- answer (d)
⚡ JEE Trick Use dimensions: potential must be (GM)/(length). Options (a) and (c) have GM/length², so they are dimensionally wrong at once. Option (b) has z² − a², which becomes imaginary for z < a (e.g. at the centre), impossible for a real potential. Only (d) survives, without any integration. Do not confuse with the field on the axis, GMz/(z²+a²)^(3/2), which does need components.
Answer: (d) −GM/√(z² + a²).
2Calculate the gravitational potential and gravitational field due to a point mass of 100 kg at a distance of 1m. — (Level-2)potential and field of point mass17 Gravitational potential due to point mass🟡 Similar−6.67×10−9 J kg−1, 6.67×10−9 N kg−1
Concept For a point mass the potential and the field have the same magnitude formula except for one power of r: V = −GM/r and E = GM/r². At r = 1 m the two numbers coincide, but they are different quantities. Potential is a negative scalar (work per kg from infinity); field is a vector pointing towards the mass. The trap is dropping the minus sign on V or writing V in N/kg.
Formula V = −GM/r,  E = GM/r² Point-mass results; E = −dV/dr gives the field magnitude GM/r² directed towards decreasing r.
Solution
1. Data: M = 100 kg, r = 1 m, G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
2. GM = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 100 = 6.67 × 10⁻⁹ N m² kg⁻¹.
3. Potential: V = −GM/r = −(6.67 × 10⁻⁹)/1 = −6.67 × 10⁻⁹ J kg⁻¹.Negative: gravity does positive work bringing 1 kg in from infinity.
4. Field: E = GM/r² = (6.67 × 10⁻⁹)/1² = 6.67 × 10⁻⁹ N kg⁻¹.
5. Direction of E: towards the 100 kg mass (attractive), i.e. along −r̂.
6. Unit check: J kg⁻¹ = N m kg⁻¹ for V; N kg⁻¹ = m s⁻² for E. Equal numbers only because r = 1 m.
7. Consistency: E = −dV/dr = −d(−GM/r)/dr = −GM/r², the minus meaning the field points to smaller r, i.e. towards M; magnitude 6.67 × 10⁻⁹ N kg⁻¹ as found.

 V  = -GM/r  = -6.67e-9 / 1 = -6.67e-9 J/kg
 E  =  GM/r^2 = 6.67e-9 / 1 =  6.67e-9 N/kg

 -----------------------------------------
 V = -6.67e-9 J/kg ; E = 6.67e-9 N/kg   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember E = |V|/r for a point mass: once you have V, divide by r to get E. Here r = 1 m, so the magnitudes match; at r = 2 m E would be half of |V|. Trap: quoting a positive potential, or giving the field a minus sign without saying it means "towards the mass". The field acceleration 6.67 × 10⁻⁹ m s⁻² is tiny, which is why lab masses barely attract.
Answer: V = −6.67 × 10⁻⁹ J kg⁻¹; E = 6.67 × 10⁻⁹ N kg⁻¹ directed towards the mass.
3Three point masses, each of mass ‘m’, are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side l. What is the gravitational field Ē (i.e., gravitational force on a unit mass) and potential due to the three masses at the centroid of the triangle? — (Level-3)field and potential at centroidThree mass points each of mass m are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side l.🟢 ExactĒ = 0; V = −3√3 Gm/l
Concept At the centroid the three masses are equidistant, so the three field vectors have equal magnitude and point 120° apart: they cancel by symmetry. Potential, being a scalar, does not cancel; the three equal negative contributions simply add. The trap is to assume zero field means zero potential, or to use the side l instead of the centroid distance l/√3.
Formula r = l/√3,  Ē = Σ Gm/r² (vectors),  V = Σ(−Gm/r) Centroid lies 2/3 of the median from each vertex; median = (√3/2)l, so r = (2/3)(√3/2)l = l/√3.
Solution
1. Distance from each vertex to the centroid: r = (2/3)·(√3/2)l = l/√3.
2. Field magnitude from each mass: E₁ = Gm/r² = Gm/(l²/3) = 3Gm/l², directed towards that vertex.
3. Three equal vectors at 120° to one another: E₁ + E₁cos120° + E₁cos240° = E₁(1 − 1/2 − 1/2) = 0 along any axis.Equal vectors spaced 120° form a closed triangle.
4. Hence net field at the centroid Ē = 0.
5. Potential from each mass: −Gm/r = −Gm/(l/√3) = −√3 Gm/l.
6. Scalars add: V = 3 × (−√3 Gm/l) = −3√3 Gm/l ≈ −5.2 Gm/l.
7. Meaning: to remove 1 kg from the centroid to infinity an external agent must supply +3√3 Gm/l of work, even though no net force acts on it at the start.
 median = (sqrt3/2) l ; r = (2/3) median = l/sqrt3
 each |E| = Gm/r^2 = 3Gm/l^2 , 120 deg apart
 E_net = 3Gm/l^2 (1 - 1/2 - 1/2)       = 0
 each V = -Gm/r = -sqrt3 Gm/l
 V_net = 3(-sqrt3 Gm/l)                = -3 sqrt3 Gm/l
 -----------------------------------------
 E = 0 ; V = -3 sqrt3 Gm/l             <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Symmetry kills vectors, never scalars: n equal masses on a regular polygon always give zero field at the centre but potential −nGm/r. A point of zero field is an equilibrium point, not a point of zero potential. Trap: using r = l/2 or r = l; the centroid-to-vertex distance l/√3 is the one to memorise.
Answer: Ē = 0 at the centroid; V = −3√3 Gm/l.

5Two heavy spheres, each of mass 100 kg and radius 0.10m, are placed 1.0 m apart on a horizontal table. What is the gravitational field Ē and potential at the mid-point of the line joining the centres of the spheres? Is an object placed at that point in equilibrium? If so, is the equilibrium stable or unstable? — (Level-5)midpoint field, potential, equilibriumTwo heavy spheres each of mass 100 kg and radius 0.10 m are placed 1.0 m apart ona horizontal table.🟢 ExactĒ = 0, V = −2.7×10−8 J kg−1; yes, unstable
Concept Each sphere acts as a point mass at its centre (the midpoint, 0.5 m away, is outside both spheres of radius 0.1 m). At the midpoint the two equal fields point in opposite directions and cancel, but the two negative potentials add. Zero net force means equilibrium; whether it is stable depends on what happens after a small displacement along the line. Trap: calling it stable because the force is zero.
Formula E = GM/r²,  V = −GM/r with r = 0.5 m for each sphere. Shell theorem: outside a uniform sphere, field and potential are those of a point mass at its centre.
Solution
1. Distance from each centre to the midpoint: r = 1.0/2 = 0.5 m (> 0.10 m, so the point is outside both).
2. Field of each: E₁ = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 100/0.25 = 2.67 × 10⁻⁸ N kg⁻¹, pointing towards its own sphere.
3. The two fields are equal and opposite ⇒ Ē_net = 0.
4. Potential: V = −2GM/r = −2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 100/0.5 = −2.67 × 10⁻⁸ J kg⁻¹ ≈ −2.7 × 10⁻⁸ J kg⁻¹.
5. Net force on any mass placed there is zero ⇒ it is in equilibrium.
6. Shift it by x towards sphere A: distance to A becomes 0.5 − x, to B 0.5 + x, so GM/(0.5−x)² > GM/(0.5+x)²; the net pull is towards A, i.e. in the direction of the displacement.Force grows away from equilibrium ⇒ unstable.
7. Hence the equilibrium is unstable (the potential is a maximum along the line joining the centres).
 r = 0.5 m from each centre
 E1 = GM/r^2 = 6.67e-11*100/0.25 = 2.67e-8 N/kg (each)
 E_net = E1 - E1                 = 0
 V = -2GM/r = -2*6.67e-11*100/0.5 = -2.67e-8 J/kg
 shift x to A: pull to A > pull to B  -> moves on

⚡ JEE Trick Stability test in one line: along the joining line V = −GM[1/(0.5−x) + 1/(0.5+x)] is most negative at the ends and least negative at x = 0, so x = 0 is a potential maximum ⇒ unstable. Trap: zero force does not mean stable. Strictly, sideways displacements are restoring (a saddle point), but along the line, the standard sense here, it is unstable.
Answer: Ē = 0; V = −2.7 × 10⁻⁸ J kg⁻¹; yes, the object is in equilibrium, but it is unstable.

2In some region, the gravitational field is zero. The gravitational potential in this region. (a) Must be variable (b) Must be constant (c) cannot be zero (d) Must be zero — (Level-1)zero field implies constant potentialIn some region, the gravitational field is zero. The gravitational potential in this region. (a) ...🟢 ExactbMust be constant
Concept The field is minus the gradient of the potential, Ē = −∇V. If the field is zero throughout a region, the potential cannot change in any direction there, so it is constant. It need not be zero: the constant value depends on the masses outside. The classic example is the inside of a spherical shell, where E = 0 but V = −GM/R everywhere.
Formula E = −dV/dr ⇒ dV = −E·dr = 0 ⇒ V = constant In 3D, each component E_x = −∂V/∂x etc.; all zero means V has no variation at all in the region.
Solution
1. Relation between field and potential: Ē = −∇V, i.e. E_x = −∂V/∂x, E_y = −∂V/∂y, E_z = −∂V/∂z.
2. Given Ē = 0 in the region ⇒ every partial derivative of V is zero there.
3. A function with zero derivative in every direction over a connected region is constant: V(B) − V(A) = −∫Ē·dr̄ = 0 for any A, B in it.
4. So (a) "must be variable" is false.
5. The constant need not be zero: inside a shell of mass M, radius R, E = 0 but V = −GM/R ≠ 0 ⇒ (c) "cannot be zero" and (d) "must be zero" are both unjustified.V depends on the zero reference at infinity and on the mass around the region.
6. Hence the potential must be constant: option (b).
 E = -dV/dr
 E = 0 everywhere in region  =>  dV/dr = 0
 V(B) - V(A) = -(integral of E.dr) = 0
 example: inside shell  E = 0 , V = -GM/R (not 0)
 -----------------------------------------
 V = constant (any value)          <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Zero field ⇔ flat potential, not zero potential. Equally, a nonzero constant is perfectly allowed; and the converse also holds: zero potential at a point (e.g. midway between +q and −q in electrostatics) does not mean zero field. Trap: picking (d) by thinking "no field, so no potential". The shell interior (E = 0, V = −GM/R) disproves it in one line.
Answer: (b) Must be constant.

4Gravitational potential is a ________ quantity. (a) Vector (b) Scalar (c) Tensor (d) Complex — (Level-1)nature of gravitational potential11 chap 8 | Gravitation 05 | Gravitation Potential : Introduction with Derivation IIT JEE MAINS/NEET🟡 SimilarbScalar
Concept Gravitational potential is the work done per unit mass in bringing a test mass from infinity to the point, V = W/m. Work is a dot product F̄·dr̄, a scalar, and dividing a scalar by a scalar mass leaves a scalar. It has a sign (negative for gravity) but no direction. Trap: the negative sign is mistaken for a direction, or potential is confused with the field, which is a vector.
Formula V = W/m = −∫ Ē·dr̄ A dot product of two vectors is a scalar; contributions from several masses add algebraically: V = Σ(−Gmᵢ/rᵢ).
Solution
1. Definition: V at P = work done per unit mass by an external agent bringing a test mass slowly from infinity to P.
2. Work W = ∫F̄·dr̄ is a dot product ⇒ scalar.
3. V = W/m: scalar ÷ scalar = scalar.So V has magnitude and sign only.
4. Superposition confirms it: for several masses V = −Gm₁/r₁ − Gm₂/r₂ − … simply added as numbers, no vector addition.
5. Contrast: field Ē = −∇V is a vector; the gradient of a scalar gives a vector.
6. Tensor (c) and complex (d) are irrelevant here: no direction-pair dependence, and V is always real. ⇒ (b).
