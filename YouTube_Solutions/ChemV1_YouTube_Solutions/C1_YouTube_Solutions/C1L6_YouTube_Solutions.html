1Which postulate of kinetic theory explains why a gas has negligible volume compared to its container?KMT postulatesA-KTG🟡 SimilarbThe actual volume of the molecules is negligible compared to the total volume.
Concept The kinetic molecular theory pictures a gas as a huge number of tiny particles darting about inside a container that is, by comparison, almost entirely empty space. A gas fills any vessel and is easily compressed precisely because the molecules themselves occupy a negligible fraction of the total volume — the "volume of the gas" we quote is really the volume of the container, not of the matter inside it. This is the specific postulate the question is testing.
Key relation Vmolecules ≪ Vcontainer, so Vgas ≈ Vcontainer. At ordinary pressure the actual bulk of the molecules is around 0.1% of the space they roam in, which is why an ideal gas is treated as point masses with zero proper volume.
Solution
1. List the relevant KMT postulates: (i) molecules are point-like with negligible own volume; (ii) they move in constant random motion; (iii) collisions are perfectly elastic; (iv) no intermolecular forces.Only one of these speaks to volume.
2. Match the question — "why is the gas volume negligible compared to the container" — to postulate (i).It is the size of the molecules relative to the vessel, not their motion or collisions, that is being asked about.
3. Therefore the correct statement is that the actual volume of the molecules is negligible compared to the total volume — option (b).
4. Eliminate distractors: statements about random motion or elastic collisions are true postulates but explain pressure/energy, not the volume approximation.
⚡ JEE Trick Tie each KMT postulate to the property it explains: negligible molecular volume → compressibility & Vgas≈Vcontainer; random motion → filling any shape; elastic collisions → constant energy; no forces → ideal behaviour. Trap: options describing motion or collisions are genuine postulates, but only the "negligible own volume" one answers a volume question.
Answer: (b) The actual volume of the molecules is negligible compared to the total volume.

Per molecule the energy is 3/2 kT, where k is the Boltzmann constant (the gas constant scaled down to a single particle, k = R/NA). The classic trap is mixing up the per-molecule constant k with the per-mole constant R — they differ by a factor of Avogadro's number, i.e. about 10²³.

3. Multiply the plain numbers first: 1.5 × 1.38 = 2.07, then 2.07 × 300 = 621.
4. Attach the power of ten: E = 621 × 10⁻²³ J = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.

3If T is doubled at constant volume, how does pressure change (kinetic theory)?P ∝ T from kinetic eqnA-KGE🟡 SimilarbPressure doubles
Concept Kinetic theory says pressure comes from molecules hammering the walls, and the average kinetic energy of those molecules is set entirely by absolute temperature. Raise T and the molecules move faster and hit harder and more often, so pressure rises in lock-step. When the volume (and amount of gas) is held fixed, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature — this is Gay-Lussac's law, which drops straight out of the ideal-gas / kinetic equation.
Formula From PV = nRT at fixed V and n, P ∝ T, so P₂ ÷ P₁ = T₂ ÷ T₁ (T in kelvin). With V and n constant, everything except P and T is a fixed constant, leaving a simple proportion.
Solution
1. Fix what is constant: volume V and moles n do not change; only T changes.
2. Write the proportion from PV = nRT: P₂/P₁ = T₂/T₁.nR/V is a constant, so P and T rise together.
3. Substitute T₂ = 2T₁: P₂/P₁ = 2T₁/T₁ = 2.
4. Therefore P₂ = 2P₁ — the pressure doubles, option (b).Doubling absolute temperature doubles the molecular kinetic energy and hence the wall force.
⚡ JEE Trick At constant V, P/T = constant — read pressure and temperature ratios off directly. Trap: temperature MUST be in kelvin; doubling from 27 °C is not 27→54 °C but 300→600 K. Here "doubled" is already absolute, so the answer is a clean ×2.
Answer: (b) Pressure doubles.
4Two gases A & B, same T & P, different molar mass — correct RMS-speed statement?urms ∝ 1/√MA-URMS🟡 SimilarbGas A with smaller molecular mass has greater RMS speed.
Concept At a given temperature every gas has the same average translational kinetic energy per molecule — that energy depends only on T, not on the identity of the gas. Since kinetic energy is ½mu², a heavier molecule reaches that fixed energy at a lower speed, while a lighter molecule must move faster. So at equal T (and pressure is irrelevant to the speed), the lighter gas always has the greater RMS speed.
Formula urms = √(3RT ÷ M), so at fixed T, urms ∝ 1 ÷ √M and uA ÷ uB = √(MB ÷ MA). Here R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹K⁻¹, T in K, M in kg mol⁻¹. M sits under the square root in the denominator, so bigger M ⇒ smaller speed.
Solution
1. Both gases are at the same T, so both have equal average KE = 3/2 kT per molecule.KE per molecule is a function of temperature alone.
2. Write urms = √(3RT/M); with T fixed, only M varies between A and B.
3. Because M is in the denominator under the root, the gas with the smaller M has the larger urms.
4. Given A has the smaller molar mass, A has the greater RMS speed — option (b). Pressure being equal has no effect on urms.
⚡ JEE Trick "Same temperature ⇒ lighter gas is faster" — H₂ outruns O₂ every time. Speed ratio = √(Mheavy/Mlight), nothing to do with pressure. Trap: options that make speed depend on pressure are wrong — urms depends only on T and M.
Answer: (b) Gas A with smaller molecular mass has greater RMS speed.
5urms=240 m/s at 150 K; heated (const V) to 600 K — new urms?urms ∝ √TA-URMS🟡 Similara480 m/s
Concept RMS speed grows with temperature, but only as its square root — so quadrupling the absolute temperature only doubles the speed. Because the gas is the same throughout, its molar mass M cancels and we never need its value; only the ratio of the two temperatures matters. This "ratio method" is the fastest route whenever one speed and both temperatures are known.

Solution
1. Note the data: u₁ = 240 m/s at T₁ = 150 K; T₂ = 600 K; find u₂.
2. Form the temperature ratio: T₂/T₁ = 600 ÷ 150 = 4.Both already in kelvin, so divide directly.
3. Take the square root: √4 = 2.Speed scales as √T, not T, so a 4× temperature gives only a 2× speed.
4. Multiply: u₂ = 240 × 2 = 480 m/s — option (a).

Concept The kinetic molecular theory rests on a short list of idealising assumptions about gas particles. A correct-assumption question is really a test of which statement belongs to that list versus which is a common misconception. The genuine postulates describe molecules as tiny, always moving, colliding elastically, and free of attractive forces — with continuous random motion being the central one that gives a gas its ability to fill any container.
Key relation KMT postulates: (i) negligible molecular volume, (ii) continuous random motion, (iii) perfectly elastic collisions, (iv) no intermolecular forces, (v) KEavg ∝ T. "Random motion" means no preferred direction, which is why gas pressure is the same on every wall.

2. Test each option against them; the statement "gas molecules are in continuous random motion" is postulate (ii) — a true assumption.
3. Reject typical wrong options: molecules attract strongly (false — forces are negligible), collisions lose energy (false — they are elastic), or molecules occupy most of the volume (false — their volume is negligible).
4. Hence the correct assumption is option (d): continuous random motion.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the five postulates as a set; any option that says molecules attract, lose energy in collisions, or have significant size is automatically a distractor. Trap: statements that are true for real gases (weak attractions exist) are false as ideal-KMT assumptions.

2According to kinetic theory, what causes the pressure exerted by a gas?Cause of pressureA-KTG🟡 SimilarcBombardment of gas molecules on container walls
Concept In kinetic theory a gas exerts pressure because its molecules are constantly flying about and striking the walls of the container. Each collision delivers a tiny push (a change of momentum); summed over the enormous number of impacts per second and spread over the wall area, these pushes register as a steady, measurable pressure. Pressure is therefore force per unit area arising purely from molecular bombardment — not from attraction, repulsion, or weight.
Key relation P = force ÷ area = (rate of momentum transfer to wall) ÷ area, formalised by P = ⅓ (N/V) m u²rms. Each wall hit reverses a molecule's momentum by 2mu; many such hits per second give a continuous average force.
Solution
1. Identify the microscopic origin of pressure: molecules colliding with and rebounding off the walls.
2. Each impact transfers momentum to the wall; the collective effect over billions of impacts is a constant average force.
3. Force ÷ wall area = pressure, so pressure = wall bombardment by molecules — option (c).
4. Reject alternatives: intermolecular attraction/repulsion or the gas's own weight are not the KMT cause of pressure.
⚡ JEE Trick Pressure = molecules hitting walls. More impacts (higher density or speed) or harder impacts (higher T) ⇒ more pressure, exactly what P = ⅓(N/V)mu² encodes. Trap: options invoking molecular attractions describe real-gas deviations, not the origin of pressure.
Answer: (c) Bombardment of gas molecules on container walls.
3Which gas law: volume ↑ at constant T → pressure ↓?Boyle's lawA-BOYLE🟡 SimilarbBoyle's Law
Concept Each gas law fixes two variables and relates the other two. The relationship described here — constant temperature, with volume and pressure moving in opposite directions — is the definition of Boyle's law. Kinetic theory explains it: spread the same molecules over a bigger volume and they strike the walls less often, so pressure falls.
Key relation Boyle: PV = constant (T, n fixed) ⇒ P ∝ 1/V. Contrast Charles: V ∝ T (P fixed) and Gay-Lussac: P ∝ T (V fixed). "Constant T + inverse P–V" is the signature that singles out Boyle's law.
Solution
1. Note the fixed variable: temperature is constant.This immediately rules out Charles' and Gay-Lussac's laws, which need constant P or V.
2. Note the relationship: V up ⇒ P down — an inverse proportionality.
3. Constant T with P ∝ 1/V is exactly Boyle's law — option (b).
4. Microscopic reason: larger V ⇒ fewer wall collisions per second ⇒ lower pressure.
⚡ JEE Trick Match the law by "what's held constant": T fixed → Boyle, P fixed → Charles, V fixed → Gay-Lussac, n & conditions → Avogadro. Trap: Charles' law also involves volume, but it needs constant pressure and gives a direct V–T link, not an inverse P–V one.
Answer: (b) Boyle's Law.

8Free expansion (A) vs reversible adiabatic (B) — correct statement (kinetic theory)KMT / ideal-gas behaviourA-KTG🟡 SimilaraGas A shows no cooling because intermolecular attractions are negligible.
Concept This contrasts two expansions. In free expansion (A) the gas expands into a vacuum, doing no work against an external pressure, and no heat is exchanged — so for an ideal gas (no intermolecular forces) the internal energy and temperature are unchanged: no cooling. In a reversible adiabatic expansion (B) the gas does work on its surroundings with no heat input, so its internal energy — and temperature — must fall. The correct statement is the one describing A's absence of cooling.
Key relation free expansion: w = 0, q = 0 ⇒ ΔU = 0 ⇒ ΔT = 0 (ideal gas); adiabatic: q = 0, w > 0 ⇒ ΔU < 0 ⇒ T falls. An ideal gas has no potential energy of attraction, so pulling molecules apart in free expansion costs no energy and T stays put.
Solution
1. Free expansion (A): expands into vacuum ⇒ external work w = 0; adiabatic ⇒ q = 0; so ΔU = q + w = 0.
2. For an ideal gas U depends only on T, so ΔU = 0 ⇒ ΔT = 0 ⇒ no cooling, because there are no intermolecular attractions to overcome.
3. Reversible adiabatic (B): q = 0 but the gas does positive work (w < 0 on the gas) ⇒ ΔU < 0 ⇒ temperature falls.
4. The statement matching this analysis is option (a): Gas A shows no cooling because intermolecular attractions are negligible.
⚡ JEE Trick Ideal-gas free expansion ⇒ ΔT = 0 (no work, no heat, no attractions); real gases cool slightly (Joule–Thomson) because attractions exist. Adiabatic expansion against a pressure always cools. Trap: don't assume every expansion cools a gas — free expansion of an ideal gas does not.
Answer: (a) Gas A shows no cooling because intermolecular attractions are negligible.
