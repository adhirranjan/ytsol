1Velocity is defined as: rate of change of displacement with respect to time (Level-1)Derivatives as definitionsClass 11 basic maths for physics — differentiation · Differentiation for physics — one shot🟡 SimilarbRate of change of displacement with respect to time
Concept This question fixes the defining equation of velocity. In kinematics every quantity is built as a rate of change — a derivative of one physical quantity with respect to another. Velocity answers "how fast is position changing?", so the quantity being changed is displacement x (a vector, in m) and the quantity it is changing against is time t (in s). Average velocity uses a finite interval Δx/Δt; instantaneous velocity is the limit of that ratio as the interval shrinks to zero, which is exactly the derivative dx/dt.
Formula v = dx/dt = limΔt→0 (Δx / Δt), where x = displacement in m, t = time in s, so v carries units m s⁻¹. Dividing metres by seconds gives m s⁻¹, which is the SI unit of velocity — the dimensions [L T⁻¹] confirm the definition is the right one. Contrast the neighbours: a = dv/dt (m s⁻²) is rate of change of velocity, not displacement.
Solution
1. Identify what is changing: displacement x (unit m), and what it changes with respect to: time t (unit s).Velocity is by definition a statement about how position evolves in time.
2. Write the average rate over a finite interval: vavg = Δx / Δt, e.g. Δx = 10 m in Δt = 5 s gives 10 m ÷ 5 s = 2 m s⁻¹.A ratio of a change in x to the change in t is exactly what "rate of change" means.
3. Shrink the interval to get the value at one instant: v = limΔt→0 Δx/Δt = dx/dt.The limit of a difference quotient is the definition of the derivative, so instantaneous velocity IS the derivative of x with respect to t.
4. Check dimensions: [x]/[t] = L / T = [L T⁻¹] = m s⁻¹ ✓ — the accepted unit of velocity.A definition that failed the dimension check would be wrong on sight.
5. Therefore velocity = rate of change of displacement with respect to time, v = dx/dt.

Fastest MCQ filter: check the units. If an option gives dx/dt it must come out m s⁻¹; anything offering dv/dt (m s⁻²) or dx/dv is instantly wrong. Trap: speed uses distance (a scalar path length) while velocity uses displacement (a vector) — the derivative form is the same but velocity keeps direction, which is why a body on a closed loop can have zero average velocity yet non-zero average speed.

2Velocity v is a function of displacement x, v = 3√(x+5). Find dv/dx (Level-2)Chain rule / √ functionDerivative of a square-root function (chain rule) · Chain rule with a square root🟡 Similar3/(2√(x+5))
Concept Here velocity is given as a function of position x, not of time, and we are asked for the velocity gradient dv/dx — how rapidly v changes per metre travelled. The function 3√(x+5) is a composite: an inner function u = x + 5 sits inside an outer square-root. Composites must be differentiated with the chain rule — differentiate the outer function keeping the inner intact, then multiply by the derivative of the inner. Forgetting that second factor is the single most common error here.
Formula Chain rule: d/dx [f(u)] = f′(u) · du/dx. Power rule: d/dx (un) = n·un−1·du/dx. Write the root as a power: v = 3(x+5)1/2, so n = 1/2 and u = x + 5. Units: v is in m s⁻¹ and x in m, so dv/dx has units m s⁻¹ / m = s⁻¹ (per second) — a velocity gradient.
Solution
1. Rewrite the surd as a fractional power: v = 3√(x+5) = 3(x+5)1/2.The power rule only applies to expressions written as (something)ⁿ, so convert the root first.
2. Identify inner and outer: inner u = x + 5, outer = 3u1/2.
3. Differentiate the outer with the power rule, holding u fixed: d/du (3u1/2) = 3 × (1/2) × u1/2 − 1 = (3/2) u−1/2.Bring the exponent 1/2 down as a multiplier and reduce the exponent by 1: 1/2 − 1 = −1/2.
4. Differentiate the inner: du/dx = d/dx (x + 5) = 1 + 0 = 1.The derivative of x is 1 and the derivative of the constant 5 is 0.
5. Multiply the two by the chain rule: dv/dx = (3/2)(x+5)−1/2 × 1 = (3/2)(x+5)−1/2.
6. Return to root form: dv/dx = 3 / (2√(x+5)), units s⁻¹.A negative exponent means the factor moves to the denominator: (x+5)⁻¹ᐟ² = 1/√(x+5).
7. Dimension check: [v]/[x] = (m s⁻¹)/(m) = s⁻¹, and the answer 3/(2√(x+5)) is indeed a per-second quantity ✓.

3Potential energy U = ax² + bx (a, b constants). Find force F = −dU/dx (Level-3)Force from potential energyU(x) and force F(x) = −dU/dx · Force from the potential-energy function🟡 Similar−2ax − b
Concept A conservative force is the negative slope of the potential-energy curve: the force always points "downhill" on the U–x graph, pushing the body toward lower potential energy. That is the physical content of F = −dU/dx. So this problem is a pure power-rule differentiation followed by a sign flip. The minus sign is not decoration — it encodes the fact that a ball rolls down a hill, i.e. toward decreasing U.
Formula F = −dU/dx, with U = potential energy in J and x = position in m, giving F in J m⁻¹ = N ✓. Power rule: d/dx(xn) = n xn−1, and constants a, b pass straight through the derivative. For U = ax² + bx to be dimensionally sound, a must carry units J m⁻² and b units J m⁻¹ — check this at the end.
Solution
1. Write the given potential energy: U = a x² + b x, with a and b constants (they do not depend on x).Constants are just multipliers and survive differentiation untouched.
2. Differentiate term by term. First term: d/dx (a x²) = a × 2x2−1 = 2ax.Power rule with n = 2: bring the 2 down, drop the exponent to 1.
3. Second term: d/dx (b x) = b × 1 × x1−1 = b × x⁰ = b.Power rule with n = 1 gives b·x⁰ = b·1 = b — the derivative of a straight line is its constant slope.
4. Add them: dU/dx = 2ax + b (units J m⁻¹ = N).
5. Apply the definition with its minus sign: F = −dU/dx = −(2ax + b) = −2ax − b.The force points toward decreasing potential energy, which is what the negative sign enforces.
6. Unit check: [a] = J m⁻², so 2ax → J m⁻² × m = J m⁻¹ = N ✓; [b] = J m⁻¹ = N ✓. Both terms are forces, so the sum is a force.
 U(x)  = a x²    +  b x            [J]

⚡ JEE Trick F = −dU/dx — the minus sign is worth marks on its own; roughly half of all errors on this question are a dropped negative. Two free extras the examiner loves: (i) set F = 0 to locate equilibrium, here x = −b/2a; (ii) test its stability with the second derivative d²U/dx² = 2a — if a > 0 it is positive, so U is a minimum and the equilibrium is stable (this U is just a shifted parabola, i.e. an SHM-type well). Trap: a and b are constants, not functions of x — never apply the product rule to "ax²".
Answer: F = −dU/dx = −(2ax + b) = −2ax − b newton.

Concept The slope of a curve at a point is the value of its derivative evaluated at that point — the gradient of the tangent line touching the curve there. So "find the slope of the T–t curve at (1, 1)" is a two-step instruction: differentiate to get the slope function dT/dt, then substitute the given t-coordinate. Formula slope = dT/dt evaluated at t = t₀. Power rule: d/dt(tn) = n tn−1; derivative of a constant = 0. Units: T is a temperature (K or °C) and t a time (s), so the slope dT/dt is a rate of heating with units K s⁻¹. Solution
1. Confirm the point lies on the curve: at t = 1, T = 1⁴ − 2(1)² + 2 = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1 ✓ — so (1, 1) is genuinely on the T–t curve.If the point were not on the curve the question would be ill-posed, so this is a worthwhile 5-second check.

Concept Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes — not how quickly position changes. It is therefore the derivative of v with respect to t. Because velocity is itself dx/dt, acceleration is the second derivative of displacement, a = d²x/dt². Recognising which quantity sits in the numerator is the whole question: put x there and you have velocity, put v there and you have acceleration.
Formula a = dv/dt = d²x/dt², with v in m s⁻¹, x in m, t in s, so a is in m s⁻² , dimensions [L T⁻²]. (m s⁻¹) ÷ (s) = m s⁻², which is precisely the accepted unit of acceleration — the units confirm the numerator must be velocity, not displacement. A third, equally valid form for motion given in terms of position is a = v·dv/dx.
Solution
1. State the definition in words: acceleration = rate of change of velocity with respect to time.The quantity named first (velocity) goes in the numerator of the derivative.
2. Translate to symbols: numerator = dv, denominator = dt ⇒ a = dv/dt.
3. Substitute v = dx/dt to get the alternative form: a = d/dt (dx/dt) = d²x/dt² — the second derivative of displacement.Differentiating position once gives velocity; differentiating again gives acceleration.
4. Verify by units: [a] = [v]/[t] = (m s⁻¹)/(s) = m s⁻² ✓, dimensions [L T⁻²].If you had written a = dx/dt the units would be m s⁻¹ — a velocity — so that option fails immediately.
5. Numerical sanity check: velocity rising from 0 to 10 m s⁻¹ in 5 s gives a = Δv/Δt = (10 m s⁻¹)/(5 s) = 2 m s⁻² ✓ — consistent with a = dv/dt.

2Which physical quantity is the rate of change of momentum with time? (Force)F = dp/dtMomentum and force (F = dp/dt) · Force–momentum equation🟡 SimilaraForce
Concept This is Newton's second law in its original and most general form: the net external force on a body equals the time rate of change of its linear momentum. Newton did not write F = ma; he wrote F = dp/dt, which is the deeper statement because it still holds when the mass itself changes (rockets burning fuel, a conveyor belt being loaded, a raindrop growing). F = ma is only the special case for constant mass.
Formula F = dp/dt, where p = mv = momentum in kg m s⁻¹ and t is in s, so F comes out in kg m s⁻² = N (newton) ✓. Expanding with the product rule: F = d(mv)/dt = m·(dv/dt) + v·(dm/dt) = ma + v(dm/dt). If the mass is constant then dm/dt = 0 and the second term vanishes, collapsing the law to the familiar F = ma.
Solution
1. Write the momentum of the body: p = m v, units kg × m s⁻¹ = kg m s⁻¹.
2. Take its time derivative, which is what "rate of change of momentum with time" means: dp/dt = d(mv)/dt.
3. Apply the product rule (m and v may both vary): dp/dt = m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt) = ma + v(dm/dt).The product rule is needed because momentum is a product of two possibly time-varying quantities.
4. For the usual constant-mass case dm/dt = 0, so dp/dt = ma — which is exactly force.This recovers F = ma, confirming that dp/dt is a force and nothing else.
5. Confirm with units: [p]/[t] = (kg m s⁻¹)/(s) = kg m s⁻² = N ✓ — a force.
6. Numerical check: momentum changing by 15 kg m s⁻¹ in 3 s gives F = 15/3 = 5 N ✓.
7. Hence the quantity that is the rate of change of momentum with time is force.

Concept Electric current is the rate at which charge flows past a point in a circuit — literally "how many coulombs per second are passing through this cross-section?" It follows the same rate-of-change template as every other physical definition: the quantity being transported is charge q and the derivative is taken with respect to time t. The instantaneous current is dq/dt; the average current over an interval is Δq/Δt.
Formula I = dq/dt, with q = charge in C (coulomb) and t in s, so I is in C s⁻¹ = A (ampere) ✓. One ampere is by definition one coulomb of charge passing a point per second, so the units are not a coincidence — they restate the definition. The inverse relation is q = ∫ I dt, i.e. charge is the area under an I–t graph.
Solution
1. Identify the transported quantity: charge q (unit C), and the variable it changes against: time t (unit s).Current is a statement about charge flow per unit time.
2. Write the average current over a finite interval: Iavg = Δq/Δt — e.g. 6 C passing in 2 s gives 6 C ÷ 2 s = 3 A.
3. Shrink the interval to reach one instant: I = limΔt→0 Δq/Δt = dq/dt.The limit of the difference quotient is by definition the derivative.
4. Check units: [q]/[t] = C/s = A ✓, which is exactly the SI definition of the ampere.
5. Reverse the operation as a cross-check: integrating gives q = ∫I dt, so a steady 3 A for 2 s delivers q = 3 A × 2 s = 6 C ✓ — consistent with step 2.

Concept Power is the rate at which work is done, or equivalently the rate at which energy is transferred. Since work W is measured in joules and time in seconds, dW/dt is measured in joules per second — the definition of the watt. The distinction being tested is between the total work done (an amount of energy, in J) and how fast that work is delivered (a rate, in W): two engines can do identical work while differing wildly in power.
Formula P = dW/dt, with W = work in J and t in s, so P is in J s⁻¹ = W (watt) ✓. Dividing energy by time gives a rate of energy transfer, which is exactly what power means. Assumption: r is measured in radians throughout — at r = 90° we substitute r = π/2 ≈ 1.5708 rad in both the trig terms and the (1 − r) bracket. Note r ≠ 1 rad, where the denominator vanishes.
Solution
1. Split the quotient: u = sin r, v = 1 − r.
2. Differentiate each: u′ = d(sin r)/dr = cos r; v′ = d(1 − r)/dr = −1.The derivative of the constant 1 is 0, and the derivative of −r is −1.
3. Apply the quotient rule: dF/dr = [(cos r)(1 − r) − (sin r)(−1)] / (1 − r)².
4. Simplify the double negative: −(sin r)(−1) = + sin r.This sign flip is easy to lose and changes the whole answer.
5. So dF/dr = [(1 − r) cos r + sin r] / (1 − r)² N rad⁻¹.
6. Convert the evaluation point to radians: r = 90° = π/2 rad ≈ 1.5708 rad.The trig derivative rules are only valid in radian measure, so this conversion is mandatory.
7. Evaluate the trig values at r = π/2: cos(π/2) = 0, sin(π/2) = 1.
8. Numerator: (1 − π/2)(0) + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1.The cosine term vanishes entirely, which is exactly why 90° was chosen.
9. Denominator: (1 − π/2)² = (1 − 1.5708)² = (−0.5708)² = 0.3258.Squaring the negative makes it positive — 0.5708² = 0.3258.
10. Divide: dF/dr = 1 ÷ 0.3258 = ≈ 3.07 N rad⁻¹.
11. Sign check: positive ✓ — the numerator is +1 and a squared denominator is always positive.

⚡ JEE Trick The instant a calculus problem quotes an angle in degrees, convert to radians — d(sin r)/dr = cos r is false in degrees (you would need an extra π/180). The lethal trap here is the bare r in (1 − r): it is the same variable as the angle, so it must be 1.5708, not 90 (using 1 − 90 = −89 gives a wildly wrong 0.000126).
