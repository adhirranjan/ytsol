1Which of the following correctly defines dipole moment of a molecule? (Level-1)
(a) The distance between two atoms in a molecule   (b) The product of magnitude of charge and the distance between centres of positive and negative charges   (c) The energy required to break a polar bond   (d) The difference between electronegativities of two atoms		Definition of μDipole moment defined — μ = q × d, Class 11 chemical bonding🟢 ExactbThe product of magnitude of charge and the distance between centres of positive and negative charges
Concept A polar molecule carries two equal but opposite partial charges separated in space — an electric dipole. To measure "how polar" it is you need both pieces of information: how much charge got separated, and how far apart the two centres of charge sit. Dipole moment is the single quantity that packages both, and it is defined exactly as their product. Notice why the other three candidate definitions fail on dimensions alone: a distance is metres, a bond energy is joules, and an electronegativity difference is a pure number — none of them is charge × length.
Formula μ = q × d q = magnitude of the negative (or positive) charge, d = distance between the centres of positive and negative charge. Both charges have the same magnitude because the molecule as a whole is electrically neutral, so one q suffices. Because μ has a direction (from the +ve to the −ve end, drawn as + ⟼ −), it is a vector.
Solution
1. Write the defining relation from the theory: μ = q × d.
2. Check the units it produces: q ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ esu and d ≈ 10⁻⁸ cm ⇒ μ ≈ 10⁻¹⁸ esu·cm = 1 Debye.The fact that the definition reproduces the Debye is the giveaway — no other option can generate esu·cm.
3. Eliminate (a): "distance between two atoms" is the bond length — it is only the d part, with the charge factor missing.
4. Eliminate (c): "energy to break a polar bond" is the bond dissociation enthalpy, a completely different quantity measured in kJ mol⁻¹.
5. Eliminate (d): the electronegativity difference decides whether a bond is polar and roughly how polar, but it is dimensionless and says nothing about bond length — HF and HCl illustrate this (bigger ΔEN for HF but shorter bond).

 (a) distance between atoms             [L]               = bond length only
 (b) charge x distance                  [Q][L] = esu cm   = DEBYE      <-- answer (b)
 (c) energy to break polar bond         [M][L]^2[T]^-2    = bond energy
 (d) electronegativity difference       dimensionless     = ΔEN, not μ

 check :  q ~ 10⁻¹⁰ esu ,  d ~ 10⁻⁸ cm
          μ ~ 10⁻¹⁰ × 10⁻⁸ = 10⁻¹⁸ esu cm = 1 D   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Settle every "which is the definition" MCQ by a dimension check — you never need to recall the sentence, only that μ must come out in esu·cm (or C·m). Trap: option (d) is the seductive one, because ΔEN really does correlate with polarity; but HF (ΔEN 1.9, μ 1.78 D) versus HCl (ΔEN 0.9, μ 1.07 D) works only because bond length happens to cooperate — for CH₃F vs CH₃Cl the same reasoning gives the wrong order.
Answer: (b) The product of magnitude of charge and the distance between the centres of positive and negative charges.
2For a molecule AB having a single polar bond, the bond length is doubled. The dipole moment of AB will: (Level-2)
(a) Become half   (b) Become double   (c) Become zero   (d) Remain unchanged		μ ∝ dDipole moment shortcut — how μ scales with charge and bond length🟡 SimilarbBecome double
Concept For a diatomic (one-bond) molecule the molecular dipole moment is the bond dipole moment — there is no vector addition to do, so μ = q × d directly. The question freezes q (the charge separation is unchanged; only the geometry is stretched) and scales d. Because μ is linear in d, whatever factor multiplies d multiplies μ by the same factor. This is exactly the reasoning that makes CH₃Cl more polar than CH₃F despite fluorine's higher electronegativity — there d wins.
Key relation μ = q × d with q held constant ⇒ μ ∝ d ⇒ μ₂ / μ₁ = d₂ / d₁ a direct (first-power) proportionality — not inverse, not square. Doubling d therefore doubles μ.
Solution
1. Start state: μ₁ = q·d.
2. New state: d₂ = 2d, q unchanged ⇒ μ₂ = q·(2d) = 2(q·d) = 2μ₁.
3. Put a number to it: take q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and d = 1.0 Å = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m ⇒ μ₁ = 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m; stretch to d = 2.0 Å ⇒ μ₂ = 3.2 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m, which is twice μ₁.Working a concrete number is the fastest way to kill the "half"/"unchanged" distractors.
4. Eliminate (a) "half" — that would require μ ∝ 1/d, which is the law for electrostatic field, not for dipole moment.
5. Eliminate (c) "zero" — μ can only vanish if q = 0 (non-polar bond) or if bond moments cancel by symmetry; a single bond that is stretched stays polar.
6. Eliminate (d) "unchanged" — that would need μ to be independent of d, contradicting the definition itself.

 distance d    d             2d                    2
 μ = q x d     q·d           q·(2d) = 2 q d        2      <-- answer (b)

 numeric check   q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C
   d = 1.0 Å = 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ m  →  μ = 1.6×10⁻²⁹ C·m
   d = 2.0 Å = 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ m  →  μ = 3.2×10⁻²⁹ C·m   (×2 ✓)
⚡ JEE Trick For proportionality MCQs, write the formula and tag each symbol with its power: μ = q¹d¹, so both variables scale linearly. The same one-line habit answers "if charge is halved" (μ halves) and "if both are doubled" (μ ×4). Trap: option (c) "zero" tempts students who half-remember "symmetrical molecules have zero dipole moment" — but AB is diatomic and can never be symmetrical unless A = B.
Answer: (b) Become double.
3For the molecules H₂O, BeCl₂, SO₂, and BF₃, identify all molecules that have non-zero dipole moment. (Level-3)
(a) H₂O only   (b) H₂O and SO₂   (c) H₂O and BeCl₂   (d) SO₂ and BF₃		Shape vs net μCounting the molecules with zero dipole moment among CH₄, BF₃, H₂O, HF, NH₃, CO₂, SO₂🟡 SimilarbH₂O and SO₂
Concept Every one of these four molecules contains polar bonds — so bond polarity alone cannot separate them. What decides the molecular dipole moment is whether those bond vectors cancel, and that is fixed by shape, which VSEPR gives from the steric number. Bent (angular) and pyramidal shapes leave a residual; linear AB₂ and trigonal-planar AB₃ cancel exactly. So the whole question reduces to: compute the steric number, read off the shape, then add vectors.
Key relation Steric number SN = (number of σ-bonded atoms) + (number of lone pairs on the central atom), and μnet = |Σ μ⃗bond|. Symmetric arrangement of identical bond vectors (linear 180°, trigonal planar 120°, tetrahedral 109.5°) ⇒ vector sum = 0. Any lone pair that distorts that symmetry ⇒ non-zero μ, and the lone-pair dipole itself adds in too.
Solution
1. H₂O: O has 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs ⇒ SN = 4, shape bent (104.5°). The two O–H moments are at an angle, so they add to a resultant pointing at oxygen: μ ≈ 1.84 D ≠ 0.
2. BeCl₂: Be has 2 bond pairs + 0 lone pairs ⇒ SN = 2, shape linear (180°). The two equal Be–Cl moments point exactly opposite: μ = 0.Be in BeCl₂ is electron-deficient with no lone pair — this is the standard exception students forget.
3. SO₂: S has 2 bonded O + 1 lone pair ⇒ SN = 3, shape bent (≈119°). The S–O moments do not cancel: μ ≈ 1.62 D ≠ 0. Compare with CO₂, which has no lone pair, is linear, and gives 0.
4. BF₃: B has 3 bond pairs + 0 lone pairs ⇒ SN = 3, shape trigonal planar (120°). Three equal vectors at 120° sum to zero: μ = 0.
5. Non-zero set = {H₂O, SO₂} ⇒ option (b). (c) is wrong because BeCl₂ is linear; (d) is wrong because BF₃ is planar-symmetric; (a) forgets SO₂'s lone pair.

 BeCl₂     Be        2   0   2  linear 180°        μ ⟵ ⟶ μ  cancel  0
 SO₂       S         2   1   3  bent  ≈119°       do not cancel     1.62 D  ≠ 0
 BF₃       B         3   0   3  trigonal planar    3 × 120° cancel   0

⚡ JEE Trick Do not compare electronegativities — count lone pairs on the central atom. For AB₂/AB₃/AB₄ species: no lone pair on the central atom ⇒ μ = 0; lone pair present ⇒ μ ≠ 0. That single rule handles CO₂/SO₂, BF₃/NF₃ and CH₄/NH₃ in one stroke. Trap: BeCl₂ and SO₂ look alike on paper (both AB₂), but Be has no lone pair and S has one — that is the whole difference.
Answer: (b) H₂O and SO₂.
4Which of the following molecules must have zero dipole moment solely due to symmetry? (Level-4, one or more than one option may be correct)
(a) CO₂   (b) H₂O   (c) NH₃   (d) BF₃		Symmetry ⇒ μ = 0JEE Advanced — picking the set of molecules with zero dipole moment🟡 Similara,dCO₂ and BF₃
Concept The phrase "solely due to symmetry" is doing real work here. It means: the individual bonds are polar, yet the molecule is not, purely because the bond vectors are arranged so that they annihilate one another. That happens only when the central atom has no lone pair and all the peripheral atoms are identical — then the bond vectors sit at the perfect VSEPR angles (180°, 120°, 109.5°) and their resultant is exactly zero. A lone pair breaks that symmetry twice over: it distorts the angles, and it contributes its own dipole.
Key relation Σ μ⃗ = 0 for a symmetric ABn with no lone pair. Two-vector case: μnet = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂cos θ); for equal moments this becomes μnet = 2μ cos(θ/2). At θ = 180° (linear) cos 90° = 0 ⇒ μnet = 0. At θ = 104.5° (water) cos 52.25° = 0.61 ⇒ μnet = 1.22 μ ≠ 0.
Solution
1. (a) CO₂: C has 2 σ-bonded O and 0 lone pairs ⇒ SN = 2 ⇒ linear, O=C=O at 180°. The two equal C=O moments are anti-parallel: μ = 0. Correct.Each C=O bond is strongly polar (ΔEN ≈ 1.0) — the cancellation is purely geometric, exactly what the question asks for.
2. (b) H₂O: 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs ⇒ bent 104.5°. Using μnet = 2μOHcos(θ/2) with μOH ≈ 1.5 D: μ ≈ 2(1.5)(0.61) ≈ 1.84 D. Not zero.
3. (c) NH₃: 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair ⇒ pyramidal 107°; the three N–H moments give a resultant along the axis and the lone-pair dipole adds to it ⇒ μ = 1.47 D. Not zero.
4. (d) BF₃: B has 3 σ-bonds and 0 lone pairs ⇒ SN = 3 ⇒ trigonal planar, 120°. Resolve: two of the three B–F vectors give a resultant of magnitude 2μcos60° = μ pointing exactly opposite the third ⇒ total 0. Correct.
5. So the answer set is (a) and (d) — the two molecules whose central atoms carry no lone pair.

Trap: "multiple correct" items are usually set so that exactly the lone-pair-free species qualify; picking H₂O or NH₃ because "the bonds are polar" is the standard error — polar bonds are a prerequisite, not the criterion.
Answer: (a, d) CO₂ and BF₃.
5Although F is more electronegative than H, the dipole moment of NH₃ is greater than that of NF₃. Select all correct statements. (Level-5, one or more than one option may be correct)
(a) In NH₃, the lone-pair dipole adds to the resultant of N–H bond dipoles   (b) In NF₃, the N–F bond dipoles oppose the lone-pair dipole   (c) In NF₃, the electronegativity difference between N and F is smaller than between N and H   (d) All bond moments in NF₃ are equal in magnitude and cancel perfectly		NH₃ vs NF₃Although fluorine is much more electronegative than hydrogen yet the dipole moment of NF₃ is smaller🟢 Exacta,bLone-pair dipole adds in NH₃; N–F dipoles oppose the lone pair in NF₃
Concept Both molecules are pyramidal with one lone pair on nitrogen, so both have two contributions to the net moment: the resultant of the three N–X bond moments, and the orbital (lone-pair) dipole pointing away from N along the lone-pair lobe. The whole puzzle is decided by the relative direction of those two contributions, which is fixed by which atom is more electronegative. In NH₃ nitrogen is the electronegative end, so the bond moments point towards N — the same way as the lone pair — and the two add. In NF₃ fluorine is more electronegative, so the bond moments point away from N — opposite to the lone pair — and they subtract.
Key relation μnet = μlone pair ± μresultant of 3 bonds — plus when they are parallel, minus when anti-parallel. Electronegativities: N = 3.0, H = 2.1, F = 4.0 ⇒ ΔEN(N–H) = 0.9 and ΔEN(N–F) = 1.0 — nearly the same magnitude, so the direction, not the size, is what decides. Observed: μ(NH₃) = 1.47 D = 4.90 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m; μ(NF₃) = 0.24 D = 0.80 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m.
Solution
1. (a) In NH₃ the N–H moments run Hδ+ → Nδ−, i.e. up towards nitrogen, the same direction as the lone-pair dipole. They add: 1.47 D. Correct.
2. (b) In NF₃ the N–F moments run Nδ+ → Fδ−, i.e. down and away from nitrogen, opposite to the lone-pair dipole, so they partly cancel it, collapsing the net moment to 0.24 D. Correct.
3. (c) ΔEN(N–F) = 4.0 − 3.0 = 1.0 and ΔEN(N–H) = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, so the N–F difference is slightly larger, not smaller — the statement is false (and it is also the reason the question calls the result surprising).
4. (d) The three N–F moments are indeed equal in magnitude, but in a pyramidal (not planar) geometry three equal vectors do not cancel — they leave an axial resultant. If they cancelled perfectly, NF₃ would have μ = 0 exactly, whereas the measured value is 0.24 D. False.Perfect cancellation of three equal vectors needs a trigonal-planar arrangement at 120°, as in BF₃.
5. Correct statements: (a) and (b).

 NH₃       0.9     H → N   (towards N)     towards lone pair   ADD  (+)        1.47 D
                                                                 = 4.90×10⁻³⁰ C·m
 NF₃       1.0     N → F   (away from N)   towards lone pair   OPPOSE (−)      0.24 D

⚡ JEE Trick Draw the pyramid and put one arrow for the lone pair (always pointing out of N) plus one resultant arrow for the three bonds; then just read whether the arrowheads agree. Same-direction ⇒ big μ (NH₃, H₂O), opposite ⇒ small μ (NF₃, NCl₃-type). Trap: option (c) is a genuine factual reversal — ΔEN for N–F is the larger one, which is precisely why the observed order looks paradoxical.

Concept VSEPR turns shape prediction into arithmetic. Count the steric number SN on the central atom (σ-bonded atoms + lone pairs); SN fixes the electron-pair geometry and the ideal angle, and then each lone pair compresses the angle because lp–bp repulsion > bp–bp repulsion. Multiple bonds count as one region (one σ), which is why HCN behaves as an AX₂ linear species even though it has a triple bond.
Formula SN = σ-bonded atoms + lone pairs on the central atom, and for a neutral central atom lone pairs = (valence e⁻ − number of σ bonds) ÷ 2. 1. CF₄: C has 4 valence e⁻, forms 4 σ bonds ⇒ lp = (4 − 4)/2 = 0, SN = 4 + 0 = 4 ⇒ tetrahedral, 109.5°.No lone pair, four identical F ⇒ perfectly symmetric, so it is also non-polar (μ = 0).
2. NF₃: N has 5 valence e⁻, 3 σ bonds ⇒ lp = (5 − 3)/2 = 1, SN = 3 + 1 = 4 ⇒ electron geometry tetrahedral, molecular shape trigonal pyramidal, angle compressed from 109.5° to about 102°.The compression is larger than in NH₃ (107°) because the bonding pairs are pulled towards the very electronegative F, so they repel each other less.

2Define and explain the term: Dipole moment.Definition & units of μDipole moment — full NCERT treatment with units and the Debye🟢 Exact—
Concept In a polar molecule one end is δ− and the other δ+, so the molecule possesses an electric dipole. Because the molecule as a whole is neutral, the negative charge is always exactly equal in magnitude to the positive charge — this is what lets a single q appear in the definition. Dipole moment quantifies the strength of that dipole and, being a vector, also records the direction of charge separation. A full board answer must give the sentence, the equation, both unit systems and the vector convention.
Definition "The product of magnitude of negative or positive charge (q) and the distance (d) between the centres of positive and negative charges is called dipole moment." Mathematically μ = q × d. It is a vector, represented by an arrow with its tail at the positive centre and head pointing to the negative end: + ⟼ −.
Solution
1. State the definition exactly as above and write μ = q × d.
2. Derive the CGS unit. q is of the order of 10⁻¹⁰ esu and the internuclear distance d of the order of 10⁻⁸ cm, so μ ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ × 10⁻⁸ = 10⁻¹⁸ esu·cm. This quantity is named one Debye (D): 1 D = 1 × 10⁻¹⁸ esu·cm.
3. Convert to SI. 1 esu = (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)/(4.803 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 3.335 × 10⁻¹⁰ C and 1 cm = 10⁻² m, therefore 1 D = 10⁻¹⁸ × (3.335 × 10⁻¹⁰ C)(10⁻² m) = 3.335 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m. The SI unit is the coulomb metre (C·m).
4. Explain the vector nature. For a diatomic like HCl the molecular moment equals the bond moment (μ = 1.07 D); for a polyatomic it is the vector sum of all bond moments, so a molecule with polar bonds can still have μ = 0 (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄).
5. Give the significance: larger ΔEN ⇒ larger bond moment, e.g. HF (1.78 D) > HCl (1.07 D) > HBr (0.79 D) > HI (0.38 D); and μ = 0 identifies a non-polar or symmetric molecule (H₂, O₂, N₂, CO₂).

3Explain dipole moments of polyatomic molecules.Vector addition of bond momentsDipole moment of polyatomic molecules — polar vs non-polar by vector sum🟡 Similar—
Concept A diatomic molecule has only one polar bond, so its molecular moment is that bond moment. A polyatomic molecule has several polar bonds — called bond moments — and the molecular dipole moment is their resultant. Since each bond moment is a vector, the resultant depends not only on the individual magnitudes but critically on their arrangement in space. That is why two molecules built from equally polar bonds can have wildly different μ, and why some molecules made entirely of polar bonds have μ = 0.
Key relation μmolecule = |Σ μ⃗bond|; for two equal bond moments at angle θ, μnet = 2 μbond cos(θ/2), and in general μnet = √(μ₁² + μ₂² + 2μ₁μ₂ cos θ). A lone pair on the central atom contributes an extra orbital dipole which must be added into the same vector sum — this is what makes NH₃ (1.47 D) so much more polar than NF₃ (0.24 D).
Solution
1. Unsymmetrical molecules — moments add. Water: two O–H bond moments at 104.5°, resultant μ = 1.84 D pointing at oxygen. Ammonia: three N–H moments plus the lone-pair dipole, resultant μ = 1.47 D along the axis.
2. Two polar bonds, net zero. In CO₂ (O=C=O), CS₂ (S=C=S) and BeF₂ (F–Be–F) the two equal bond moments are exactly anti-parallel (180°), so μ = 0 even though each bond is polar.Substituting θ = 180° in μ = 2μbondcos(θ/2) gives 2μcos90° = 0.
3. Symmetrical molecules, net zero. BF₃ (trigonal planar, three F at 120°), CH₄ and CCl₄ (tetrahedral, four identical atoms at 109.5°) all cancel exactly ⇒ μ = 0 — the molecule is symmetrical.
4. Consequence used as a test. If a molecule with three or more atoms shows μ ≠ 0 it cannot be symmetrical (H₂O 1.84 D, H₂S 0.95 D, NH₃ 1.47 D are bent/pyramidal); if μ = 0 the arrangement of identical atoms about the central atom must be symmetrical (BF₃, CH₄, CCl₄).
5. Direction matters as much as magnitude — NH₃ and NF₃ have nearly equal ΔEN per bond (0.9 vs 1.0) yet μ differs six-fold, purely because the lone-pair dipole adds in NH₃ and opposes in NF₃.

4Find the dipole moment of BF₃ molecule.BF₃ — μ = 0Dipole moment of BF₃ worked out from its trigonal-planar geometry🟢 Exact—

5What is the contribution of lone pairs of electrons toward dipole moment of a molecule?Orbital (lone-pair) dipoleDipole Moments — presence of lone pairs, examples of non-polar and polar compounds🟡 Similar—
Concept A lone pair is a concentrated blob of negative charge sitting on one side of the central atom and not balanced by any nucleus on the far side. That asymmetric charge cloud is itself a dipole — the orbital dipole or lone-pair moment — with its negative end pointing away from the atom along the lone-pair lobe. It must therefore be included in the vector sum along with the bond moments. Ignoring it makes NH₃ vs NF₃, and H₂O vs its bond moments, impossible to explain.
Key relation μnet = μ⃗bond resultant + μ⃗lone pair The lone-pair vector always points outwards from the central atom, along the lobe. Whether it reinforces or cancels the bond resultant depends on whether the central atom is the δ− end (bond moments point towards it — they reinforce) or the δ+ end (bond moments point away — they oppose).
Solution
1. A lone pair contributes its own dipole. The unshared pair creates charge asymmetry, so it behaves as an additional moment directed along the lone-pair orbital away from the nucleus.
2. It can reinforce. In NH₃, N is more electronegative than H, so all three N–H moments point towards N, i.e. the same way as the lone pair. Sum: 1.47 D — much larger than the bond resultant alone.
3. It can oppose. In NF₃, F is more electronegative than N, so the three N–F moments point away from N, opposite to the lone pair. They partly cancel it and the net collapses to only 0.24 D — even though each N–F bond is more polar than an N–H bond (ΔEN 1.0 vs 0.9).
4. It is why bent/pyramidal molecules are polar at all. H₂O carries two lone pairs whose moments add to the O–H resultant, giving 1.84 D — larger than NH₃ (1.47 D) despite having one fewer bond.
5. And why lone-pair-free symmetric molecules are not. CO₂, BF₃, CH₄ and CCl₄ have no lone pair on the central atom, so the bond moments alone must cancel, and they do ⇒ μ = 0.

3. NF₃: fluorine is the more electronegative partner, so density flows N → F. Each bond dipole now points down from nitrogen towards the fluorines — opposite to the lone pair. Destructive ⇒ the two contributions nearly annihilate ⇒ tiny μ = 0.2 D.
4. Match to the options: NH₃ same direction, NF₃ opposite direction ⇒ option (d). Option (b) is exactly the reverse statement and is the trap. Options (a) and (c) claim the two molecules behave identically, which cannot possibly explain a sevenfold difference in μ.

Concept The book repeats this item verbatim (it is also Level-6 Q3), and the key gives (d) both times — worth taking as a deliberate reinforcement rather than a misprint. The deeper point being drilled is the split between two different questions: is the bond polar? is answered by electronegativity, while is the molecule polar? is answered by vector addition over the molecular geometry. A molecule can be non-polar while every single bond in it is polar, provided the geometry is symmetric enough to cancel them.
Statement check A : true  ·  R : true  ·  R ⇏ A ⇒ (d) The chain that would explain A is: identical substituents + no lone pair on C + regular tetrahedral geometry ⇒ Σμ⃗ = 0. The printed reason supplies none of those three links.
Solution
1. Classify the bond. ΔEN(C–Cl) = 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5. That is well above the ≈0.4 threshold for a polar covalent bond and far below the ≈1.7 needed for ionic ⇒ the C–Cl bond is polar covalent. Reason = true.
2. Classify the molecule. Carbon has four bond pairs and zero lone pairs (SN = 4) with four identical chlorines ⇒ regular tetrahedron, bond angle 109°28′. Resolve the four moments: any one C–Cl vector is exactly cancelled by the resultant of the other three ⇒ Σμ⃗ = 0 ⇒ μ = 0. Assertion = true.
