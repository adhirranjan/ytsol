Concept When velocity is handed to us as a function of position rather than time, a = dv/dt cannot be applied directly — differentiating v(x) with respect to t needs a chain rule, because x itself depends on t. The standard escape is the identity a = v·dv/dx, which converts a position-dependent velocity law straight into acceleration without ever solving for x(t). The problem statement itself hands us this identity, so the whole task reduces to one derivative and one multiplication.
Formula a = dv/dt = (dv/dx)·(dx/dt) = v·(dv/dx). Units: v in m s⁻¹, x in m, so dv/dx has units s⁻¹, and v·(dv/dx) → (m s⁻¹)(s⁻¹) = m s⁻². The chain rule inserts dx/dt = v, which is exactly what turns a d/dx derivative into a d/dt one. Given v = 5x + 2 (v in m s⁻¹, x in m; so 5 carries units s⁻¹ and 2 carries m s⁻¹).
Solution
1. Write the given velocity law: v = (5x + 2) m s⁻¹.Velocity depends on position x, not on time — this is the signal to use a = v·dv/dx.
2. Differentiate v with respect to x: dv/dx = d(5x + 2)/dx = 5 s⁻¹.The derivative of 5x is 5; the constant 2 differentiates to 0.
3. Substitute into a = v·(dv/dx): a = (5x + 2) m s⁻¹ × 5 s⁻¹.
4. Expand the product: a = (25x + 10) m s⁻².5 × 5x = 25x and 5 × 2 = 10; the s⁻¹ × s⁻¹ gives s⁻², confirming an acceleration.
5. Dimensional check: 25x → (s⁻²)(m) = m s⁻²; the constant 10 is in m s⁻². Both terms are accelerations, so the sum is consistent.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the three faces of acceleration and pick by what the data depends on: given v(t) use a = dv/dt; given v(x) use a = v·dv/dx; given a(x) and needing v use v dv = a dx. Shortcut: for any linear v = kx + c, a = v·dv/dx = k(kx + c) = k²x + kc — always linear in x with slope k². Here k = 5 ⇒ 25x + 10 on sight. Trap: writing a = dv/dx = 5 forgets the factor v — that is a velocity gradient (s⁻¹), not an acceleration.
Answer: a = (25x + 10) m s⁻² — acceleration varies linearly with position x.

4For a moving body, at any instant of time, which statement about acceleration/velocity/distance is correct (conceptual MCQ)Kinematics concept-checkFor a moving body at any instant of time — if not moving, is acceleration necessarily zero? · Same MCQ — AAKASH🟢 Exactbif the body is slowing, the acceleration is negative
Concept A pure concept-check on the vector nature of acceleration and its relation to velocity. Test each statement against a physical counterexample. The key idea: acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, it points along the change in v, and its sign is only meaningful relative to a chosen positive direction — being "zero-velocity" or "slowing down" says nothing absolute about the sign of a.
Formula a = dv/dt (a vector). A body slowing down has a directed opposite to v (retardation); with the direction of motion taken positive, that makes a negative. Judge each option by finding a case that satisfies or breaks it.
Solution
1. "If the body is not moving, acceleration is necessarily zero" — false. At the top of a vertical throw v = 0 yet a = g downward. Instantaneous zero speed ≠ zero acceleration.
2. "If the body is slowing down, the acceleration is negative" — taking the direction of motion as positive, a slowing body has a opposite to v, hence a < 0. Correct in the standard sign convention (motion along +x).
3. Distractor type "uniform speed ⇒ zero acceleration" — false for circular motion: speed constant but a = v²/r ≠ 0 (centripetal).
4. Distractor type "acceleration always along velocity" — false; in deceleration or projectile motion a and v point differently.
5. Only statement (b) survives every test, so it is the correct choice.
 (a) v=0 => a=0 ?   NO  (top of throw: v=0, a=g)
 (b) slowing => a<0 ?  YES (a opposite v, +x taken)   <-- correct
 (c) const speed => a=0 ?  NO (circular: a=v^2/r)
 (d) a always || v ?  NO  (deceleration/projectile)
⚡ JEE Trick Break conceptual kinematics MCQs with three killer counterexamples: (i) top of a vertical throw — v = 0, a ≠ 0; (ii) uniform circular motion — |v| constant, a ≠ 0; (iii) any deceleration — a antiparallel to v. One of them usually falsifies every wrong option. Trap for (b): "slowing ⇒ a negative" is only true when the direction of motion is taken as positive; if the body moves along −x while slowing, a is positive. In the conventional set-up (motion = +x) the statement is the intended correct one.
Answer: (b) If the body is slowing down, the acceleration is negative (with the direction of motion taken as positive).

Concept The word "uniform" in kinematics always means "constant in time," and it must be read against the quantity it labels. Uniform velocity means velocity does not change (so a = 0). Uniform acceleration means the acceleration is constant, so velocity changes but always at the same steady rate. Non-uniform (variable) acceleration is the negation of that: the acceleration itself changes with time, so velocity still changes — but at a rate that is itself changing.
Key relation a = dv/dt = the rate of change of velocity, in m s⁻². Acceleration is a rate; saying it is non-uniform means that rate is not fixed — a = a(t) rather than a = constant. So: a = 0 ⇒ constant velocity; a = constant ≠ 0 ⇒ velocity changes at a constant rate; a = a(t) ⇒ velocity changes at a variable rate.
Solution
1. Parse the term: "acceleration" already means velocity is changing, so any option claiming constant velocity is out.Rules out (a) constant velocity, which would require a = 0.
2. Parse "non-uniform": it directly denies that the acceleration is constant.Rules out (b) constant acceleration — that is the definition of UNIFORM acceleration, the exact opposite.
3. Distinguish (c) from (d): "changing velocity at a constant rate" is precisely uniform acceleration (a = constant, m s⁻² fixed), so (c) is wrong.
4. That leaves (d): velocity changes at a variable rate, i.e. a = a(t) is itself a function of time — the definition of non-uniform acceleration.
5. Concrete check: a = 3t m s⁻² gives a = 0 at t = 0 s, a = 3 m s⁻² at t = 1 s, a = 6 m s⁻² at t = 2 s — the rate of velocity change keeps growing, so the motion is non-uniformly accelerated.

 a = 3t m/s²           varies with t    changes at varying rate  (d) ✓
