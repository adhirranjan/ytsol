★ The A.P. ↔ G.P. dictionaryEverything in M-8A carries over with + replaced by ×: difference → ratio, a + (n−1)d → arn−1, "sum given" → "product given", arithmetic mean (a+c)/2 → geometric mean √(ac), and ar = mid-average → ar = √(ar−k·ar+k). Taking log of a G.P. turns it into an A.P. — that is the bridge between the two chapters.
⚠ Three killers(1) The sum formula fails at r = 1 — that case is Sn = na. (2) S∞ exists only for |r| < 1; at r = −1 the partial sums oscillate and at |r| > 1 they blow up. (3) Adding a constant to every term of a G.P. does not give a G.P. (unlike an A.P., where it does).
💡 The product identityFor three terms in G.P., b² = ac. For a whole G.P., a1an = a2an−1 = a3an−2 = … — the multiplicative twin of "equidistant terms sum to a constant". Half of the Strong Box is this one line.
L-1

What is a G.P. · The nth Term

  • A G.P. is a sequence in which each term is the previous one multiplied by a fixed non-zero number — the common ratio r. The first term is a.
  • So a1 = a, a2 = ar, a3 = ar², a4 = ar³ — the power of r is always one less than the position.
  • Written out: a, ar, ar², …, arn−1 if finite; a, ar, ar², ar³, ar⁴, … if infinite.
the defining testa2/a1 = a3/a2 = a4/a3 = … = an/an−1 = r , constant and non-zero
general terman = a·rn−1 — for a finite G.P. of n terms this is the last term

mth term from the end of a finite G.P.

walk backwards ⇒ ratio becomes 1/ram (from end) = ℓ · (1/r)m−1 where ℓ is the last term
⚠ Watch the sign of rA negative r makes the terms alternate in sign. In a9 = (1/4)(−2)8 the exponent 8 is even, so the answer is positive. Getting the parity wrong flips the sign of the whole answer — check whether n − 1 is odd or even before you compute.
💡 Divide two terms to kill aGiven any two terms, dividing them cancels a instantly: a7/a4 = r³. Never solve for a first — get r from a ratio, then back-substitute. This single move opens almost every G.P. question.

Book Example-1 · L-1
Which term of the G.P. 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4, … is 1/128?
a = 2, r = 1/2. Set a·rn−1 = 1/128: 2(1/2)n−1 = 1/128.
(1/2)n−1 = 1/256 = (1/2)8. Equal bases ⇒ equal exponents.
n − 1 = 8.
the 9th term
Book Example-2 · L-1
The 4th, 7th and last terms of a G.P. are 10, 80 and 2560. Find the first term and the number of terms.
ar³ = 10 and ar⁶ = 80. Dividing kills a: r³ = 80/10 = 8 ⇒ r = 2.
Back-substitute: a(2)³ = 10 ⇒ 8a = 10 ⇒ a = 5/4.
Last term: (5/4)·2n−1 = 2560 ⇒ 2n−1 = 2560 × 4/5 = 2048 = 211.
a = 5/4, and there are n = 12 terms
Book Example-3 · L-1
If the first and nth terms of a G.P. are a and b, and P is the product of the first n terms, prove that P² = (ab)n.
b = arn−1 ⇒ b/a = rn−1, i.e. r = (b/a)1/(n−1).
P = a·(ar)·(ar²)···(arn−1) = an·r1+2+…+(n−1) = an·rn(n−1)/2.
Substituting r: P = an·[(b/a)1/(n−1)]n(n−1)/2 = an(b/a)n/2.
= an·bn/2·a−n/2 = an/2bn/2 = (ab)n/2.
P² = [(ab)n/2]² = (ab)n ∎
Book Example-4 · L-1
What will Rs. 500 amount to in 10 years in a bank paying 10% annual interest, compounded annually?
After one year: P + PR/100 = P(1 + R/100). After two years the same factor applies again: P(1 + R/100)².
So the yearly amounts form a G.P. with common ratio (1 + R/100).
The amount after 10 years is the 11th term: P(1 + R/100)10 = 500(1 + 10/100)10.
Rs. 500 × (1.1)10 (≈ Rs. 1296.87)
Book Example-5 · L-1
Find the 5th term from the end of the G.P. 2/27, 2/9, 2/3, …, 162.
a = 2/27 and r = (2/9) ÷ (2/27) = 27/9 = 3; last term ℓ = 162.
5th from the end = ℓ(1/r)4 = 162 × (1/3)⁴ = 162/81.

Sum of the First n Terms

the sum (r ≠ 1)Sn = a(1 − rn)/(1 − r) = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1)
using the last term ℓSn = (a − ℓr)/(1 − r) = (ℓr − a)/(r − 1)
the special caser = 1 ⇒ every term equals a ⇒ Sn = na
The multiply-and-subtract derivation
Write Sn = a + ar + ar² + … + arn−1 … (i), then multiply throughout by r: rSn = ar + ar² + … + arn−1 + arn … (ii). Subtracting (ii) from (i), every middle term cancels and only the two ends survive: Sn − rSn = a − arn, so Sn(1 − r) = a(1 − rn). This "multiply by the ratio, then subtract" trick is the same one used for arithmetic-geometric series later.
💡 The two forms are identicalSome books quote a(1 − rn)/(1 − r) "for r < 1" and a(rn − 1)/(r − 1) "for r > 1". They are the same expression with numerator and denominator both negated — use whichever keeps your arithmetic positive. The only genuine restriction is r ≠ 1.
⚠ Don't divide by zeroAt r = 1 the formula reads 0/0. If a question's algebra produces r = 1, switch to Sn = na rather than pushing the formula through.
Worked example from the module
Book Example · L-2 theory
Find the sum of 7 terms of the G.P. 3, 6, 12, …
a = 3, r = 6/3 = 2, n = 7. Since r > 1 use Sn = a(rn − 1)/(r − 1).
S7 = 3(27 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 3(128 − 1) = 3 × 127.
S7 = 381

Selecting Terms in a G.P.

When a question gives you the product of some unknown terms in G.P., centre them on a — the r's then cancel out of the product completely.

3 termsa/r , a , ar product = a³
4 termsa/r³ , a/r , ar , ar³ product = a⁴ (ratio between neighbours is r²)
5 termsa/r² , a/r , a , ar , ar² product = a⁵
★ Mirror of the A.P. ruleWhere an A.P. uses a − d, a, a + d so the sum is 3a, a G.P. uses a/r, a, ar so the product is a³. For an even count the ratio between adjacent chosen terms is r², exactly as the A.P.'s four-term layout has common difference 2d.
💡 The r + 1/r substitutionAfter the product fixes a, the sum condition always collapses into a polynomial in x = r + 1/r (or √r + 1/√r for four terms), because a/r + ar = a(r + 1/r). Solve for x first, then recover r from r² − xr + 1 = 0. Trying to expand in r directly gives a quartic for no reason.
Worked example from the module
Book Example · L-2 theory
If the sum of three numbers in G.P. is 38 and their product is 1728, find them.
Take them as a/r, a, ar. Product = a³ = 1728 ⇒ a = 12.
Sum: a(1/r + 1 + r) = 38 ⇒ 12(1 + r + r²)/r = 38.
6(1 + r + r²) = 19r ⇒ 6r² − 13r + 6 = 0 ⇒ (3r − 2)(2r − 3) = 0 ⇒ r = 3/2 or 2/3.
r = 3/2 gives 8, 12, 18; r = 2/3 gives the same three numbers reversed.
8, 12, 18 (or 18, 12, 8)
L-2

Sum of an Infinite G.P.

the only case that converges|r| < 1 ⇒ rn → 0 as n → ∞ ⇒ S∞ = a / (1 − r)
the cases that don't|r| > 1 ⇒ Sn → ∞ (no sum) · r = −1 ⇒ the partial sums oscillate · r = 1 ⇒ Sn = na → ∞
⚠ Always test |r| < 1 before using itWhen a question produces two candidate ratios (say r = 2/3 and r = 3/2), the infinite-sum condition −1 < r < 1 is what rejects one of them. Skipping that check is the standard way to lose an otherwise-correct answer.
💡 Three standard uses(1) Recurring decimals: 0.5̄ = 5/10 + 5/100 + … = (5/10)/(1 − 1/10) = 5/9. (2) Infinite exponent towers: 61/2·61/4·61/8··· = 6(1/2+1/4+1/8+…) = 61. (3) Trig series: Σ cos2nθ is a G.P. with ratio cos²θ, so it sums to 1/(1 − cos²θ) = 1/sin²θ = cosec²θ.
Worked example from the module
Book Example · L-2 theory
Find the sum to infinity of the G.P. −5/4, 5/16, −5/64, …
a = −5/4 and r = (5/16) ÷ (−5/4) = −1/4. Here |r| = 1/4 < 1, so the sum exists.
S = a/(1 − r) = (−5/4)/(1 + 1/4) = (−5/4)/(5/4).
S = −1

Solved Examples — sums

Solved Examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-2
Find the sum of 10 terms of the G.P. 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, …
a = 1, r = 1/2, n = 10. Use Sn = a(1 − rn)/(1 − r).
S10 = [1 − (1/2)10]/(1/2) = 2(1 − 1/1024) = 2(1023/1024).
S10 = 1023/512
Book Example-2 · L-2
Find four numbers in G.P. whose sum is 85 and product is 4096.
Take a/r³, a/r, ar, ar³. Product = a⁴ = 4096 = 8⁴ ⇒ a = 8.
Sum: a(r³ + 1/r³) + a(r + 1/r) = 85, i.e. 8(r³ + 1/r³) + 8(r + 1/r) = 85.
Put x = r + 1/r. Then r³ + 1/r³ = x³ − 3x, so 8(x³ − 3x) + 8x = 85 ⇒ 8x³ − 16x − 85 = 0.
Factorise: (2x − 5)(4x² + 10x + 17) = 0; the quadratic has imaginary roots, so x = 5/2.
r + 1/r = 5/2 ⇒ 2r² − 5r + 2 = 0 ⇒ (r − 2)(2r − 1) = 0 ⇒ r = 2 or 1/2.
1, 4, 16, 64 (or 64, 16, 4, 1)
Book Example-3 · L-2
Find the sum to n terms of the sequence an = 2n + 3n.
Split the sum into the two families it is made of: Σ2k (a G.P.) and Σ3k (an A.P.).
2 + 2² + … + 2n = 2(2n − 1)/(2 − 1) = 2(2n − 1).
3(1 + 2 + … + n) = 3·n(n + 1)/2.
Sn = 2(2n − 1) + 3n(n + 1)/2
Book Example-4 · L-2
Find the sum of 0.7 + 0.77 + 0.777 + … to n terms.
Pull out 7 and then multiply-and-divide by 9: = (7/9){0.9 + 0.99 + 0.999 + … } = (7/9){9/10 + 99/100 + 999/1000 + …}.
Each bracket is 1 − 1/10k, so the sum is (7/9){n − (1/10 + 1/10² + … + 1/10n)}.
The inner series is a G.P. with a = r = 1/10: its sum is (1/9)(1 − 1/10n).
= (7/9){n − (1/9)(1 − 1/10n)}.
Sn = (7/81){9n − 1 + 1/10n}
Book Example-5 · L-2
How many terms of the geometric series 1 + 4 + 16 + 64 + … make the sum 5461?
a = 1, r = 4. So (4n − 1)/(4 − 1) = 5461.
4n − 1 = 3 × 5461 = 16383 ⇒ 4n = 16384 = 47.
n = 7
Book Example-6 · L-2
In an increasing G.P. the sum of the first and last terms is 66, the product of the second and second-last is 128, and the total sum is 126. How many terms are there?
a2·an−1 = a1·an (equidistant pairs have equal products), so a·(arn−1) = 128 ⇒ arn−1 = 128/a.
Then a + 128/a = 66 ⇒ a² − 66a + 128 = 0 ⇒ (a − 2)(a − 64) = 0 ⇒ a = 2 or 64.
a = 2 gives rn−1 = 32; a = 64 gives rn−1 = 1/32, which needs r < 1 — rejected since the G.P. is increasing.
Sum: 126 = 2(rn − 1)/(r − 1) ⇒ (r·rn−1 − 1)/(r − 1) = 63 ⇒ (32r − 1) = 63(r − 1) ⇒ 31r = 62 ⇒ r = 2.
2n−1 = 32 = 25 ⇒ n − 1 = 5. (Check: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 — sum 126 ✓, ends 2 + 64 = 66 ✓, 4 × 32 = 128 ✓.)
6 terms
Book Example-7 · L-2
If Sn, S2n, S3n are the sums of n, 2n and 3n terms of a G.P., prove that Sn(S3n − S2n) = (S2n − Sn)².
Write k = a/(r − 1). Then Sn = k(rn − 1), S2n = k(r2n − 1), S3n = k(r3n − 1).
LHS = k(rn − 1)·k(r3n − r2n) = k²(rn − 1)·r2n(rn − 1) = k²r2n(rn − 1)².
RHS = [k(r2n − rn)]² = [k·rn(rn − 1)]² = k²r2n(rn − 1)².
LHS = RHS ∎ — equivalently, Sn, S2n − Sn, S3n − S2n are themselves in G.P.
Book Example-8 · L-2
Find the sum to infinity of 1/2 + 1/3² + 1/2³ + 1/3⁴ + 1/2⁵ + 1/3⁶ + …
This is two interleaved G.P.s. Separate the odd and even positions.
Series I: 1/2 + 1/2³ + 1/2⁵ + … has a = 1/2, r = 1/4 ⇒ sum = (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3.
Series II: 1/3² + 1/3⁴ + 1/3⁶ + … has a = 1/9, r = 1/9 ⇒ sum = (1/9)/(8/9) = 1/8.
Total = 2/3 + 1/8 = (16 + 3)/24.
19/24
Book Example-9 · L-2
Prove that 61/2 × 61/4 × 61/8 × … ∞ = 6.
Same base multiplied ⇒ add the exponents: the product is 6(1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …).
That exponent is an infinite G.P. with a = 1/2, r = 1/2: sum = (1/2)/(1 − 1/2) = 1.
61 = 6 ∎
Book Example-10 · L-2
The sum of an infinite G.P. is 57 and the sum of the cubes of its terms is 9747. Find the G.P.
a/(1 − r) = 57 … (i). The cubes a³, a³r³, a³r⁶, … form a G.P. with ratio r³, so a³/(1 − r³) = 9747 … (ii).
Divide the cube of (i) by (ii): (1 − r³)/(1 − r)³ = 57³/9747 = 185193/9747 = 19.
(1 − r)(1 + r + r²)/(1 − r)³ = (1 + r + r²)/(1 − r)² = 19 ⇒ 1 + r + r² = 19(1 − 2r + r²).
18r² − 39r + 18 = 0 ⇒ (3r − 2)(6r − 9) = 0 ⇒ r = 2/3 or 3/2. Reject 3/2 — an infinite G.P. needs |r| < 1.
From (i): a = 57(1 − 2/3) = 57/3 = 19.
19, 38/3, 76/9, …
Book Example-11 · L-2
If x = Σ cos2nθ, y = Σ sin2nφ, z = Σ cos2nθ sin2nφ (all n = 0 to ∞, with 0 < θ, φ < π/2), prove that xz + yz − z = xy.
Each is an infinite G.P. with first term 1. x = 1/(1 − cos²θ) = 1/sin²θ ⇒ sin²θ = 1/x.
y = 1/(1 − sin²φ) = 1/cos²φ ⇒ cos²φ = 1/y.
z has ratio cos²θ·sin²φ, so z = 1/(1 − cos²θ sin²φ) = 1/[1 − (1 − sin²θ)(1 − cos²φ)].
= 1/[1 − (1 − 1/x)(1 − 1/y)] = 1/(1/x + 1/y − 1/xy) = xy/(x + y − 1).
Cross-multiplying: z(x + y − 1) = xy.
xz + yz − z = xy ∎
Book Example-12 · L-2
Use a geometric series to express 0.555… = 0.5̄ as a rational number.
0.5̄ = 0.5 + 0.05 + 0.005 + … = 5/10 + 5/100 + 5/1000 + …
An infinite G.P. with a = 5/10 and r = 1/10, so S = (5/10)/(1 − 1/10) = (5/10)/(9/10).

L-3

Properties of a G.P.

property 1 — what survives, what doesn'tai·K and ai/K → G.P. · air → G.P. · 1/ai → G.P. BUT ai ± K → NOT a G.P.
property 2 — combining two g.p.s{aibi} and {ai/bi} are G.P.s · {ai ± bi} is not, unless both have the same ratio
property 3 — the log bridgea1, a2, a3, … positive in G.P. ⇔ log a1, log a2, log a3, … in A.P.
property 4 — equidistant productsa1an = a2an−1 = a3an−2 = … constant
property 5 — every term is a geometric meana2² = a1a3 , a3² = a2a4 , … and generally ar = √(ar−k·ar+k)
⚠ The one that breaksAdding a constant to every term destroys a G.P. — 2, 4, 8 is a G.P. but 3, 5, 9 is not. This is the exact opposite of an A.P., where shifting is harmless and scaling changes d. Options built on "ai + K is in G.P." are always wrong.
💡 Take logs and the problem becomes M-8AAny G.P. statement about positive terms converts to an A.P. statement by taking logs, and back again by exponentiating. That is why "if a, b, c are in G.P. then log a, log b, log c are in A.P." shows up every year — and why exponent questions like ax = by = cz tie the three chapters M-8A, M-8B and M-8C together.

Book Example · L-3 theory
If a, b, c are in A.P. and x, y, z are in G.P., show that xb−c·yc−a·za−b = 1.
A.P. ⇒ 2b = a + c … (i). G.P. ⇒ y² = xz, so y = √(xz) … (ii).
Substitute (ii): xb−c·(xz)(c−a)/2·za−b.
Collect the x-exponent: (b − c) + (c − a)/2 = [2b − 2c + c − a]/2 = [2b − (a + c)]/2 = 0 by (i).
Collect the z-exponent: (a − b) + (c − a)/2 = [2a − 2b + c − a]/2 = [(a + c) − 2b]/2 = 0 likewise.
x⁰·z⁰ = 1 ∎
Solved Examples from the module
Book Example-1 · L-3
If a, b, c are in G.P. (a, b, c > 0), prove that log an, log bn, log cn are in A.P.
G.P. ⇒ b² = ac. Raise both sides to the nth power: b2n = ancn.
Take logs: log(bn)² = log(ancn) = log an + log cn.
So 2 log bn = log an + log cn — which is exactly the A.P. condition.
log an, log bn, log cn are in A.P. ∎
Book Example-2 · L-3
Three numbers whose sum is 15 are in A.P. Adding 1, 4, 19 to them respectively makes them a G.P. Find the numbers.
Take them as a − d, a, a + d. Sum = 3a = 15 ⇒ a = 5, so they are 5 − d, 5, 5 + d.
After adding 1, 4, 19: 6 − d, 9, 24 + d. G.P. ⇒ 9² = (6 − d)(24 + d).
81 = 144 − 18d − d² ⇒ d² + 18d − 63 = 0 ⇒ (d + 21)(d − 3) = 0 ⇒ d = 3 or −21.
d = 3 ⇒ 2, 5, 8 (adding gives 3, 9, 27 ✓). d = −21 ⇒ 26, 5, −16 (adding gives 27, 9, 3 ✓).
2, 5, 8 or 26, 5, −16
⚠ Book misprintThe module's answer line reads "25, 5, −16". With a = 5 and d = −21 the first term is 5 − (−21) = 26, not 25 — and only 26 makes the sum 15 (26 + 5 − 16 = 15 ✓, while 25 + 5 − 16 = 14 ✗). The d = −21 root itself is correct; only the arithmetic in the final line slipped.
Book Example-3 · L-3
If a, b, c, d are in G.P., prove that an + bn, bn + cn, cn + dn are also in G.P.
Write b = ar, c = ar², d = ar³.
an + bn = an(1 + rn) · bn + cn = anrn(1 + rn) · cn + dn = anr2n(1 + rn).
The three expressions are the same quantity an(1 + rn) multiplied by 1, rn, r2n.
They are in G.P. with common ratio rn ∎ (equivalently (bn+cn)² = (an+bn)(cn+dn))
Book Example-4 · L-3
If a, b, c, d are in G.P., show that (b − c)² + (c − a)² + (d − b)² = (a − d)².
Put b = ar, c = ar², d = ar³.
LHS = a²r²(1 − r)² + a²(r² − 1)² + a²r²(r² − 1)².
Expanding: a²[r² + r⁴ − 2r³ + r⁴ + 1 − 2r² + r⁶ + r² − 2r⁴] = a²[r⁶ − 2r³ + 1].
= a²(1 − r³)² = (a − ar³)².
= (a − d)² ∎ (sanity check with 1, 2, 4, 8: 4 + 9 + 36 = 49 = (1 − 8)² ✓)

JEE Main 2026 (28 Jan Shift-1) · Level-4
In a G.P. the product of the first three terms is 27, and the set of all possible values of the sum of the first three terms is R − (a, b). Find a² + b².
Take the three terms as A/r, A, Ar. Product = A³ = 27 ⇒ A = 3.
Sum = 3(1/r + 1 + r) = 3 + 3(r + 1/r).
For real r ≠ 0, r + 1/r ∈ (−∞, −2] ∪ [2, ∞) — it can never lie strictly between −2 and 2.
So the sum lies in (−∞, 3 − 6] ∪ [3 + 6, ∞) = (−∞, −3] ∪ [9, ∞); the missing open interval is (−3, 9).
Hence a = −3, b = 9 and a² + b² = 9 + 81.

JEE Main 2025 (22 Jan Shift-1) · Level-7
Let a1, a2, a3, … be a G.P. of increasing positive terms with a1a5 = 28 and a2 + a4 = 29. Then a6 is — (a) 628 (b) 812 (c) 526 (d) 784

So a2 and a4 are the roots of t² − 29t + 28 = 0 ⇒ t = 1 or 28. Increasing ⇒ a2 = 1, a4 = 28.
a4/a2 = r² = 28, and a6 = a4·r² = 28 × 28.

JEE Main 2024 (30 Jan Shift-2) · Level-6
a and b are distinct positive reals. The 11th term of a G.P. with first term a and third term b equals the pth term of another G.P. with first term a and fifth term b. Find p. (a) 20 (b) 25 (c) 21 (d) 24

The sum and product of four positive consecutive terms of a G.P. are 126 and 1296. The sum of the common ratios of all such G.P.s is — (a) 7 (b) 9/2 (c) 3 (d) 14
Terms a, ar, ar², ar³. Product = a⁴r⁶ = 1296 ⇒ (a²r³)² = 1296 ⇒ a²r³ = 36 ⇒ a = 6/r3/2.
Sum = a(1 + r)(1 + r²) = 126 ⇒ (1 + r)(1 + r²)/r3/2 = 21.
Put s = √r + 1/√r. Then (1+r)/√r = s and (1+r²)/r = s² − 2, so the equation becomes s³ − 2s = 21.
s = 3 is a root (27 − 6 = 21) and the remaining factor s² + 3s + 7 has no real roots.
√r + 1/√r = 3 ⇒ squaring, r + 1/r = 7 ⇒ r² − 7r + 1 = 0. The two ratios are the roots of this quadratic, so their sum is 7 by Vieta.
(a) 7
