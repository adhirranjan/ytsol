Concept Permanganate ion is deep purple while its reduced form Mn2+ is almost colourless. Because the reagent changes colour by itself at the end point, no external dye is needed. The trap is to think a titration always needs an added indicator like phenolphthalein or starch.
Key relation MnO4− (purple) + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ (colourless) + 4H2O The purple colour is destroyed as long as any reducing agent remains, and returns at the first drop of excess KMnO4.
Solution
1. KMnO4 is taken in the burette; the reducing agent (Fe2+ or C2O42−) sits in the flask and is pale or colourless.
2. Each added drop of purple MnO4− is reduced to colourless Mn2+, so the colour disappears while reductant is left.
3. At the end point every bit of reductant is used up, so the next drop of KMnO4 is not consumed. Nothing is left to decolourise it.
4. That tiny excess tints the whole flask a permanent pale pink — the signal of the end point.
5. The reagent thus marks its own end point, so it acts as a self indicator; no phenolphthalein or starch is added.
6. This works only in acidic medium, where MnO4− is reduced to colourless Mn2+; in neutral/basic medium it gives brown MnO2 and the sharp colour change is lost. So dilute H2SO4 is added, not HCl or HNO3.

       indicator
⚡ JEE Trick A faint pink that lasts about 30 s is the true end point; a deep pink means you overshot. Only intensely coloured reagents (KMnO4, I2) can serve as self indicators — a colourless oxidant could not.

Concept An indicator is a substance that signals the end point of a titration by a sharp, visible colour change. It is the tool that tells the experimenter exactly when to stop adding titrant. The trap is confusing the broad word “indicator” with the special case “self indicator”.
Key relation end point ≡ the drop at which the indicator changes colour A good indicator changes colour abruptly very close to the equivalence point, so the colour flip and the stoichiometric point nearly coincide.
Solution
1. In a titration one solution is added from a burette until the reaction is just complete — the equivalence point.
2. The eye cannot see that the reaction is complete, so a helper substance is used to make it visible.
3. This substance shows a distinct colour change at (or very near) the equivalence point. That observed colour change is the end point.
4. Such a colour-signalling substance is called an indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein, methyl orange, starch).
5. When the titrant itself provides this colour change, it is the special case called a self indicator; otherwise an external indicator is added.
6. A good indicator must give a sharp, clear change and be sensitive right at the equivalence point, so that the end-point error stays negligible. Examples: phenolphthalein (acid–base), methyl orange (acid–base), starch (iodometry), K2CrO4 (argentometry).

 ------------------------------------------

   = INDICATOR
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the pair: “equivalence point” is theoretical (stoichiometry), “end point” is what you see (indicator). A well-chosen indicator makes the two almost identical, keeping the titration error small.

Concept In a titration the solution of known concentration is delivered from the burette (the titrant) onto the solution being analysed in the flask (the analyte, or titrand). Keeping the two straight is essential for writing the molarity-equation correctly. The trap is swapping which vessel holds which.
Key relation burette → titrant  |  flask → analyte (titrand) The titrant is added in measured volume; the analyte is the unknown whose amount we want.
Solution
1. A burette lets you add a liquid drop by drop and read its volume accurately.
2. The solution placed in the burette is of known strength and is added controllably — it is the titrant.
3. The solution placed in the titration (conical) flask is the one being tested — it is the analyte (also called titrand).
4. The titrant reacts with the analyte until the indicator marks the end point. At that point equivalents of titrant = equivalents of analyte.
5. From the burette volume and the titrant’s known concentration, the analyte’s amount is calculated.
6. For example, standard KMnO4 in the burette (titrant) is run into an oxalate solution in the flask (analyte); the burette reading at the pink end point gives the oxalate content. The known always goes in the burette, the unknown in the flask.

 flask    : unknown soln held  -> ANALYTE
 ------------------------------------------
 at end point: eq(titrant) = eq(analyte)
⚡ JEE Trick Mnemonic: the titrant is in the burette (it is the one you add). Whatever waits in the flask is the analyte. Mixing them up flips the known/unknown and ruins the molarity-equation N1V1 = N2V2.
Answer: titrant (burette), analyte (flask).
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: In electrochemical cell .......... is negative and ............. is positive.Redox titration & galvanic cellGalvanic Cell Or Voltaic Cell | Salt Bridge | Animation | Chemistry Ask🟡 Similaranode, cathode
Concept An electrochemical (galvanic / voltaic) cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy by running a spontaneous redox reaction in two separated half-cells. In it the anode is the negative terminal and the cathode is the positive terminal, because that is where electrons are produced and consumed. The trap is carrying over the electrolytic-cell convention, where an external battery forces the reaction and the signs are reversed.
Key relation galvanic cell: anode = − (oxidation), cathode = + (reduction) Electrons are released by oxidation at the anode, making it electron-rich (negative), and flow to the cathode.
Solution
1. A galvanic cell runs a spontaneous redox reaction and pushes electrons through the external wire.
2. Oxidation happens at the anode, which produces electrons there. An electron source is a negative terminal.
3. So the anode is negative in a galvanic cell.
4. Electrons travel through the wire to the other electrode, where reduction occurs — the cathode.
5. The cathode draws electrons and is electron-deficient, so the cathode is positive.
6. In the standard Daniell cell, the Zn electrode (Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−) is the negative anode and the Cu electrode (Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu) is the positive cathode. Conventional current flows cathode→anode outside, opposite to the electron flow.
 anode  : oxidation, gives e- -> NEGATIVE
 cathode: reduction, takes e- -> POSITIVE
 ------------------------------------------
 e- flow (wire): anode(-) --> cathode(+)
⚡ JEE Trick Signs flip between cell types: galvanic → anode (−), cathode (+); electrolytic → anode (+), cathode (−). But the function is fixed everywhere: anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction (“An-Ox, Red-Cat”).
Answer: anode is negative, cathode is positive.

1 (vi)Fill in the blank: Salt bridge maintains ............ in the solutions of two half cells.Redox titration & galvanic cellGalvanic Cell Or Voltaic Cell | Salt Bridge | Animation | Chemistry Ask🟡 Similarelectrical neutrality
Concept As a galvanic cell runs, ions build up in each half-cell and would stop the current unless the charge imbalance is cancelled. The salt bridge supplies ions that maintain electrical neutrality in the two half-cell solutions. The trap is to think its job is to carry electrons — it carries ions, not electrons.
Key relation anode soln gains + charge (Mn+), cathode soln loses + charge The bridge feeds anions toward the anode side and cations toward the cathode side to balance both.
Solution
1. At the anode metal dissolves (M → Mn+ + ne−), so positive ions accumulate there.
2. At the cathode cations are deposited, so the solution there becomes deficient in positive charge.
3. These growing charge imbalances would oppose electron flow and halt the cell. Charge build-up blocks the reaction.
4. The salt bridge releases anions into the anode compartment and cations into the cathode compartment.
5. This exactly cancels the surplus and deficit, keeping both solutions electrically neutral so current keeps flowing.
6. The bridge also completes the internal circuit, so the salt bridge has two linked roles: maintain electrical neutrality and close the circuit. Without a closed loop of charge, no steady current can flow.

 ------------------------------------------

   maintains ELECTRICAL NEUTRALITY
⚡ JEE Trick Two jobs of a salt bridge: (1) complete the internal circuit, (2) keep each half-cell neutral. Remove it and the cell dies almost at once — not from lack of wire, but from charge build-up.
Answer: electrical neutrality.

Concept A salt bridge completes the internal circuit of a galvanic cell by letting ions migrate between the two half-cells. It conducts by the movement of ions, not electrons; electrons travel only through the external metallic wire. The trap is to say it allows electrons to pass — an electrolyte solution conducts by ions, while a metal conducts by free electrons.
Key relation internal conduction = movement of ions; external conduction = movement of electrons The bridge holds an inert electrolyte (e.g. KCl, KNO3) whose free ions drift to balance charge.
Solution
1. The salt bridge is a tube of inert electrolyte set in agar jelly connecting the two half-cells.
2. As charge imbalance develops, its ions move: anions toward the anode, cations toward the cathode.
3. This flow of ions carries charge through the solution and completes the circuit inside the cell. Current must flow in a closed loop.
4. Electrons never pass through the bridge; they go only through the external metallic wire.
5. Hence a salt bridge allows the movement of ions through it.
6. The agar jelly stops the two solutions from mixing by bulk flow, yet its dissolved ions are free to migrate and carry the charge. So it isolates the half-cells chemically while connecting them electrically.

Concept In any cell the electrode where oxidation occurs is the anode and the electrode where reduction occurs is the cathode — this definition is fixed regardless of cell type. In a galvanic cell oxidation is at the anode and reduction is at the cathode. The trap is tying anode/cathode to the + / − signs, which do flip between cell types.
Key relation anode ≡ oxidation (loss of e−); cathode ≡ reduction (gain of e−) These names are defined by the reaction, not by the sign of the terminal.
Solution
1. By definition, the electrode at which oxidation takes place is named the anode.
2. The electrode at which reduction takes place is named the cathode.
3. In a galvanic cell the spontaneous reaction oxidises the anode metal, releasing electrons. So oxidation is at the anode.
4. Those electrons travel through the wire and reduce the species at the other electrode, the cathode.
5. Hence oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode.
6. In the Daniell cell this is literal: Zn → Zn2+ + 2e− (oxidation) at the Zn anode and Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu (reduction) at the Cu cathode. The two half-reactions add to the spontaneous overall cell reaction.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise “An-Ox, Red-Cat”: Anode = Oxidation, Reduction = Cathode. This holds for both galvanic and electrolytic cells — only the + / − signs of the electrodes swap between them.

Key relation galvanic: chemical energy → electrical energy (ΔG < 0, E°cell > 0) A spontaneous redox reaction (negative ΔG) does electrical work on the external circuit.
