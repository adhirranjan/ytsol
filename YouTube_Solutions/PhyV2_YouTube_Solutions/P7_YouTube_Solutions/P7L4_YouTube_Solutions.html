1A 10 kg block rests on a rough floor (μs = 0.5). A force F pulling at 37° above the horizontal is gradually increased from zero. Determine whether the block will first slide or lift up. (Level-1)Pull at an angle Force Applied at an Angle with Friction 🟡 Similar Fslide ≈ 45.5 N < Flift ≈ 166.7 N — the block SLIDES first
Concept A slanted pull does two things at once: its horizontal part tries to slide the block, its vertical part unloads the floor. Whichever threshold F reaches first as F grows from zero is what actually happens.
Formula Sliding: F cosθ = μ(mg − F sinθ); Lifting: F sinθ = mg (N → 0). Both are threshold conditions on the same single unknown F, so they can be compared directly.
Solution
1. θ = 37°, sin37° = 0.6, cos37° = 0.8, mg = 10 × 10 = 100 N.
2. Sliding threshold: F(0.8) = 0.5(100 − 0.6F) → 0.8F + 0.3F = 50 → Fslide = 50/1.1 ≈ 45.5 N.
3. Lifting threshold: F(0.6) = 100 → Flift ≈ 166.7 N.Lift-off needs the vertical component alone to carry the whole weight — a much bigger demand.
4. 45.5 N < 166.7 N, so as F is raised from zero the sliding condition is met long before lift-off.

       /  37 deg
      /
  [ 10 kg ]  <-- f = mu*N (backwards)
  ///////// N = mg - F sin37

Answer: Fslide ≈ 45.5 N is reached before Flift ≈ 166.7 N — the block will first slide.

2The angle of repose is defined as: (a) The angle at which friction is maximum (b) The angle at which object sticks to the plane (c) The angle at which the object starts sliding (d) The angle between normal and frictional forceAngle of repose Angle of Repose — Friction | Class 11 Physics JEE and NEET 🟡 Similar cthe angle at which the object starts sliding
Concept Tilt an inclined plane slowly with a body on it. Up to a certain inclination static friction can balance the down-slope pull; beyond it the body slides. That critical inclination is the angle of repose.
Key relation mg sinα = μ mg cosα ⟹ tanα = μ
Solution
1. Below α the body is in equilibrium (static friction < limiting).
2. At α the driving force exactly equals limiting friction — sliding is just about to begin.This is why (a) is also loosely "true" at α, but the definition is the onset of sliding.
3. Above α the body accelerates down.
⚡ JEE Trick α does not depend on mass — a 1 g grain and a 100 kg crate of the same material slide at the same tilt. Trap: option (b), which describes every angle below α.
Answer: (c) the angle at which the object starts sliding.
3At the angle of repose α, which of the following is true? (a) Limiting friction = 0 (b) Driving force = 0 (c) Limiting friction = Driving force (d) Frictional force > Driving forceAngle of repose condition Class 11 chap 5 | Friction Force 02 | Angle Of Repose | Friction on Inclined Plane 🟡 Similar cLimiting friction = Driving force
Concept The angle of repose is a knife-edge equilibrium: the block is still at rest, but friction is at its ceiling.
Key relation mg sinα = μ mg cosα = fL
Solution
1. Driving force down the incline = mg sinα ≠ 0, so (b) is out.
2. Limiting friction fL = μN = μmg cosα ≠ 0, so (a) is out.
3. Friction can never exceed what is needed for equilibrium of a static body, so (d) is impossible.
4. Equilibrium at the threshold ⟹ fL = mg sinα exactly.
⚡ JEE Trick Static friction is a "reaction", never a fixed number: it equals whatever the driving force is, up to its μN ceiling. Trap: (d) — friction bigger than the applied force would accelerate the block uphill.
Answer: (c) Limiting friction = Driving force.

5Which of the following statements is true when θ < α (inclination less than the angle of repose)? Concept Below the angle of repose the incline is "safe": the block's tendency to slide is smaller than the friction the surface can supply, so it stays put with friction below its ceiling.
Key relation Driving force = mg sinθ; limiting friction = μ mg cosθ = mg tanα cosθ
Solution
1. fL/(driving) = μ mg cosθ/(mg sinθ) = tanα/tanθ.

⚡ JEE Trick The actual friction here is only mg sinθ (less than the ceiling). "Limiting friction" is a capacity, not the force acting. Trap: mixing the two up.
Answer: (c) Limiting friction > Driving force.

If the answer is xμmg/a, then x = ?Time-varying force, slip onset 2 Block Problems | Friction | JEE Physics 🟡 Similar x = 2 (t = 2μmg/a)
Concept As long as they move together, the friction on the upper block grows in step with F. Slipping starts the instant that demanded friction hits μmg.
Formula asys = at/2m, f = m·asys = at/2, slip when f = μmg
Solution
1. Total mass = 2m, smooth floor ⟹ common acceleration = at/(2m).
2. Upper block's only horizontal force is friction: f = m × at/(2m) = at/2.Half the applied force is "spent" on the upper block because the masses are equal.
3. Slipping begins when at/2 = μmg ⟹ t = 2μmg/a.
4. Comparing with xμmg/a gives x = 2.
⚡ JEE Trick For unequal masses the factor becomes (m1+m2)μg/(a) × (mupper/mupper)… simply: t = μg(m1+m2)/a when the upper block is the passenger. Here (m+m)/1 gives the 2. Trap: setting at = μmg (forgetting the factor of two).
Answer: t = 2μmg/a, so x = 2
2A block of mass 2 kg is pushed against a rough vertical wall with a force of 40 N, the coefficient of static friction being 0.5. Another horizontal force of 15 N is applied on the block in a direction parallel to the wall. Will the block move? If yes, in which direction? If no, find the frictional force exerted by the wall on the block.Friction on a vertical wall HC Verma Chapter 6 Solution 29 | Friction | Class 11 Physics 🟢 Exact Yes — it moves at 53° to the 15 N force (resultant 25 N > fmax = 20 N)
Concept The wall's normal force is the 40 N push, so the friction ceiling is μ × 40. In the plane of the wall two forces act — weight (down) and 15 N (horizontal) — and friction must fight their resultant.
Formula fmax = μN, resultant in-plane force R = √(W² + F²)
Solution
1. N = 40 N ⟹ fmax = 0.5 × 40 = 20 N.
2. In-plane forces: weight = 2 × 10 = 20 N downward, and 15 N horizontal.
3. Resultant R = √(20² + 15²) = √625 = 25 N.These are perpendicular in the wall plane, so combine them as a 3-4-5 triangle.
4. 25 N > 20 N ⟹ the block does move; friction (20 N) can only partially oppose it, leaving a net 5 N.
5. Direction: along R, i.e. at tan⁻¹(20/15) = 53° from the 15 N force (equivalently 37° from the vertical).
  Wall plane (looking at the wall):
        15 N ------>
             |
        20 N |  (weight)
             v      R = 25 N at 53 deg below the 15 N direction
⚡ JEE Trick Friction on a wall is two-dimensional — always combine weight and the sideways push before comparing with μN. Trap: comparing 15 N alone with 20 N and concluding "it does not move".
Answer: Yes, the block moves — at 53° with the 15 N force (net force 25 − 20 = 5 N in that direction).
3A person (40 kg) manages to stay at rest between two vertical walls by pressing one wall A with his hands and feet and the other wall B with his back. The friction coefficient between his body and the walls is 0.8 and limiting friction acts at all contacts. (a) Show that the person presses the two walls with equal force. (b) Find the normal force exerted by either wall on the person. Take g = 10 m/s².Body wedged between two walls A person (40 kg) managing to be at rest between two vertical walls 🟢 Exact (a) proved by horizontal equilibrium; (b) N = 250 N
Concept Horizontally the two normal forces are the only forces, so they must be equal and opposite. Vertically, two friction forces (one per wall) together hold up the weight.
Formula Horizontal: NA = NB = N; Vertical: 2μN = mg
Solution
1. (a) The person is in equilibrium and the only horizontal forces are NA (from A) and NB (from B), acting oppositely ⟹ NA = NB. By Newton's third law he presses each wall with that same force.
2. (b) Limiting friction at each wall = μN, and both act upward.The person tends to slide down at both contacts, so friction is upward at both.
3. 2μN = mg ⟹ 2(0.8)N = 40 × 10 = 400.
4. N = 400/1.6 = 250 N.
  Wall A |            | Wall B
     N-> | [ person ] | <-N
     f up|            | f up      2*mu*N = mg = 400 N
              mg down             N = 250 N
⚡ JEE Trick Two contacts ⟹ factor of 2 in the friction equation; the answer is N = mg/(2μ). Trap: writing μN = mg and getting 500 N.
Answer: NA = NB = 250 N
4A small block of mass m is kept at the left end of a larger block of mass M and length ℓ. The system can slide on a horizontal road and is started towards the right with initial velocity v. The friction coefficient between the road and the bigger block is μ, and that between the two blocks is μ/2. Find the time elapsed before the smaller block separates from the bigger block.Relative sliding on a moving block Figure (6-E12): a small block of mass m kept at the left end of a larger block 🟢 Exact t = √[4Mℓ / ((M+m)μg)]
Concept Both blocks decelerate, but the big block decelerates faster (it fights road friction as well). The small block therefore creeps forward relative to it and eventually reaches the right end — a relative-motion problem.
Formula arel = am − aM, ℓ = ½arelt²
Solution
1. Small block: only friction from M acts, deceleration = (μ/2)g.
2. Big block: road friction μ(M+m)g backward, plus the reaction (μ/2)mg forward from m.The road presses on M with the combined weight, so its normal force is (M+m)g.
3. aM = −[μ(M+m)g − (μ/2)mg]/M = −μg(2M+m)/(2M).
4. Relative acceleration of m w.r.t. M = −μg/2 + μg(2M+m)/(2M) = μg(M+m)/(2M), directed forward.
5. Starting from zero relative velocity, ℓ = ½ · μg(M+m)/(2M) · t² ⟹ t = √[4Mℓ/((M+m)μg)].
   [m]________
   [    M     ] -> v
  ////////////   road, mu
  a_rel = mu*g*(M+m)/(2M)  forward for m
  l = 0.5 * a_rel * t^2
⚡ JEE Trick Never track absolute positions here — one relative-acceleration equation replaces two kinematics chains. Trap: forgetting that the road's normal force on M includes m's weight.
Answer: t = √[4Mℓ / (μ(M+m)g)]
5A block of mass m slips on a rough horizontal table under the action of a horizontal force applied to it. The coefficient of friction between the block and the table is μ. The table does not move on the floor. Find the total frictional force applied by the floor on the legs of the table. Do you need the friction coefficient between the table and the floor, or the mass of the table?Friction on the table legs A block of mass m slips on a rough horizontal table under a horizontal force 🟢 Exact μmg; neither the table–floor μ nor the table's mass is needed
Concept Treat the table as a body in equilibrium. The block drags its top surface forward; the floor must supply exactly that much friction backward at the legs.
Key relation Table equilibrium: flegs = fblock on table = μmg
Solution
1. The block slips, so kinetic friction on the block from the table = μmg, backward on the block.
2. By Newton's third law the block exerts μmg forward on the table top.
3. The table stays at rest ⟹ its horizontal forces balance ⟹ the floor supplies μmg backward at the legs.Their vertical weights and the floor's μ only decide whether the table can stay put, not how much friction it actually experiences.
4. Hence total leg friction = μmg, and the table's mass and the table–floor μ are not required.
       F --> [ m ]  slips, f = mu*m*g on block (backward)
      ==================  table top
      ||              ||   f_legs = mu*m*g (backward, from floor)
     ////////////////////
⚡ JEE Trick Static friction is a "whatever it takes" force — you compute it from equilibrium, never from μfloorN. Trap: computing μfloor(M+m)g, which is only the ceiling.
Answer: total friction on the legs = μmg (directed opposite to the block's motion). No, neither the table–floor coefficient nor the table's mass is needed.
6A block of mass M is kept on a rough horizontal surface with coefficient of static friction μ. The block is to be pulled by applying a force to it. What minimum force is needed to slide the block, and in which direction should this force act?Minimum force to slide A block of mass m on a rough horizontal surface — minimum force to pull it 🟢 Exact Fmin = μMg/√(1+μ²), at angle tan⁻¹μ above the horizontal
Concept Pulling at an angle helps twice over: the horizontal component drives the block while the vertical component lightens it, reducing friction. There is an optimum tilt.
Formula F = μMg/(cosθ + μ sinθ), minimised when cosθ + μ sinθ is maximum
Solution
1. Pull F at angle θ above the horizontal. Normal force N = Mg − F sinθ.
2. Threshold of sliding: F cosθ = μ(Mg − F sinθ) ⟹ F = μMg/(cosθ + μ sinθ).
3. cosθ + μ sinθ = √(1+μ²)·cos(θ − tan⁻¹μ), whose maximum √(1+μ²) occurs at θ = tan⁻¹μ.Write a cosθ + b sinθ in the amplitude form √(a²+b²) cos(θ − δ) — no calculus needed.
4. Fmin = μMg/√(1+μ²), applied at θ = tan⁻¹μ = the angle of friction.
⚡ JEE Trick The optimum pull angle is exactly the angle of friction — a fact worth memorising. Also Fmin = Mg sinφ where tanφ = μ. Trap: assuming the best pull is horizontal (which gives the larger μMg).
Answer: Fmin = μMg/√(1+μ²), directed at tan⁻¹μ above the horizontal.
7The friction coefficient between the board (mass m, on the floor) and the floor is μ. A man of mass M stands on the board and pulls a rope passing over pulleys attached to the board. Find the maximum force the man can exert on the rope so that the board does not slip on the floor.Man on a board pulling a rope The friction coefficient between the board and the floor is μ — find the maximum force 🟢 Exact Fmax = μ(M+m)g / (1+μ)
Concept Pulling the rope does two competing things: it drags the board forward with the rope tension, and it also lifts part of the man's weight off the board, reducing the normal force on the floor — so the friction available shrinks as F grows.
Formula Normal on floor N = (M+m)g − F; no slip requires F ≤ μN
Solution
1. Take man + board + pulleys as one system. The rope leaves upward, so the external rope force on the system is F upward.The rope's other end is fixed to the ceiling/wall, so the tension F pulls the system up.
2. Vertical: N = (M+m)g − F.
3. Horizontal: the net horizontal pull tending to slide the board is F, resisted by friction f ≤ μN.
4. F ≤ μ[(M+m)g − F] ⟹ F(1+μ) ≤ μ(M+m)g ⟹ Fmax = μ(M+m)g/(1+μ).
⚡ JEE Trick The (1 + μ) in the denominator is the signature of "the applied force also reduces N". Trap: writing Fmax = μ(M+m)g and forgetting the unloading effect.
Answer: Fmax = μ(M+m)g/(1+μ)
8A 2 kg block is placed over a 4 kg block and both are placed on a smooth horizontal surface. The coefficient of friction between the blocks is 0.20. Find the acceleration of the two blocks if a horizontal force of 12 N is applied to (a) the upper block, (b) the lower block. Take g = 10 m/s².Two-block system, both cases A 2 kg block is placed over a 4 kg block on a smooth horizontal surface 🟢 Exact (a) upper 4 m/s², lower 1 m/s²; (b) both 2 m/s²
Concept The same 12 N produces completely different behaviour depending on which block it is applied to, because the passenger block changes.
Formula fmax = μmupperg; check atogether = F/(m1+m2) against the passenger's ceiling
Solution
1. fmax = 0.20 × 2 × 10 = 4 N.
2. (a) F on the 2 kg block: together a = 12/6 = 2 m/s² would need friction 4 × 2 = 8 N on the lower block > 4 N ⟹ they slip.
3. Upper: a = (12 − 4)/2 = 4 m/s². Lower: a = 4/4 = 1 m/s².
4. (b) F on the 4 kg block: together a = 2 m/s² needs friction 2 × 2 = 4 N on the upper block = fmax exactly ⟹ just barely no slipping.This is the borderline case — friction is exactly at its ceiling but not exceeded.
5. Both blocks: 2 m/s².
  (a) 12 N --> [ 2 kg ]  a = 4 m/s^2
               [ 4 kg ]  a = 1 m/s^2      (slipping, f = 4 N)
  (b)          [ 2 kg ]  a = 2 m/s^2
      12 N --> [ 4 kg ]  a = 2 m/s^2      (together, f = 4 N exactly)
⚡ JEE Trick Pushing the lighter top block is far more likely to cause slipping, because friction then has to drag the heavier partner. Trap: giving 2 m/s² for both in part (a).
Answer: (a) upper block 4 m/s², lower block 1 m/s²; (b) both blocks 2 m/s².
9A block m rests on a block M on a smooth floor; the friction coefficient between the two blocks is μ. A string attached to m runs horizontally to the right over a fixed pulley and its other end is attached to M. A horizontal force F is applied to m towards the left. (a) What maximum F can be applied without disturbing the equilibrium of the system? (b) If the applied force is double that value, find the accelerations of the two masses.Blocks linked by a string over a fixed pulley The friction coefficient between the two blocks shown in figure is μ but the floor is smooth 🟢 Exact (a) Fmax = 2μmg; (b) each block 2μmg/(M+m), in opposite directions
Concept The string over a fixed pulley couples the two blocks so that if m moves left by x, M must move right by x — equal speeds, opposite directions. Friction acts at the single m–M interface.
Formula Equilibrium: F = T + μmg and T = μmg; motion: F − 2μmg = (M+m)a
Solution
1. (a) At the verge, friction on m is limiting: μmg. Block M is held by tension T against friction μmg from m ⟹ T = μmg.
2. Block m: F = T + μmg = 2μmg. So Fmax = 2μmg.F has to beat friction twice — once directly, once through the string that holds M.
3. (b) With F = 4μmg the blocks slide. m (mass m, leftward) : F − T − μmg = ma. M (mass M, rightward): T − μmg = Ma.The constraint forces equal magnitudes a for both.
4. Add: F − 2μmg = (M+m)a ⟹ a = (4μmg − 2μmg)/(M+m) = 2μmg/(M+m).
   F <-- [ m ]-----string-----(O) fixed pulley
         [ M ]-------------------+
  //////////// smooth floor
   m moves LEFT, M moves RIGHT, same |a|
⚡ JEE Trick With a fixed pulley the string makes the two blocks mirror each other, so one combined equation F − 2μmg = (M+m)a does the whole job. Trap: assuming they move together in the same direction.
Answer: (a) Fmax = 2μmg; (b) a = 2μmg/(M+m) for each block, in opposite directions.
10Suppose the entire system of the previous question is kept inside an elevator coming down with an acceleration a (a < g). Repeat parts (a) and (b).Friction in an accelerating frame The friction coefficient between the two blocks shown in figure is μ but the floor is smooth 🟡 Similar (a) Fmax = 2μm(g−a); (b) each block 2μm(g−a)/(m+M), in opposite directions
Concept Inside a lift accelerating downward with a, the effective gravity is geff = g − a. Every friction force shrinks accordingly; the horizontal dynamics is otherwise unchanged.
Formula Replace g → (g − a) everywhere the normal force appears: fmax = μm(g−a)
Solution
1. In the lift frame add a pseudo-force ma upward on each body ⟹ effective weight m(g−a).The lift accelerates downward, so the pseudo-force is upward, reducing the apparent weight.
2. Normal force at the m–M interface becomes m(g−a), so fmax = μm(g−a).
3. (a) Repeating part (a): Fmax = 2μm(g−a).
4. (b) With F = 4μm(g−a): F − 2μm(g−a) = (M+m)a′ ⟹ a′ = 2μm(g−a)/(m+M), the two blocks moving oppositely as before.
⚡ JEE Trick Free fall (a = g) gives Fmax = 0 — no normal force, no friction, nothing to hold. That limiting check confirms the (g − a) factor. Trap: writing (g + a), which is the case for a lift accelerating upward.
Answer: (a) Fmax = 2μm(g − a); (b) a′ = 2μm(g − a)/(m + M) for each block, in opposite directions.
