1Natural isotopic abundance of ¹⁰B is 19.60% and ¹¹B is 80.40%; exact masses 10.013 u and 11.009 u. Calculate the average atomic mass of boron.Average atomic mass (B)Average atomic mass of boron (10.013 / 11.009)🟢 Exact—
Concept Naturally occurring boron is a mixture of two isotopes, ¹⁰B and ¹¹B, so the atomic mass printed on the periodic table is not the mass of any single atom — it is the abundance-weighted average of the isotopic masses. Each isotope contributes in proportion to its fractional abundance. The trap is taking a simple (unweighted) mean of the two masses, which ignores the fact that ¹¹B atoms are about four times as common as ¹⁰B atoms.
Formula Mavg = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass) = (%₁ × m₁ + %₂ × m₂) ÷ 100. Each atom "votes" with its own mass; the abundance is its voting weight, and the weights must sum to 1 (i.e. 100%). Data: ¹⁰B — 19.60%, 10.013 u; ¹¹B — 80.40%, 11.009 u.
Solution
1. Check the data are complete: 19.60% + 80.40% = 100.00%, so only these two isotopes need be considered.
2. Convert percents to fractional abundances: f(¹⁰B) = 19.60 ÷ 100 = 0.1960; f(¹¹B) = 80.40 ÷ 100 = 0.8040.
3. Contribution of ¹⁰B = 0.1960 × 10.013 = 1.9625 u.Multiply each isotopic mass by its own fraction, not by the other's.
4. Contribution of ¹¹B = 0.8040 × 11.009 = 8.8512 u.
5. Add the contributions: Mavg = 1.9625 + 8.8512 = 10.8138 u ≈ 10.81 u.
6. Sanity check: 10.81 lies between 10.013 and 11.009 and sits much closer to 11.009 — exactly as expected, since ¹¹B is ~80% of all boron atoms.
 isotope   mass (u)   fraction   f × m (u)
 ¹⁰B       10.013     0.1960     1.9625
 ¹¹B       11.009     0.8040     8.8512
 ─────────────────────────────────────────
 total                1.0000     10.8138 ≈ 10.81 u   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick The weighted average must land between the two isotopic masses, pulled toward the more abundant one — here ~80% of the way from 10 to 11, so expect ≈ 10.8 before touching the calculator. Trap: the simple mean (10.013 + 11.009)/2 = 10.511 is wrong, and forgetting the ÷100 when using percents directly inflates the answer 100-fold.
Answer: average atomic mass of boron = 10.8138 u ≈ 10.81 u.

3Calculate the number of moles and molecules of acetic acid present in 22 g of it.Moles & moleculesMoles & molecules of acetic acid in 22 g🟢 Exact—
Concept A classic two-rung mole-concept ladder: grams → moles → molecules. The molar mass converts the measured mass into moles, and Avogadro's number converts moles into a literal count of molecules. Acetic acid is CH₃COOH, i.e. molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ — the trap is miscounting its hydrogens (it has 4 H: three in CH₃ and one in COOH) and getting the wrong molar mass.
Formula n = m ÷ M and N = n × NA, with NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. One mole of any substance contains exactly NA particles, so the molecule count is just moles scaled by Avogadro's number. Atomic masses: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16 u.
Solution
1. Molar mass of CH₃COOH (C₂H₄O₂): M = 2×12 + 4×1 + 2×16 = 24 + 4 + 32 = 60 g/mol.
2. Moles: n = m ÷ M = 22 ÷ 60 = 11/30 = 0.3667 mol ≈ 0.367 mol.22 g is a bit more than a third of one mole (60 g), so n just above 0.33 is reasonable.
3. Molecules: N = n × NA = 0.3667 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.208 × 10²³ ≈ 2.21 × 10²³ molecules.
4. Cross-check the order of magnitude: about one-third of Avogadro's number (6.022 ÷ 3 ≈ 2.0 × 10²³) — consistent.
 given mass        22 g CH₃COOH

                   = 2.21 × 10²³ molecules      <-- answer (molecules)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise M(acetic acid) = 60 g/mol — it appears constantly; then 22/60 = 11/30 keeps the fraction exact until the last step. Trap: counting only 3 H in CH₃COOH (M = 59) or confusing 22 g of acetic acid with 22 g of CO₂ (M = 44, n = 0.5 mol) — the "22 g" is deliberate bait for the half-mole reflex.
Answer: n = 22/60 ≈ 0.367 mol, containing ≈ 2.21 × 10²³ molecules of acetic acid.
424 g of carbon reacts with some oxygen to make 88 g of carbon dioxide. Find out how much oxygen must have been used.Law of conservation of massConservation of mass🟡 Similar—
Concept The law of conservation of mass states that in any chemical reaction the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products — atoms are only rearranged, never created or destroyed. Here carbon and oxygen combine into carbon dioxide (C + O₂ → CO₂), so the mass of the CO₂ formed must equal the mass of carbon plus the mass of oxygen that reacted. Knowing two of the three masses fixes the third by simple subtraction.
Key relation mass(reactants) = mass(products), i.e. mass(C) + mass(O₂) = mass(CO₂). Because no mass is lost, the oxygen consumed is just the product mass minus the carbon mass.
Solution
1. Write the mass balance for C + O₂ → CO₂: mass(C) + mass(O₂) = mass(CO₂).
2. Insert the known masses: 24 + mass(O₂) = 88.
3. Rearrange for the unknown: mass(O₂) = 88 − 24.
4. Subtract: 88 − 24 = 64 g of oxygen.The 64 g is exactly the O₂ that combined with the carbon — nothing is unaccounted for.
5. Cross-check by chemistry: 24 g C = 2 mol C; each needs 1 mol O₂ (32 g), so 2 mol O₂ = 64 g ✓.
 reactant/product   mass
 carbon (C)         24 g
 oxygen (O₂)        ?  = 88 − 24 = 64 g
 carbon dioxide     88 g   (must equal 24 + 64)
⚡ JEE Trick "Find the missing mass" problems are pure conservation of mass — total in = total out, so subtract the known reactant from the product. Trap: don't get pulled into molar-mass arithmetic unless asked; the balance 88 − 24 = 64 g answers it directly (though the mole check confirms it).
Key point: by conservation of mass, mass(O₂) = mass(CO₂) − mass(C) = 88 − 24 = 64 g of oxygen.
