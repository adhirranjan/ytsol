1What is the expression for mass per unit length (λ) of a uniform rod of mass M and length L? (a) λ = M×L (b) λ = L/M (c) λ = M/L (d) λ = 1/(ML)Linear mass densityCentre of Mass of a Uniform Rod | Class 11 Physics Derivation | NEET • JEE🟡 Similarcλ = M/L
Concept "Per unit length" is a rate: it answers "how many kilograms sit on every one metre of this rod?" A rate is always the quantity you are distributing divided by the extent you distribute it over, so mass must sit on top and length underneath. The obvious wrong instinct is to multiply M and L because both symbols appear in the problem — but M×L grows when you make the rod longer, whereas the same rod cut longer at fixed thickness must keep the SAME kg per metre. Only division produces a quantity that is a property of the material and cross-section, not of how much of it you happen to have.
Formula λ = M/L, so that dm = λdx and ∫dm = λL = M. λ is defined precisely so that multiplying it back by a length returns the mass of that length — this is the identity every integration problem in this lecture leans on.
Solution
1. Definition applied directly: λ = (total mass)/(total length) = M/L → option (c).Uniform means λ is the same at every x, so the local rate equals the global average — no integration needed.
2. Dimensional check: [λ] = [M]/[L] = ML−1, SI unit kg m−1 = kg/m; option (a) gives kg·m, (b) gives m/kg, (d) gives kg−1m−1 — three different units, so only (c) can be a linear density.Dimensional screening kills 3 of 4 options in five seconds; do this before any algebra.
3. Consistency test: mass of an element dx must be dm = λdx = (M/L)dx, and ∫0L(M/L)dx = (M/L)×L = M ✓.If you had used λ = ML you would get ∫MLdx = ML2 ≠ M — the definition would contradict itself.
4. Numerical sanity: a 12 kg rod of length 6 m gives λ = 12/6 = 2 kg/m, so 1 m weighs 2 kg and the whole 6 m weighs 6×2 = 12 kg ✓.The same λ = 2 kg/m is reused in Suggested Problem 3 to weigh a 2 m piece.
5. Scaling argument: cut the rod in half → M → M/2 and L → L/2, so λ = (M/2)/(L/2) = M/L, unchanged.An intensive property must survive cutting; M×L would fall to ML/4, which is physically absurd.
6. Why it matters downstream: xcm = ∫x dm/∫dm = ∫0Lx(M/L)dx / M = (M/L)(L2/2)/M = L/2, the familiar midpoint.Getting λ wrong wrecks every continuous-body COM integral that follows.

2A rod has mass 10 kg and length 4 m. What is its mass per unit length? (a) 0.4 kg/m (b) 2.5 kg/m (c) 4 kg/m (d) 10 kg/mLinear density numericThe linear mass density i.e. mass per unit length of a rod of length L is given by🟡 Similarb2.5 kg/m
Concept This is Suggested Problem 1 with numbers attached: the rod is uniform, so every metre of it carries the same mass, and that common value is the total mass shared equally among the total number of metres. The tempting error is to divide the wrong way round — 4/10 = 0.4 — which is exactly why option (a) is on the paper. Formula λ = M/L. Uniform rod ⇒ λ is constant along the rod, so the local mass-per-metre equals the global average M/L with no integration.

1. Substitute straight into the definition: λ = M/L = 10 kg / 4 m = 2.5 kg/m → option (b).Keep the units attached while dividing; kg ÷ m automatically produces kg/m and blocks the flipped division.

4A uniform thin rod of length 1.2 m is bent at its midpoint to form a right angle (90°). Find the distance of the centre of mass from the bending point.Bent rod COMA uniform rod of length one meter is bent at its midpoint to make 90°. The distance of centre🟡 Similar0.212 m
Concept Bending does not destroy mass, it only moves it: after the fold you have two straight rods of equal length and equal mass meeting at 90°. Each half can be replaced by a single point particle sitting at its own midpoint, and the system COM is then the ordinary two-particle result. The classic error is to imagine the COM still lies ON the metal — it does not. It floats in the empty square between the two arms, on the bisector of the right angle, which is why the answer is smaller than half an arm length would suggest along either axis alone.
Formula xcm = Σmixi/Σmi applied to two equal point masses, then d = √(xcm2 + ycm2). Each uniform arm is legitimately replaced by its full mass concentrated at its own centre — that substitution is exact for COM purposes.
Solution
1. Geometry after the fold: each arm has length ℓ = 1.2/2 = 0.6 m and mass m = M/2; put the bend at the origin with one arm along +x and the other along +y.Choosing the bending point as origin makes the required distance simply |rcm|, with no subtraction at the end.
2. Replace each arm by a point mass at its midpoint: arm 1 → m at (0.3, 0); arm 2 → m at (0, 0.3).Midpoint is correct only because each arm is uniform and straight — the bend is at an end of each arm, not inside it.
3. xcm = (m×0.3 + m×0)/(2m) = 0.3/2 = 0.15 m, and by the mirror symmetry of the two arms ycm = 0.15 m as well.Equal masses ⇒ the COM is the plain midpoint of the two representative points; the m's cancel, so M is never needed.

Concept The centre of mass is a mass-weighted average position, so it always drifts TOWARDS the heavier side of a system and therefore ends up farthest from the lightest corners. Here the two 2 kg masses R and S pull the COM to their edge of the square, leaving the two 1 kg masses P and Q as the remote pair. The trap is answering "R only" or "P only" — a single corner — when the mass pattern is symmetric about the perpendicular bisector of the square: whatever is true of P is true of Q by mirror symmetry, so the answer must name a PAIR.
Formula rcm = Σmiri/Σmi, then compare di = |ri − rcm|. Point masses need no integration; the COM is a pure weighted average, and distances are then ordinary Pythagoras.
Solution
1. Set coordinates around the square in order: P(0,0), Q(a,0), R(a,a), S(0,a) with masses 1, 1, 2, 2 kg; total M = 6 kg.Taking the corners in cyclic order keeps the two 2 kg masses adjacent, matching the figure — the heavy pair shares the side RS.
2. xcm = (1×0 + 1×a + 2×a + 2×0)/6 = 3a/6 = a/2.The x-coordinate lands exactly on the centre line because each vertical edge carries 1 + 2 = 3 kg — balanced left/right.

Each rod is uniform, so each collapses to a single point mass of the same size m at its own midpoint, and the frame's COM is then the plain average of those three midpoints. Solution
1. 2. Cone: m1 = ρπR2h/3 at y1 = +h/4, since a solid cone's centroid is one quarter of the height above the base.h/4 above the base, not h/3 — h/3 is the centroid of a triangular lamina, a classic mix-up in this very question.
3. Hemisphere: m2 = ρ(2/3)πR3 at y2 = −3R/8, measured from its flat face into the solid.
4. Balance: ρ(πR2h/3)(h/4) = ρ(2πR3/3)(3R/8) ⇒ πρR2h2/12 = πρR4/4, since (2/3)(3/8) = 1/4.
5. Cancel πρR2 on both sides: h2/12 = R2/4 ⇒ h2 = 3R2 ⇒ h = √3 R ≈ 1.73R.Both ρ and π drop out, confirming the result is a pure shape condition — if they survive, a volume formula was mis-written.
6. Consistency check on the masses: m1/m2 = (R2h/3)/(2R3/3) = h/2R = √3/2 ≈ 0.87, and 0.87×(h/4 = 0.433R) = 0.375R = 3R/8 exactly, so the moments do balance.
7. Sanity check on magnitude: the hemisphere is squat and reaches only 3R/8 below the join, so a slim cone would sit too high — h must exceed R but not enormously, and √3R sits right in that window while 3R and 2√3R are far too tall.

4A thin uniform sheet of metal of uniform thickness is cut into the shape bounded by the line x = a and y = ±kx2, as shown. Find the coordinates of the centre of mass. (a) (2/3)a (b) (3/4)a (c) (4/5)a (d) (5/6)aCOM by integration, parabolic laminaA thin sheet of metal of uniform thickness is cut into the shape bounded by the line x=a and....🟢 Exactb(3/4)a, ycm = 0
Concept This is a genuine continuous distribution, so the summation becomes an integral — but the right choice of element makes it a two-line problem. Slice the lamina into vertical strips at position x: every point of such a strip has the same x, so the strip may be treated as a point mass at x, and its own y-centroid is 0 by the up–down symmetry of y = ±kx2. The strip is not of constant height — it grows as x2 — which is why the answer is pushed well past the midpoint a/2 towards the wide end. A horizontal-strip slicing would work too but needs an inverse-square-root integral for no gain.
Formula xcm = ∫x dm / ∫dm with dm = σ(2kx2)dx for a vertical strip of height 2kx2 and width dx. Uniform thickness and density make σ constant, so mass is proportional to area and σ cancels between numerator and denominator.
Solution
1. At abscissa x the shape runs from y = −kx2 to y = +kx2, so the strip height is 2kx2 and dm = σ(2kx2)dx, with x running from 0 (the cusp at the origin) to a (the cutting line).The full height is 2kx2, not kx2 — the sheet is bounded by both branches; luckily the factor cancels, but the limits 0 to a must be right.
2. Denominator: ∫dm = ∫0a2σkx2dx = 2σk[x3/3]0a = 2σka3/3, which is just the total mass M.
3. Numerator: ∫x dm = ∫0ax(2σkx2)dx = 2σk[x4/4]0a = 2σka4/4 = σka4/2.
4. xcm = ∫x dm/∫dm = (2σka4/4)/(2σka3/3) = (a4/4)(3/a3) = 3a/4, and both σ and k cancel as they must.k dropping out is the tell that the answer cannot contain the parabola's steepness — any option with k in it would be wrong on inspection.
5. ycm = 0 by symmetry: for every element at +y there is an identical element at −y, so the whole centre of mass lies on the x-axis at (3a/4, 0).
6. Structural check: for a strip height proportional to xn the result is always xcm = a(n+1)/(n+2); here n = 2 gives 3a/4, and n = 0 (a rectangle) correctly returns a/2.
7. Sanity check: the sheet is narrow near the origin and widest at x = a, so the centre of mass must sit beyond a/2 but inside a — 0.75a sits exactly where a quadratic flare should put it.

Answer: (b) (3/4)a, with ycm = 0.
