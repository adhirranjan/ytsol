1What is the expression for work done by a variable force F(x) over a small displacement Δx? (a) F(x) + Δx (b) F(x)·Δx (c) Δx / F(x) (d) F(x)²·Δx — (Level-1)Elementary work ΔW = F·ΔxWork done by a variable force (Class 11 / JEE / NEET)🟡 SimilarbF(x)·Δx
Concept The whole variable-force machinery is built on one idea: over a displacement so small that the force has no time to change, the force behaves like a constant force. For a constant force along the motion, work is simply force × displacement. So the elementary work is ΔW = F(x)·Δx, and the total work is the sum (→ integral) of all such slivers. The "obvious" wrong move is to look for something fancier — but the correct expression is deliberately the simplest one, because all the complexity is pushed into the summation, not into the single term.
Formula Elementary work ΔW = F(x)·Δx — F(x) in newton (N), Δx in metre (m), ΔW in joule (J). Total work W = limΔx→0 Σ F(x)Δx = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx. Dimensionally N × m = kg·m·s⁻² × m = kg·m²·s⁻² = J ✓ — this is the only one of the four options that comes out in joules.
Solution
1. Take a strip of the F–x curve of width Δx, so narrow that F changes negligibly across it.This is exactly the assumption stated on the lecture's first page: "If the displacement Δx is small, we can take the force F(x) as approximately constant."
2. Over that strip the constant-force result applies: ΔW = F(x)·Δx — this is the area of the shaded rectangle in the book figure.
3. Test the other options by units: (a) F(x) + Δx adds N to m — illegal, you cannot add unlike quantities. (c) Δx/F(x) gives m/N = m²·s²·kg⁻¹, not joules. (d) F(x)²Δx gives N²·m, not joules.One dimensional check kills three of the four distractors instantly.
4. Adding the strips: W = Σ F(x)Δx, and letting Δx → 0 turns the sum into W = ∫F(x)dx, the area under the F–x graph.
   F(x) ↑
        |        ___
        |     __/   \__       ΔA = F(x)·Δx  (one strip)
        |   _/  |█|    \_
        |  /    |█|      \
        | /     |█|       \
        +-------|-|--------------→ x
              x_i  Δx      x_f

   ΔW = F(x)·Δx      →      W = ∫ F(x) dx  = area under curve
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an option list mixes sums and products of F and x, run the unit test first — work must come out in N·m. Only a product F × x survives. Trap: option (d) F(x)²Δx looks "more advanced" and tempts students who half-remember ½kx² (where the square is on x, not on F).
Answer: (b) ΔW = F(x)·Δx.
2A spring is compressed by 0.1 m and has k = 200 N/m. Find work done by the spring. (a) 1 J (b) 2 J (c) −1 J (d) −2 J — (Level-2)Work done by spring forceSpring PE and work done by a spring (negative work in compression)🟡 Similarc−1 J
Concept The spring force always points back toward the natural length, so when you push the free end in, the spring pushes out — force and displacement are antiparallel and the spring's work is negative. Both signs matter here: three of the four options are numerically right in magnitude, so the question is really testing the sign convention. The magnitude follows from the book's own result Wspring = ½k(x₁² − x₂²).
Formula Wspring = ½k(x₁² − x₂²) — k in N/m, x in m (measured from the natural length), W in J. Starting from relaxed (x₁ = 0) and ending at deformation x: Wspring = −½kx². (N/m)·m² = N·m = J ✓. Note x is squared, so a compression (−0.1 m) and a stretch (+0.1 m) give the same magnitude.
Solution
1. Read the data: k = 200 N/m, initial deformation x₁ = 0 (relaxed), final deformation x₂ = 0.1 m of compression."Compressed by 0.1 m" means the free end moves 0.1 m from the natural length, so |x₂| = 0.1 m; the sign of x is irrelevant because only x² appears.
2. Apply the book's formula:
  W = ½k(x₁² − x₂²) = ½(200)(0² − 0.1²)
3. Evaluate the bracket: 0 − 0.01 = −0.01 m².
4. W = ½ × 200 × (−0.01) = 100 × (−0.01) = −1 J (units: N/m × m² = J ✓).
5. Sanity check on the sign: during compression the spring pushes outward while the end moves inward ⇒ F and d are opposite ⇒ W < 0 ✓. The agent doing the compressing does +1 J, which is stored as elastic PE.
  relaxed:   |\/\/\/\/\/\/●        x = 0
                          |
  compressed: |\/\/\/\●   |        x = 0.1 m  (end moved LEFT)
                      →              spring pushes RIGHT (outward)

     displacement ←     force →      ⇒  W_spring is NEGATIVE

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the one-liner: a spring taken away from its natural length always does negative work; a spring returning to natural length always does positive work. You never need to think about whether it was a stretch or a compression. Trap: option (a) +1 J is the work done by the external agent, not by the spring — the question asks for the spring.
Answer: (c) −1 J (the spring does −1 J; 1 J is stored as elastic potential energy).

4A force F = 0.5x + 10 acts on a particle. Here F is in newton and x is in metre. Calculate the work done by the force during the displacement of the particle from x = 0 to x = 2 metre. — (Level-4)∫F dx for a linear forceWork done by variable force — Unacademy JEE (WEP L4)🟡 Similar21 J
Concept The force depends on position, so W = F·d is illegal — you must integrate. Because F is linear in x, there is also a one-line graphical shortcut: the area under a straight line is a trapezium, i.e. (average force) × (displacement). Both routes must agree, and checking them against each other is the fastest way to catch an algebra slip.
Formula W = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx — F in N, x in m, W in J. For F = 0.5x + 10: ∫(0.5x + 10)dx = 0.25x² + 10x. Shortcut for a linear F: W = ½(F₁ + F₂)(x₂ − x₁). 0.5 has units N/m so 0.5x is in N, and 10 is in N — the sum is a genuine force; N × m = J ✓.
Solution
1. Set up the integral over the given limits:
  W = ∫₀² (0.5x + 10) dxForce and displacement are both along +x, so the dot product is just the product — no cosθ factor.
2. Integrate term by term: ∫0.5x dx = 0.5·x²/2 = 0.25x², and ∫10 dx = 10x.
  W = [0.25x² + 10x]₀²
3. Substitute the upper limit x = 2 m: 0.25(4) + 10(2) = 1 + 20 = 21.
4. Substitute the lower limit x = 0: 0.25(0) + 10(0) = 0.
5. Subtract: W = 21 − 0 = 21 J.
6. Graphical cross-check: F(0) = 10 N, F(2) = 0.5(2) + 10 = 11 N. Average force = (10 + 11)/2 = 10.5 N; W = 10.5 N × 2 m = 21 J ✓The average-force shortcut is valid only because F is a straight line in x — do not use it for F = kx² or e−bx.
   F(N) ↑
   11   |               ____ 11 N   (at x = 2)
   10   |____________---
        |█████████████████|         area = trapezium
        |█████████████████|
     0  +--------+--------+--→ x (m)
        0        1        2

   W = ½(10 + 11) × 2 = 21 J
     = [0.25x² + 10x]₀²  = (1 + 20) − 0 = 21 J  ✓
⚡ JEE Trick For any force that is linear in x, work = mean force × displacement — a five-second answer with no calculus. Trap: using the final force 11 N × 2 m = 22 J, or the initial 10 N × 2 m = 20 J. Both are near-misses designed to catch students who skip the averaging.
Answer: W = 21 J.
5A particle of mass 0.5 kg travels in a straight line with velocity v = ax3/2 where a = 5 m−1/2s−1. What is the work done by the net force during its displacement from x = 0 to x = 2 m? — (Level-5)Work–energy theorem with v(x)Work and kinetic-energy theorem decoded for problems🟡 Similar50 J
Concept The question asks for the work of the net force, and the net force is exactly what the work–energy theorem talks about. So there is no need to find F(x) at all — you only need the speed at the two ends. Trying to build F = ma = mv(dv/dx) first is a legal but much slower route, and it is the trap the problem sets.
Formula Work–energy theorem: Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf² − ½mvi² — m in kg, v in m/s, W in J. Given v = a x3/2 ⇒ v² = a²x³. a has units m−1/2s−1, so a·x3/2 has units m−1/2s−1·m3/2 = m·s−1 ✓ — a genuine speed.
Solution
1. Initial state, x = 0: v_i = 5 × 03/2 = 0 ⇒ KE_i = 0.The particle starts from rest, which kills one whole term.
2. Final state, x = 2 m: v_f = 5 × 23/2. Rather than take the root, square it directly:
  v_f² = a²x³ = (5)²(2)³ = 25 × 8 = 200 m²/s²KE needs v², never v — squaring first avoids the messy √8 = 2.828.
3. Final kinetic energy: KE_f = ½ m v_f² = ½ (0.5 kg)(200 m²/s²)
4. Evaluate: ½ × 0.5 × 200 = 0.25 × 200 = 50 J.
5. Apply the theorem: Wnet = KE_f − KE_i = 50 − 0 = 50 J (units: kg·m²/s² = J ✓).
6. Long-route confirmation (optional): a = v dv/dx = (ax3/2)(1.5a x1/2) = 1.5a²x², so F = m(1.5a²x²) and W = ∫₀² 1.5ma²x² dx = 0.5ma²x³|₀² = 0.5(0.5)(25)(8) = 50 J ✓Same answer, four times the work — proof that the theorem was the right first move.
   x = 0  ─────────────────────▶  x = 2 m
   v = 0                          v² = a²x³ = 25 × 8 = 200 m²/s²
   KE = 0                         KE = ½(0.5)(200) = 50 J

   W_net = ΔKE = 50 − 0 = 50 J     (no need to ever find F(x))
⚡ JEE Trick The moment a problem gives you v as a function of x and asks for work by the net (or "total", or "all") force, write ΔKE and stop. Trap: computing v_f = 14.14 m/s and then rounding it before squaring — 14.1² = 198.8 gives 49.7 J and you lose the clean 50. Always square the exact expression.
Answer: Wnet = 50 J.

1A particle of mass 12 kg is acted upon by a force f = (100 − 2x²) where 'f' is in newton and 'x' is in metre. Calculate the work done by this force in moving the particle x = 0 to x = +10 m. Concept A two-stage board question: first integrate a position-dependent force to get the work, then convert that work into speed with the work–energy theorem. The subtlety is that f = 100 − 2x² reverses sign at x = √50 ≈ 7.07 m, so over the last 3 m of the trip the force actually decelerates the particle — yet the net work is still positive, and it is the net work that fixes the final speed.
Formula W = ∫₀¹⁰ (100 − 2x²) dx — f in N, x in m, W in J. Then W = ½mv² − 0 ⇒ v = √(2W/m) — m in kg, v in m/s. √(J/kg) = √(kg·m²·s⁻²/kg) = m/s ✓. The constant 100 is in N and the coefficient 2 carries N/m², so 2x² is also in N.
Solution
1. Integrate: ∫100 dx = 100x, and ∫2x² dx = 2x³/3.
  W = [100x − (2/3)x³]₀¹⁰
2. Upper limit x = 10 m: 100(10) = 1000, and (2/3)(10)³ = (2/3)(1000) = 666.67.
  W = 1000 − 666.67
3. Lower limit x = 0 gives 0, so
  W = 333.33 J ≈ 333 JPositive overall: the large positive area from 0 to 7.07 m outweighs the negative area from 7.07 m to 10 m.
4. The particle starts from rest and this is the only force acting, so W is the net work and KE_i = 0:
  ½ m v² = 333.33 J
5. Solve for v²: v² = 2W/m = 2(333.33)/12 = 666.67/12 = 55.56 m²/s²
6. v = √55.56 = 7.45 m/s ≈ 7.46 m/s (the book carries an extra digit in W before taking the root).

1What does the area under a force vs. displacement graph represent? (a) Velocity (b) Momentum (c) Work done (d) Acceleration — (Level-1)Meaning of area under F–xArea of force–displacement graph gives work🟡 SimilarcWork done
Concept "Area under a graph" always means the product of the two axis quantities, accumulated. Here the axes are force (N) and displacement (m), and N × m is the joule — the unit of work. This single identity, W = ∫F dx, is the entire content of this lecture, and every graph question in Levels 5 and 6 reduces to it.
Formula W = ∫x₁x₂ F dx = area under the F–x curve — F in N, x in m, W in J. Unit of area on this plot = (y-axis unit) × (x-axis unit) = N × m = J, which by itself picks out option (c).

1. Slice the graph into strips of width Δx. One strip has height F and width Δx, so its area is F·Δx.
2. But F·Δx is precisely the elementary work ΔW done over that displacement.This is the definition established at the top of the lecture: for a small enough Δx the force is effectively constant.

2If the force and displacement have opposite signs, what is the nature of the work done? (a) Zero (b) Infinite (c) Positive (d) Negative — (Level-1)Sign of workWork done when force is at an angle (dot product)🟡 SimilardNegative
Concept Work is a scalar (dot) product, W = F·d = Fd cosθ, so its sign is carried entirely by cosθ. Force and displacement "having opposite signs" in one dimension means θ = 180°, cos180° = −1, and the work is negative. Physically, negative work means the force is removing energy from the body — friction, air drag, gravity on a rising ball, a spring being deformed.
Formula W = F·d = F d cosθ — F in N, d in m, W in J, θ the angle between the vectors. cosθ is dimensionless, so W keeps the unit N·m = J at every angle; only the sign changes. Antiparallel ⇒ θ = 180° ⇒ W = −Fd.
Solution
1. One-dimensional case: if F = −|F| and d = +|d| (or vice versa), then W = F d = −|F||d|.
2. |F| and |d| are both positive, so their product is positive and the minus sign survives: W < 0.
3. Cross-check with the general formula: antiparallel ⇒ θ = 180° ⇒ cosθ = −1 ⇒ W = −Fd < 0 ✓
4. Rule out the rest: W = 0 needs θ = 90° (normal reaction, centripetal force); W is never infinite for finite F and d; W > 0 needs θ < 90°.Through the work–energy theorem, negative net work means the body slows down — exactly what friction and drag do.
     displacement  ────────────▶  (+)
     force         ◀────────────  (−)          θ = 180°

     W = F d cos180° = −F d   <  0

     θ = 0°   → W = +Fd   (maximum positive)
     θ = 90°  → W = 0      (normal reaction, centripetal force)
     θ = 180° → W = −Fd   (friction, drag, gravity on a rising body)
⚡ JEE Trick Ask "is this force speeding the body up or slowing it down?" — slowing down ⇒ negative work, no algebra needed. Trap: option (a) zero, chosen by students who remember "opposite forces cancel"; cancellation applies to the net force, never to the work of one individual force.
Answer: (d) Negative.
3For a spring, what is the relation between the force F and displacement x? (a) F = kx² (b) F = −kx (c) F = k/x (d) F = x − k — (Level-1)Hooke's law, restoring forceHooke's law (spring force) with examples🟡 SimilarbF = −kx
Concept Hooke's law says the spring force is proportional to the deformation and directed opposite to it — that "opposite to it" is the minus sign, and it is what makes the spring a restoring force and the ultimate source of the ½kx² energy formula. The lecture writes the force applied on the spring as F = kx and the force exerted by the spring as F = −kx; the question asks for the spring force, hence (b).
Formula Fspring = −kx — F in N, k the spring constant in N/m, x the displacement of the free end from the natural length in m. (N/m) × m = N ✓; option (a) would need k in N/m² and option (c) k in N·m, so neither is a spring constant. Integrating gives Wspring = −½kx².
Solution
1. Stretch the spring to x > 0 (free end pulled right). The spring pulls the end back to the left, so F < 0.A force that always points back toward x = 0 must be a negative multiple of x.
2. Compress it to x < 0 (free end pushed left). The spring pushes the end back to the right, so F > 0.
3. One expression covering both cases with F and x always opposite in sign: F = −kx, with k > 0.
4. Reject the rest: (a) F = kx² is always positive — it would push the same way whether stretched or compressed, so it could not restore. (c) F = k/x blows up at the natural length x = 0, where the real spring force must vanish. (d) F = x − k is not proportional to x and is non-zero at x = 0.

8Passage (Q8–Q10). A force F = 50 N is applied at one end of a string, the other end of which is tied to a block of mass 10 kg. The block is free to move on a frictionless horizontal surface. The string passes over a small pulley fixed 2 m above the surface. Take the initial instant as θ = 30° and the final instant as θ = 37° (θ = angle of the string with the horizontal). Q8. Net work done by the force F on the block is (a) 50/3 J (b) 100/3 J (c) 75 J (d) None of these — (Level-6)Work = F × length of string pulled inWork, energy and power — block pulled at an angle🟡 Similarb100/3 J
Concept The force F acts on the free end of the string, which is pulled in over the pulley. Since the string is inextensible and the tension is constant at 50 N, the work delivered to the block equals F × (the length of string that disappears over the pulley) = F × (L_initial − L_final). Trying to integrate ∫F cosθ dx along the floor is legal but far harder, because θ changes continuously. Notice also that the angle increases from 30° to 37°, which means the block moves toward the foot of the pulley.
Formula String length from block to pulley: L = h / sinθ — h = 2 m, L in m. Work by F: W = F (L_i − L_f) — F in N, W in J. N × m = J ✓. Use sin30° = 1/2, sin37° = 3/5 = 0.6 (the standard 3-4-5 triangle values the question is built on).
Solution
1. Geometry: the pulley is at height h = 2 m, the block on the floor, and θ is the angle the string makes with the horizontal, so sinθ = h/L ⇒ L = h/sinθ.
2. Initial length at θ = 30°: L_i = 2 / sin30° = 2 / 0.5 = 4 m.
3. Final length at θ = 37°: L_f = 2 / sin37° = 2 / 0.6 = 10/3 m ≈ 3.333 m.
4. Length of string pulled in: ΔL = L_i − L_f = 4 − 10/3 = 12/3 − 10/3 = 2/3 m.This is exactly how far the point of application of F moves, which is what work needs — not how far the block slides along the floor.
5. Work done by F: W = F ΔL = 50 × (2/3) = 100/3 J ≈ 33.3 J — option (b).
6. Cross-check the block's own displacement: horizontal distances are x = h/tanθ, so x_i = 2/tan30° = 3.46 m and x_f = 2/tan37° = 2/0.75 = 2.67 m; the block slides 0.79 m. Multiplying 50 N by 0.79 m would give 39.5 J — wrong, because the force is not horizontal and its direction changes.

9Passage (Q8–Q10), continued. What is the final velocity of the block if initially it was at rest? (a) √(25/3) ms⁻¹ (b) 5 ms⁻¹ (c) √(20/3) ms⁻¹ (d) None of these — (Level-6)Work–energy theorem on the blockBlock pulled at an angle: work–energy theorem for the final speed🟡 Similarc√(20/3) ms⁻¹
Concept Once Q8 has given the work, the speed is one line of the work–energy theorem. The surface is frictionless and horizontal, so gravity and the normal reaction are both perpendicular to the block's motion and do zero work — the 100/3 J from the string is the entire net work. The block starts from rest, so that work is all kinetic energy.
Formula Wnet = ½mv² − 0 ⇒ v = √(2W/m) — W in J, m in kg, v in m/s. √(J/kg) = √(m²/s²) = m/s ✓. Here W = 100/3 J and m = 10 kg.
Solution
1. List the forces on the block: string tension (does the 100/3 J from Q8), weight mg (vertical), normal reaction N (vertical), friction (zero — frictionless).
2. The block moves horizontally, so weight and normal reaction are perpendicular to the displacement and do no work. Net work = 100/3 J.Note the string is not horizontal, but its work has already been correctly accounted for in Q8 via the change of string length.
3. Work–energy theorem from rest: ½ m v² = 100/3.
4. Substitute m = 10 kg: ½ (10) v² = 100/3 ⇒ 5v² = 100/3.
5. Solve for v²: v² = 100/15 = 20/3 m²/s².
6. v = √(20/3) ≈ √6.667 ≈ 2.58 m/s — option (c).
7. Sanity: 100/3 ≈ 33 J spread over a 10 kg block is a modest energy, so a speed of about 2.6 m/s is entirely reasonable; option (b) 5 m/s would need ½(10)(25) = 125 J.

⚡ JEE Trick On a horizontal frictionless surface, weight and normal force are automatically zero-work — so the net work is whatever the applied agent delivers, and v = √(2W/m) finishes the problem. Trap: option (a) √(25/3) comes from using W = 125/3 or m = 8; option (b) 5 m/s is the "nice" number the question dangles.
Answer: (c) v = √(20/3) m/s ≈ 2.58 m/s.
10Passage (Q8–Q10), continued. Find the ratio of initial acceleration to final acceleration of the block. (a) 3√5/8 (b) 8√3/5 (c) 8√5/3 (d) 5√3/8 — (Level-6)a = F cosθ / m, ratio of cosinesWork done by tension force and gravity in a pulley–block system🟡 Similard5√3/8
Concept The block accelerates horizontally, so only the horizontal component of the string tension matters: a = F cosθ/m. The tension is the same 50 N at both instants and the mass is the same, so the ratio of accelerations is simply the ratio of cosines — every other quantity cancels. As θ grows from 30° to 37°, cosθ falls, so the block is decelerating in the sense that its acceleration shrinks.
Formula Horizontal Newton's second law: F cosθ = m a ⇒ a = F cosθ / m — F in N, m in kg, a in m/s². Ratio: a_i / a_f = cosθ_i / cosθ_f. F and m cancel, leaving a pure dimensionless ratio ✓. Standard values: cos30° = √3/2, cos37° = 4/5 = 0.8.
Solution
1. Resolve the string tension along the floor: horizontal component F cosθ; the vertical component F sinθ is absorbed by the normal reaction (the block stays on the surface).As long as N > 0 the block does not lift off, so only the horizontal equation is needed for a.
2. Initial acceleration at θ = 30°: a_i = F cos30°/m = 50(√3/2)/10 = 2.5√3 ≈ 4.33 m/s².
3. Final acceleration at θ = 37°: a_f = F cos37°/m = 50(0.8)/10 = 4 m/s².
4. Take the ratio, letting F and m cancel:
  a_i/a_f = cos30°/cos37° = (√3/2)/(4/5)
5. Simplify: (√3/2) × (5/4) = 5√3/8 — option (d).
6. Numerical check: 5(1.732)/8 = 8.66/8 = 1.083; and 4.33/4 = 1.083 ✓ The ratio is slightly greater than 1, consistent with cosθ decreasing as θ goes from 30° to 37°.
