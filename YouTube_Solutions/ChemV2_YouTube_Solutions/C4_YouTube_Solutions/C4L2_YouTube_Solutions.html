1For a 3p valence electron, electrons present in the (n−1) shell contribute how much to the shielding constant (σ)?Slater's rules — (n−1) shellSlater's rule — the 0.35 / 0.85 / 1.00 contributions explained🟡 Similarb0.85 each
Concept Slater's rules turn the vague idea of "shielding" into arithmetic. A valence electron is pushed away from the nucleus by every other electron, but not equally: an electron in the same shell sits at roughly the same distance and screens only weakly; an electron one shell inside spends most of its time between the nucleus and the test electron and screens strongly; an electron two or more shells inside is essentially always inside and screens completely. Slater assigned the three regimes the numbers 0.35, 0.85 and 1.00. The question fixes the test electron in a 3p orbital, so n = 3 and the (n−1) shell is the second shell.
Formula σ = 0.35 × (electrons in the ns,np group, excluding the test electron) + 0.85 × (electrons in the (n−1) shell) + 1.00 × (electrons in (n−2) and lower shells) the 1s orbital is the one exception — when the test electron itself is a 1s electron its partner contributes 0.30, not 0.35.
Solution
1. Identify the test electron: it is a 3p electron, so its principal quantum number is n = 3.
2. Identify the shell being asked about: the (n−1) shell = the 2nd shell (2s, 2p).These electrons lie one shell inside the test electron — the "strong but incomplete" screening regime.
3. Read off Slater's value for that regime: each such electron contributes 0.85.
4. Eliminate the distractors: (a) 0.35 belongs to electrons in the same (ns, np) group; (c) 1.00 belongs to electrons in (n−2) or lower shells; (d) 0 belongs to electrons in groups lying to the right of the test electron's group, which never shield it.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the triple as a ladder 0.35 → 0.85 → 1.00 read inwards from the test electron: the deeper an electron lies, the closer its screening gets to a full unit of nuclear charge. Trap: the rule changes completely when the test electron itself is in an nd or nf orbital — then every electron in any group to its left contributes 1.00 and the 0.85 tier disappears. Always check the orbital of the test electron before picking a number.

Concept Moving one step to the right in a period, two things change at once and they pull in opposite directions: the nuclear charge Z rises by exactly 1, while the extra electron joins the same shell and therefore adds only 0.35 to the screening constant. Since 1.00 > 0.35, Z* = Z − σ climbs by about 0.65 per step. That single number is why Z* rises steadily across a period and why atomic radius shrinks.
Formula ΔZ* = ΔZ − Δσ = 1 − 0.35 = 0.65 per element across a period. For F: σ = 0.35 × 6 + 0.85 × 2 = 2.10 + 1.70 = 3.80 ⇒ Z* = 9 − 3.80 = 5.20. For Ne: σ = 0.35 × 7 + 0.85 × 2 = 2.45 + 1.70 = 4.15 ⇒ Z* = 10 − 4.15 = 5.85.
Solution
1. Write the Slater sum for F (Z = 9), valence electron in 2p: six other electrons in the (2s, 2p) group at 0.35, two 1s electrons at 0.85 ⇒ σ = 3.80, Z* = 5.20.
2. Write it for Ne (Z = 10): now seven other electrons in the (2s, 2p) group ⇒ σ = 4.15, Z* = 5.85.
3. Compare: 5.85 > 5.20, so Ne wins — the extra proton outweighs the extra same-shell electron by 0.65.

5Which statement best explains why atomic radius decreases across a period?Atomic radius trend — causeWhy does atomic radius decrease across a period?🟢 ExactbEffective nuclear charge increases
Concept Atomic size is set by a tug-of-war between the nucleus pulling the valence shell in and the inner electrons screening that pull. Across a period the shell number n does not change — every new electron enters the same outermost shell — so the only variable left is how hard that shell is being pulled. Because each step adds a full proton (+1.00) but only a weak same-shell screener (+0.35), Z* rises, the valence shell is drawn inwards, and the radius contracts. The theory states it in one line: "on moving from left to right along a period, atomic radii decrease because effective nuclear charge increases."
Key relation r ∝ 1 ÷ Z* at constant n, with Z* = Z − σ rising by ≈ 0.65 per step. Period 2 shows it: Li 1.55 Å → Be 1.12 → B 0.88 → C 0.77 → N 0.70 → O 0.66 → F 0.64 Å, a steady contraction as Z* climbs 1.30 → 5.20.
Solution
1. Fix what stays constant: the number of shells is the same for every element of one period, so the size change cannot come from n.
2. Identify what changes: Z increases by 1 each step, σ by only 0.35 ⇒ Z* increases.
3. Apply the consequence: larger Z* means a stronger inward pull on the valence electrons, so the cloud is drawn closer ⇒ smaller radius. That is option (b).
4. Eliminate (a): shielding does increase, but only by 0.35 per electron — far too weakly to reverse the trend; if shielding dominated, the radius would grow.
5. Eliminate (c): the number of shells is constant across a period — that factor explains the trend down a group, not across one.
6. Eliminate (d): if nuclear charge and shielding rose equally, Z* would be constant and so would the radius, contradicting the observed contraction.

 Li → F            constant     +1/step    +0.35/step  +0.65/step   shrinks
 Li 1.55 Å  Be 1.12  B 0.88  C 0.77  N 0.70  O 0.66  F 0.64 Å

⚡ JEE Trick Learn the two trends as two different causes: across a period the shell count is fixed so Z* decides (radius ↓); down a group a whole new shell is added so n decides (radius ↑) even though Z is far larger. Whenever an option mentions "number of shells" in a period question, or names effective nuclear charge as the dominant term in a group question, it is the planted distractor.

1Explain the screening effect with a suitable example.Screening / shielding effectScreening (shielding) effect explained with examples — periodic properties🟢 Exact—
Concept A valence electron in a many-electron atom does not feel the bare nuclear charge. Between it and the nucleus sit the inner electrons, and their negative charge repels the valence electron outwards, partly cancelling the nuclear attraction. The theory phrases it as: "inner electrons create a protective screen between the nucleus and the valence electrons", so the valence electron behaves as though a portion of the nuclear charge were simply absent. This is the screening effect, equivalently called the shielding effect.
Definition & relation Screening effect: the phenomenon in which inner electrons shield the valence electron from the full attraction of the nucleus. Its quantitative measure is the screening constant σ, and the charge that actually survives is Z* = Z − σ Z = true nuclear charge (atomic number), σ = screening constant, Z* = effective nuclear charge — the charge the valence electron really experiences.
Solution
1. Set up the picture: the outer (test) electron experiences two opposing forces — attraction from the nucleus, and repulsion from all the inner electrons.
2. State the effect: the inner-electron repulsion weakens the nuclear attraction, so the outer electron is held less tightly than a bare nucleus of charge Z would hold it.
3. Name it: this shielding of the valence electron by inner electrons is the screening (shielding) effect; the inner electrons act as a screen.
4. Give the worked example — sodium, Na (Z = 11), 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹: the single 3s electron is screened by the eight electrons of shell 2 (0.85 each) and the two of shell 1 (1.00 each).σ = 0.85 × 8 + 1.00 × 2 = 6.80 + 2.00 = 8.80, so Z* = 11 − 8.80 = 2.2 — the 3s electron feels only about 2.2 units of positive charge, not 11.
5. Give a second, contrasting example — nitrogen, N (Z = 7), 1s² (2s, 2p)⁵: σ = 0.35 × 4 + 0.85 × 2 = 1.40 + 1.70 = 3.10 ⇒ Z* = 3.90.Comparing the two shows that screening grows with the number of inner shells, which is why Z* stays nearly constant down a group (Li 1.3, Na 2.2, K 2.2, Rb 2.2, Cs 2.2).
6. State the consequences: more screening ⇒ smaller Z* ⇒ looser hold on the valence electron ⇒ larger atomic size, lower ionisation energy and greater metallic character.
 nucleus (+Z) ---- inner e⁻ (screen) ---- valence e⁻
    attraction  <-------------------------
    repulsion   ------------------------->  (weakens the pull)

 example  Na (Z = 11) : 1s² , 2s² 2p⁶ , 3s¹
   σ = 0.85 × 8 + 1.00 × 2 = 8.80
   Z* = 11 − 8.80 = 2.2   ⇒  the 3s e⁻ "sees" only ≈ 2.2 units, not 11
 order of screening power :  s > p > d > f
⚡ JEE Trick Learn the screening-power order s > p > d > f — it is the reason d- and f-electrons shield so poorly, which in turn produces lanthanoid contraction and the small size of post-d elements. Trap: writing "screening increases Z*" — screening always reduces Z*, since Z* = Z − σ; the Quick Recap statement "as the screening effect increases effective nuclear charge also increases" is marked False for exactly this reason.
Key point: inner electrons repel the valence electron and partly cancel the nuclear attraction (the screening/shielding effect), measured by σ, so the valence electron feels only Z* = Z − σ — e.g. in Na the 3s electron feels 2.2 units instead of 11.
2Explain Effective nuclear charge.Effective nuclear charge Z*Shielding / screening effect and effective nuclear charge (Z*)🟢 Exact—
Concept If an atom had only one electron, that electron would feel the entire nuclear charge Z. In a many-electron atom the other electrons get in the way, and the charge that actually reaches a given electron is smaller. Chemists keep the simple one-electron picture and simply replace Z by a reduced number: the effective nuclear charge Z*. It is defined as "the reduced nuclear charge, or the nuclear charge actually experienced by an electron", and it is the single quantity that controls atomic size, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.
Formula Z* = Z − σ Z* = effective nuclear charge, Z = atomic number (true nuclear charge), σ = screening constant computed from Slater's rules. Z* is not the same for every electron in an atom — it varies with how deeply the electron sits, e.g. in Zn the 4s electron has Z* = 4.35 while the 3d electron has Z* = 8.85.
Solution
1. Define it: Z* is the net positive charge experienced by a particular electron after the shielding by all the other electrons has been subtracted.
2. Give the origin: the test electron feels attraction from the nucleus and repulsion from the inner electrons; the repulsion cancels part of the attraction, and the leftover attraction is expressed as Z*.
3. Give the formula and the meaning of each symbol: Z* = Z − σ, with σ from Slater's rules (0.35 same group, 0.85 for (n−1), 1.00 for (n−2) and deeper).
4. Work an example — K (Z = 19), 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹: σ = 0.85 × 8 + 1.00 × 10 = 6.8 + 10 = 16.8 ⇒ Z* = 19 − 16.8 = 2.2.
5. Show that Z* is electron-specific: in Zn (Z = 30), for the 4s electron σ = 0.35 × 1 + 0.85 × 18 + 1.00 × 10 = 25.65 ⇒ Z* = 4.35; for a 3d electron σ = 0.35 × 9 + 1.00 × 18 = 21.15 ⇒ Z* = 8.85.The 3d electron lies deeper, is shielded by fewer electrons, and therefore feels a much larger effective charge.
6. State the trends: Z* increases left→right across a period (≈ +0.65 per step) and stays almost unchanged down a group (Na, K, Rb, Cs all ≈ 2.2), which is why size shrinks across a period but grows down a group.

⚡ JEE Trick Quote the two trend facts alongside the definition — they earn the marks and answer most MCQs: Z* rises by 0.65 per step across a period and is essentially constant down a group. Trap: treating Z* as a property of the atom. It is a property of a chosen electron in that atom, which is why Zn has two different values in the same question.
Key point: effective nuclear charge Z* = Z − σ is the reduced nuclear charge an individual electron actually experiences after shielding by the other electrons; it differs from electron to electron, rises ≈ 0.65 per step across a period, and stays nearly constant down a group.
3Define atomic radius.Definition of atomic radiusWhat is Atomic Radius? Periodic Trends🟢 Exact—
Concept The naive definition — "the average distance of the valence-shell electron from the nucleus" — is correct in spirit but useless in the laboratory, for two reasons the theory states explicitly: (i) isolating a single atom is very difficult, and (ii) an atom has no well-defined boundary, because the electron cloud fades away gradually rather than stopping. So chemists replace it with an operational definition based on a measurable quantity — the distance between two nuclei, which diffraction methods give precisely.
Definition atomic radius = ½ × (internuclear distance d between two atoms of the same element) d is also called the bond length; because both atoms are identical, half of d belongs to each. The value of d depends on how the two atoms are held together, which is why the single word "radius" splits into four operational types.
Solution
1. Give the conceptual definition: the average distance of the valence-shell electrons from the nucleus.
2. State why it cannot be measured that way: a single atom cannot be isolated, and the atom has no sharp boundary.
3. Give the accurate, working definition: half the internuclear distance between two atoms of the same element; the internuclear distance is the bond length, and it depends on the type of bond joining the atoms.
4. List the four types based on the bond:
  • Covalent radius — used for non-metals: half the distance between the nuclei of two covalently bonded like atoms, e.g. Cl₂ gives 1.98/2 = 0.99 Å.
  • van der Waals radius — for non-bonded atoms (noble gases): half the distance between nuclei of adjacent atoms in the solid state, e.g. Cl 1.80 Å.
  • Metallic (crystal) radius — for metals: half the distance between two adjacent metal nuclei in the close-packed metallic lattice.
  • Ionic radius — the distance from the nucleus to the outermost shell of an ion.
5. Quote the size ordering, which is the usual follow-up question: van der Waals radius > metallic radius > covalent radius, because a non-bonded contact is loosest and a covalent bond pulls the nuclei closest together.

Key point: atomic radius is the average distance of the valence electrons from the nucleus; operationally it is half the internuclear distance between two like atoms, and it comes in four bond-dependent types — covalent, van der Waals, metallic and ionic — with van der Waals > metallic > covalent.

Concept The word "effective" is the whole answer: Z* is not the charge the nucleus has, it is the charge an electron experiences once the screening by the other electrons has been subtracted. 4. Eliminate (b) "Charge felt by nucleus": nonsense — the nucleus does not "feel" a charge; it is the source of it. The direction of the sentence is reversed.
5. Eliminate (d) "Atomic number": the atomic number is Z, again the unscreened value; Z* equals Z only for a one-electron species such as H, He⁺ or Li²⁺.

Concept An electron in the (n−2) shell or deeper is, to an excellent approximation, always closer to the nucleus than the test electron. Gauss's law then says it cancels a full unit of nuclear charge as far as the outer electron is concerned. Slater therefore assigns it the maximum possible value, 1.00 — complete shielding. 2. Same-group partners = 5 − 1 = 4 → 4 × 0.35 = 1.40.

4. (n−2) and deeper = 2 + 8 = 10 → 10 × 1.00 = 10.00.
