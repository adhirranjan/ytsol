1In combined rotational and translational motion, which law governs the rotational part? (a) Newton's first law  (b) Newton's second law for translation  (c) Newton's second law for rotation  (d) Newton's third law — (Level-1)Two equations of combined motionRolling Series 01 | Combined Translation + Rotational Motion | JEE/NEET🟡 SimilarcNewton's second law for rotation, τ = Iα
Concept A rolling body is doing two things at once, and each needs its own equation. The centre of mass obeys the same law a point particle would — it cannot tell that the body is spinning — so ΣF = macm handles the translation. The spin about the centre is driven by the turning effect of those same forces, i.e. by torque, and the resistance to being spun up is the moment of inertia. So the rotational half is Newton's second law rewritten in angular variables: τ = Iα. Neither equation can be dropped: two unknowns (a and α) demand two equations, closed by the rolling constraint a = Rα.
Formula ΣF = macm and Στcm = Icmα. Torque replaces force, I replaces mass, α replaces a — the angular sentence has the same grammar as the linear one.
Solution
1. Split the motion: velocity of any point = vcm (translation) + ω×r (rotation about the centre). The two pieces are independent, so they need two laws.
2. Translation is settled by ΣF = macm; it says nothing about spin because the internal forces and the point of application cancel out of it. This is why a force at the top and the same force at the centre give the same acm but very different α.
3. Rotation needs the point of application, which enters only through τ = r×F. The governing statement is therefore τ = Iα — option (c).
4. Check it on the book's own case: cylinder on two strings, mg − 2T = ma and 2TR = (½mR²)(a/R), giving a = 2g/3 — the second equation is exactly τ = Iα.
5. Reject (a): Newton's first law only says an unaccelerated body needs no net force; it governs nothing here because the body is accelerating.
6. Reject (b) and (d): (b) is the translational half, which the question explicitly excludes, and (d) — action–reaction — only pairs the friction on the body with the friction on the ground; it never yields α. Hence (c).
 combined motion = two equations + one constraint
 ------------------------------------------------
 translation : sum F   = m a_cm
 rotation    : sum tau = I_cm alpha   <-- the answer
 constraint  : a_cm    = R alpha      (pure rolling)
 3 equations -> a, alpha and the friction f
 drop any one and the system is unsolvable
⚡ JEE Trick The tempting wrong pick is (b): students who have only met F = ma try to stretch it over the whole problem and end up with one equation and two unknowns. The tell-tale sign that you need τ = Iα is that the answer depends on where the force acts — top, centre or rim. Whenever a rolling question quotes a point of application, it is asking for the torque equation.
Answer: (c) Newton's second law for rotation, τ = Iα.
2A homogenous solid sphere is placed on an inclined plane making an angle 'θ' with the horizontal. For what value of the coefficient of static friction 'μ' would the sphere roll down the plane without slipping? — (Level-2)Minimum μ for pure rolling on an inclinePure rolling on inclined plane — minimum friction for ring, disc and sphere🟢 Exactμ ≥ (2/7) tanθ
Concept On a frictionless incline a sphere would slide without ever turning, so friction is the only agent that can spin it up. Because the contact point does not slip, that friction is static, and static friction is not a fixed number — it takes whatever value pure rolling demands, up to a ceiling μN. So the recipe is: first ask how much friction rolling needs, then insist that the surface can supply it. The needed amount grows with tanθ, which is why every incline has a steepness beyond which a given surface can no longer hold a body in pure rolling.
Formula fneeded = mg sinθ/(1 + mR²/I) and fmax = μmg cosθ. The first comes from solving ma = mg sinθ − f with fR = I(a/R); the second is the Coulomb ceiling.
Solution
1. Translation along the incline: mg sinθ − f = ma; rotation about the centre: fR = Iα with I = (2/5)mR².
2. Pure rolling links them: a = Rα, so f = (2/5)mR²(a/R)/R = (2/5)ma. Friction is the whole torque here — gravity acts at the centre and the normal force is radial, so neither turns the sphere.
3. Substitute: mg sinθ − (2/5)ma = ma → (7/5)a = g sinθ → a = 5g sinθ/7.
4. Hence the friction actually needed is f = (2/5)m(5g sinθ/7) = 2mg sinθ/7.
5. Impose the ceiling: 2mg sinθ/7 ≤ μmg cosθ → μ ≥ (2/7)tanθ. mg cancels — the answer is mass-independent, so a marble and a bowling ball need the same surface.
6. Sanity check the shape-dependence: the same algebra gives (2/5)tanθ for a shell and (1/3)tanθ for a disc, all bigger than 2/7 ≈ 0.286 tanθ, because a solid sphere is the easiest body to keep rolling.
 body        I/mR^2   a/(g sin)   mu_min / tan(theta)
 -------------------------------------------------
 sphere       2/5      5/7            2/7 = 0.286
 disc         1/2      2/3            1/3 = 0.333
 shell        2/3      3/5            2/5 = 0.400
 ring          1       1/2            1/2 = 0.500
 more mass far from axis -> more friction needed
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is writing μ = tanθ, the sliding-block condition, which is 3.5× too strict here. Rolling needs far less friction than sliding needs, because friction only has to torque the body, not stop it. Memorise the one-line master formula μmin = tanθ/(1 + mR²/I) and every shape falls out in seconds.
Answer: μ ≥ (2/7) tanθ.
3Find the acceleration of a system consisting of a cylinder of mass 'm' and radius 'R' and a plank of mass 'M' placed on a smooth surface if it is pulled with a force 'F' as shown in fig. Given that sufficient friction is present between the cylinder and the plank surface to prevent sliding of the cylinder. (The cylinder rides on the plank and its axle is tied by a string that runs to a fixed pulley on the wall and back to the plank.) — (Level-3)Cylinder on a plank · string constraintRolling Motion | Combined Rotational + Translation Motion (CRTM) | JEE🟡 Similara = F/(3m + M)
Concept Three things are coupled here and each supplies one equation. The string over the fixed wall pulley makes the cylinder's centre move exactly as fast as the plank but the other way, so one number a describes the whole system. Rolling without slipping on a moving floor means the cylinder's contact point must match the plank, not the ground — that fixes α in terms of a. Finally the ground is smooth, so only F, the string tension and the plank–cylinder friction act horizontally on the plank.
Formula acontact = acm − αR = aplank, τcm = Iα, I = ½mR². The rolling condition is always "contact points share velocity"; on a moving surface that is a relative statement, not v = ωR.
Solution
1. String constraint: with the pulley fixed to the wall, if the plank goes right by x the cylinder's axle goes left by x, so the plank has +a and the cylinder's centre −a.
2. Rolling on the plank: −a − αR = +a → αR = −2a, i.e. the cylinder spins backwards at 2a/R. The surfaces separate at 2a, and all of that relative motion has to be taken up by spin.
3. Torque about the cylinder's centre (the tension acts at the axle, zero arm): fR = I|α| = (½mR²)(2a/R) → f = ma, directed forward on the cylinder.
4. Newton for the cylinder: f − T = m(−a) → T = f + ma = 2ma. The string is doing the work of holding the axle back while friction drags it forward.
5. Newton for the plank (smooth ground, three horizontal forces): F − f − T = Ma → F − ma − 2ma = Ma.
6. Collect: F = (3m + M)a → a = F/(3m + M). The cylinder contributes 3m, not m — its spin and the string's pull triple its inertial cost.
 forces on the plank (smooth floor)
 -----------------------------------
   T <---[========= M =========]---> F
              ^ f (from cylinder)
 f = m a ,  T = 2 m a  ->  F = (M + 3m) a
 cylinder axle moves LEFT at a, plank RIGHT at a

⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit in this one figure. First, writing a = αR as if the cylinder rolled on the ground — it rolls on the plank, so use relative acceleration. Second, forgetting that the string pulls the plank as well as the cylinder; drop T from the plank equation and you get F/(m+M), an answer that looks respectable and is wrong. Whenever a pulley is bolted to a wall, expect equal-and-opposite motion of the two ends.
Answer: a = F/(3m + M).
4A heavy solid cylinder of mass 6 kg and radius 1/8 m is suspended by two ropes wrapped round it as shown. The cylinder is released from rest. Calculate (i) the acceleration of the cylinder and (ii) the tension in each rope. Given : g = 10 ms−2. — (Level-4)Falling cylinder on two ropesRolling Series 01 | Combined Translation + Rotational Motion | JEE/NEET🟡 Similara = 2g/3 = 6.67 m/s² ; T = mg/6 = 10 N in each rope
Concept The ropes are vertical and unwinding, so the points where they leave the cylinder are momentarily at rest — exactly the condition that defines rolling, only here the "surface" is the pair of ropes. That makes the rope line the instantaneous axis, so the centre falls with a = αr. Gravity acts at the centre and cannot turn the cylinder; the two tensions do all the turning. Notice the radius never survives to the final answer: it cancels between the torque and the moment of inertia, so a 1/8 m drum and a 1 m drum fall alike.
Formula mg − 2T = ma, 2Tr = Iα = (½mr²)(a/r). Two ropes means the torque arm carries 2T; the ½ is the solid cylinder's shape factor.
Solution
1. Rope constraint: the string leaves the rim with zero velocity, so a = αr and the rim speed always matches the fall speed.
2. Rotation: 2Tr = (½mr²)(a/r) gives 2T = ½ma = (½)(6)a = 3a. r cancels here — this is why the printed value 1/8 m is a decoy.
3. Translation: mg − 2T = ma → 60 − 3a = 6a → 9a = 60.
4. Hence a = 60/9 = 20/3 = 6.67 m/s², which is the standard result a = 2g/3 for a solid cylinder.
5. Back-substitute: 2T = 3a = 3(20/3) = 20 N, so each rope carries T = 10 N = mg/6 = 60/6. The pair of ropes supports only a third of the weight; the other two-thirds is the unbalanced force that accelerates the fall.
6. Check by energy after a drop h: mgh = ½mv² + ½(½mr²)(v/r)² = (3/4)mv² → v² = (4/3)gh = 2(2g/3)h — the same a = 2g/3. A third of the released energy has gone into spin, so only two-thirds is left to speed up the fall.

Read the question: it asks for the tension in each rope. 5A man pushes a cylinder of mass m1 with the help of a plank of mass m2 as shown in fig. There is no slipping at any contact. The horizontal component of the force applied by the man is F. Find : (i) the acceleration of the plank and the centre of mass of the cylinder; (ii) the magnitudes and directions of frictional forces at contact points. — (Level-5)Plank on a rolling cylinder · two friction pairsRolling Motion | Combined Rotational + Translation Motion (CRTM) | JEE🟡 Similara1 = 4F/(3m1+8m2), a2 = 8F/(3m1+8m2); f1 = 3m1F/(3m1+8m2), f2 = m1F/(3m1+8m2)
Concept The cylinder rolls on the ground, so its contact point is at rest and the ground line is the instantaneous axis. Every point of the cylinder then moves at a speed proportional to its height above that line: the centre at v, the top at 2v. The plank sits on the top and cannot slip, so the plank simply is the top point — its acceleration is twice the centre's. Two different friction forces act, one from the plank on top and one from the ground below, and only the torque equation can separate them.
Formula atop = 2acm, τcontact = Icontactα with Icontact = (3/2)m1R². Taking torques about the ground contact kills the unknown ground friction in one stroke.
Solution
1. Let the cylinder's centre have a1; then the plank has a2 = 2a1 and the spin is α = a1/R.
2. Torque about the ground contact (only f1, at height 2R, has an arm): f1(2R) = (3/2)m1R²(a1/R) → f1 = (3/4)m1a1, forward on the cylinder.
3. Newton for the cylinder: f1 + f2 = m1a1 → f2 = m1a1 − (3/4)m1a1 = (1/4)m1a1, also forward. Both frictions push the cylinder along; the ground one is small because most of the torque job is already done by f1.
4. Newton for the plank (F forward, reaction f1 backward): F − f1 = m2a2 = 2m2a1.
5. Substitute f1: F = (3/4)m1a1 + 2m2a1 = a1(3m1 + 8m2)/4 → a1 = 4F/(3m1+8m2) and a2 = 2a1 = 8F/(3m1+8m2).
6. Feed a1 back: f1 = 3m1F/(3m1+8m2) — forward on the cylinder, backward on the plank — and f2 = m1F/(3m1+8m2), forward on the cylinder from the ground. The key prints f2 with a minus sign only to say it opposes f1's reaction on the plank; its magnitude is as above.

Take torques about the contact point and f2 disappears from the first equation, which is what makes the algebra short.

1A sphere is rolling on a rough surface. The only forces acting on the sphere are its weight and the contact force from the ground. The direction of frictional force on the sphere is (a) right  (b) left  (c) zero  (d) can't be predicted — (Level-1)Friction on a freely rolling bodyHow to find the Direction of Friction in Rolling and Slipping🟡 Similarczero
Concept Static friction is a servant force: it takes exactly the value needed to enforce the no-slip condition, and if nothing threatens the no-slip condition it takes the value zero. On a horizontal surface with no applied force and no torque, a body already rolling at v = ωR can simply carry on for ever — v is constant, ω is constant, and the rolling condition stays satisfied by itself. Since no friction is required, none appears. Assuming friction "must" act because the floor is rough is the standard error.
Formula f = macm and fR = Iα with acm = Rα. Three equations, one unknown f — they are consistent only if f = 0, so the answer is forced, not chosen.
Solution
1. Suppose friction f acts forward. Translation: f = ma → a = f/m.
2. Rotation about the centre: friction at the bottom gives −fR = Iα = (2/5)mR²α → α = −5f/(2mR), i.e. the spin slows while the centre speeds up.
3. Impose rolling: a = Rα → f/m = −5f/(2m) → f(1 + 5/2) = 0 → f = 0. Any non-zero f would speed up the translation and slow down the spin at the same time, breaking v = ωR immediately.
4. Cross-check with the other two forces: the weight acts at the centre (no torque) and the normal reaction is vertical through the centre (no torque), so nothing wants to change ω.
5. Reject (a) and (b): a horizontal force would accelerate the centre of mass, yet the problem lists no agency that could do so — a rolling ball on a level rough floor does not slow down in ideal mechanics.
6. Reject (d): the situation is fully determined, so "can't be predicted" is wrong; the answer is (c) zero. In real life a ball does stop, but that is rolling friction from surface deformation, a separate effect this chapter treats later.
 does friction need to act?  test v = wR

              v grows, w shrinks -> slipping
 f backward -> v shrinks, w grows -> slipping

⚡ JEE Trick The word "rough" is the bait: roughness gives friction a capacity, not an obligation. Ask instead "what would go wrong at the contact if f were zero?". On a flat floor with no applied force the answer is "nothing", so f = 0. The same test instantly gives f = 0 for a ring pushed at its top and f ≠ 0 the moment the floor tilts.
Answer: (c) zero.
2If a solid sphere, disc and cylinder are allowed to roll down an inclined plane from the same height (a) the cylinder will reach the bottom first  (b) the disc will reach the bottom first  (c) the sphere will reach the bottom first  (d) all will reach the bottom at the same time — (Level-1)Race down an incline · role of I/mR²Pure rolling on inclined plane — ring, disc and sphere compared🟡 Similarcthe sphere will reach the bottom first
Concept Every body released from the same height is handed the same energy per kilogram, mgh, but each must spend part of it on spin before it can spend the rest on speed. The share taken by spin is fixed by the single dimensionless number k²/R² = I/mR², which measures how far the mass sits from the axis. Mass and radius themselves drop out completely, so the race is decided by shape alone: the body that keeps its mass nearest the axis keeps most of the energy as translation and wins.
Formula a = g sinθ/(1 + I/mR²), v = √(2gh/(1 + I/mR²)). Both follow from mgh = ½mv²(1 + I/mR²) with v = ωR.
Solution
1. Write energy conservation for a body rolling down height h: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω² = ½mv²(1 + I/mR²).
2. Solve: v² = 2gh/(1 + k²/R²), so the smaller the shape factor, the larger v at the bottom — and, since a = g sinθ/(1 + k²/R²) is constant, the smaller the time.
3. Insert the shape factors: sphere 2/5 = 0.4, disc 1/2 = 0.5, cylinder (solid) 1/2 = 0.5. A solid cylinder and a disc are the same body in this chapter — same I = ½mR² — so options (a) and (b) are the same claim and both must be wrong.
4. Accelerations: asphere = 5g sinθ/7 = 0.714 g sinθ against adisc = acyl = 2g sinθ/3 = 0.667 g sinθ.
5. Over the same slope length L, t = √(2L/a), so the sphere's larger a gives the smaller t: the sphere arrives first — (c).
6. Reject (d): "all together" is true only for sliding on a smooth incline, where a = g sinθ for every body. Rolling is what breaks the tie, because it charges each shape a different energy tax.
 same h, same theta -> ranking by k^2/R^2
 ---------------------------------------
 sphere    0.40   a = 0.714 g sin(th)   1st
 disc      0.50   a = 0.667 g sin(th)   tie

⚡ JEE Trick Spot the giveaway before doing any physics: a solid cylinder is a disc, so (a) and (b) cannot both be right and therefore neither is. That leaves (c) and (d), and (d) is the frictionless-sliding answer. Remember the one-line ranking sphere > disc > shell > ring, and that it never depends on m or R.
Answer: (c) the sphere will reach the bottom first.
3One hollow and one solid cylinder of the same outer radius rolls down on a smooth inclined plane. The foot of the inclined plane is reached by (a) solid cylinder earlier  (b) hollow cylinder earlier  (c) simultaneously  (d) the heavier earlier irrespective of being solid or hollow — (Level-1)Smooth vs rough incline · who rolls at allPure rolling on inclined plane — ring, disc and sphere compared🟡 Similarasolid cylinder earlier ⚠
Concept Two separate questions hide in this one line: can the bodies roll, and if so which rolls faster. Rolling needs a tangential force at the contact to create torque about the centre, and on a smooth plane there is none — gravity acts at the centre and the normal reaction points straight at it. So on a genuinely smooth incline both cylinders slide, keep whatever spin they started with, and descend identically. Only when the plane is rough does the shape factor I/mR² enter and separate them.
Formula smooth: a = g sinθ for every body; rough (pure rolling): a = g sinθ/(1 + I/mR²). The second reduces to the first when I → 0, which is why frictionless sliding is the fastest possible descent.
Solution
1. On the printed smooth plane, take torques about the centre: mg acts there (zero arm) and N is radial (zero arm), so τ = 0 and α = 0 — no cylinder can start rolling.
2. Both then slide with a = g sinθ, independent of mass and of how the mass is distributed, so both reach the foot at the same instant — option (c). This is the same reason all objects fall together in vacuum: the driving force and the inertia both scale with m.
3. Discrepancy: the official key prints (a). That letter belongs to the rough version of the question, so the printed word "smooth" is the defect — the sentence also says the cylinders "roll", which a smooth plane forbids. The flagged letter is kept above.
4. Work the rough case, which is what (a) answers: solid cylinder I/mR² = ½ gives a = 2g sinθ/3 = 0.67 g sinθ.
5. Hollow cylinder (thin shell) I/mR² = 1 gives a = g sinθ/2 = 0.50 g sinθ, distinctly slower, so the solid one wins — matching (a). The hollow body carries all its mass at radius R, the worst possible place, so it must buy the most rotational KE.
6. Reject (d) either way: mass cancels from a in both the smooth and the rough analysis, so "the heavier earlier" is never true.

4A ball rotating with angular velocity ω about its centre of mass strikes a rough horizontal surface as shown in the fig. (ball falling with speed v, spin ω clockwise). After the collision the path of centre of mass of ball will be (a) a parabola to the right  (b) a parabola to the left  (c) a straight line  (d) a circle — (Level-1)Friction impulse on a spinning ball at impactHow to find the Direction of Friction in Rolling and Slipping🟡 Similaraa parabola to the right
Concept Before impact the ball only falls, so its centre of mass moves straight down — a spin about the centre carries no sideways momentum. The impact changes that, because the surface is rough: the contact patch is sliding, and sliding friction is a real horizontal force acting for the short duration of the collision. That horizontal impulse gives the centre of mass a sideways velocity it did not have before. Once the ball leaves the ground it is again a free projectile, so with a horizontal velocity plus gravity the path must be a parabola; the only thing left to decide is which way it leans.
Formula slip velocity of contact point vP = vcm + ω×r; impulse Jf = μJN, directed opposite to vP. Friction never chooses a side on its own — it simply opposes the way the contact patch is actually skidding.
Solution
1. Just before contact vcm is vertically down, so the horizontal part of vP comes entirely from the spin: vP,horiz = ω×r with r pointing from the centre down to the contact.
2. For the clockwise ω drawn in the figure this gives vP,horiz = ωR to the left: the bottom of a clockwise-spinning ball skids leftward. Check it on a car wheel — a wheel rolling right spins clockwise and its bottom is momentarily at rest because the leftward ωR cancels the rightward v.
3. Friction therefore acts to the right on the ball, and it acts for the whole contact time, delivering a rightward impulse Jf = μJN.
4. Horizontal momentum after impact: mvx = μJN ⇒ vx = μJN/m, directed right; the normal impulse simultaneously reverses the vertical velocity, so the ball leaves with vx right and vy up.
5. In flight the only force is gravity (down), so ax = 0 and ay = −g — the textbook projectile, whose trajectory is a parabola opening downward and drifting right. Hence (a).
6. Reject the rest: (b) reverses the spin sense; (c) a straight line would need zero horizontal impulse, i.e. a smooth surface; (d) a circle needs a permanent centre-seeking force, which a free ball in air does not have.

5Fig. shows an inclined surface. Half of the surface is absolutely smooth and the other half rough enough to prevent slipping. Sphere A is placed at the top on the rough part and sphere B on the smooth part at the same time. Both spheres move down the plane, then (a) A and B reach the bottom at same time  (b) B reaches bottom earlier  (c) A reaches bottom earlier  (d) which sphere will reach earlier cannot be decided — (Level-1)Rolling vs sliding down the same heightSolid sphere rolling down an incline vs a block of the same mass🟡 SimilarbB reaches bottom earlier
Concept Both spheres start from the same height h and the same rest state, so gravity hands each of them the same energy mgh. The difference is how each is allowed to spend it. Sphere B is on the frictionless lane: nothing can exert a torque about its centre, so it never spins up and every joule goes into translation. 4. Now add all four equations; every tension cancels: Mg − mg = Ma + ma + 2(I/R²)a.
5. Hence a = (M − m)g/(M + m + 2I/R²). The middle tension is never needed — it appears once with each sign and disappears.
6. The pulleys add inertia but no weight, so they can only ever slow the system, never bias it either way.

Concept Two separate conditions have to be met. At the very top the sphere must still be pressed against the track, and the critical case is N = 0, where gravity alone supplies the centripetal force — that fixes the minimum speed at the top. Then energy conservation, valid because rolling friction does no work, carries that requirement back down to the horizontal part. 1. Critical condition at the top: the track can only push inward, so the limiting case is N = 0 and gravity alone turns the sphere: mg = mvt²/(R − r) ⇒ vt² = g(R − r).
2. Kinetic energy of a rolling sphere: KE = ½mv² + ½(2/5)mr²(v/r)² = (7/10)mv², at both the bottom and the top. The sphere's own radius r cancels out of the kinetic energy, and survives only through the loop geometry R − r.
3. Energy from the horizontal part to the top, a rise of 2(R − r) for the centre: (7/10)vb² = (7/10)vt² + 2g(R − r).
4. Substitute vt²: (7/10)vb² = (7/10)g(R − r) + 2g(R − r) = g(R − r)(7/10 + 2) = (27/10)g(R − r).

6. Consistency: the friction must be large enough to keep it rolling throughout, and the track is stated as rough; the 27/7 result assumes pure rolling all the way, which is the intended reading.
