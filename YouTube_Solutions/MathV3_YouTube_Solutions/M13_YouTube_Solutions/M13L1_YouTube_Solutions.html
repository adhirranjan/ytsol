1 (i)Fill in the blanks: If the axes co-ordinate X′OX and Y′OY are not at right angle, then they are said to be ______.Oblique axes4 Oblique axes🟡 Similaroblique axes
Concept In the standard Cartesian frame the two reference lines meet at a right angle — that is what the word rectangular (or orthogonal) in “rectangular co-ordinates” means. The question strips away exactly that 90° condition, so the frame has a different name.
Formula X′OX ⊥ Y′OY ⇒ rectangular axes; angle ≠ 90° ⇒ oblique axes. Every distance / section / area formula in this chapter is derived assuming perpendicular axes, so naming the exception matters.
Solution
1. Draw the usual axes X′OX and Y′OY crossing at O; when they are perpendicular the system is called rectangular.
2. The phrase “not at right angle” removes the perpendicularity between the two axes.
3. A pair of axes inclined to each other at an angle α ≠ 90° is called oblique axes. The chapter’s opening figure shows exactly this inclined pair.
4. A point is still located by (x, y) read parallel to the two axes, but the handy Pythagoras-based distance formula no longer applies directly.
5. Hence the blank must be filled by “oblique axes”.
  Y                 Y
  |                    |____ X   vs.   ____\____ X
  O  (90 deg)     O  (angle != 90 deg)
  rectangular        oblique
⚡ JEE Trick Spot the opposite-pair vocabulary fast: rectangular/orthogonal = perpendicular axes, oblique = slanted axes. If a problem ever quotes an angle ω between the axes, remember distance becomes √(x2+y2+2xy cosω), which collapses to the familiar form only when ω = 90°.
Answer: oblique axes.
1 (ii)Fill in the blanks: If for A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2), P(x,y) is the mid-point of the line segment joining the points A & B, then x = ______ and y = ______.Mid-point formulaMid point formula Derivation - Coordinate Geometry🟡 Similarx = (x1+x2)/2,  y = (y1+y2)/2
Concept The mid-point is the point that divides a segment in the ratio 1 : 1. Putting equal weights on both ends of the section formula averages the two x-coordinates and the two y-coordinates independently, which is why the result is just the component-wise mean of A and B.
Formula internal section (m : n): ((mx2+nx1)/(m+n), (my2+ny1)/(m+n)). Mid-point is the special case m = n, where the equal weights cancel from numerator and denominator to leave a plain average — no ratio bookkeeping survives.
Solution
1. P is the mid-point, so it splits AB in the ratio 1 : 1.
2. Put m = n = 1 in the internal section formula.
3. x = (1·x2 + 1·x1)/(1+1) = (x1+x2)/2.
4. y = (1·y2 + 1·y1)/(1+1) = (y1+y2)/2.
5. So P = ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2) — each coordinate is the average of the end values. The mid-point always lies on AB and is equidistant from A and B.
6. Check on A(2,4), B(6,8): mid = (4, 6), which is visibly halfway along the segment.
7. The same averaging works in each coordinate separately, so the rule extends unchanged to 3-D as ((x1+x2)/2, (y1+y2)/2, (z1+z2)/2).
  A(x1,y1) ----- P ----- B(x2,y2)
             1  :  1
  x = (x1 + x2)/2
  y = (y1 + y2)/2
⚡ JEE Trick Average the coordinates for a mid-point; never bother with √ here. Reverse use is just as common: given a mid-point M and one end A, the other end is B = 2M − A (component-wise) — faster than re-deriving the section formula. This “double the mid, subtract the known end” move settles most parallelogram and reflection questions in one line.
Answer: x = (x1+x2)/2, y = (y1+y2)/2.
1 (iii)Fill in the blanks: The distance between the points A(2, 0) and B(0, 2) is ______.Distance formulaHow To Find The Distance Between Two Points🟡 Similar2√2
Concept The distance between two points is the length of the straight segment joining them, obtained from the Pythagoras theorem applied to the horizontal and vertical gaps between the points. Here A lies on the x-axis and B on the y-axis, so the segment is the hypotenuse of a right isosceles triangle whose two equal legs are the axes intercepts.
Formula AB = √((x2−x1)2 + (y2−y1)2). The two differences are squared, so the signs of the gaps never matter — only their magnitudes do, and swapping A and B gives the same length.
Solution
1. A(2, 0) and B(0, 2): horizontal gap Δx = 2 − 0 = 2, vertical gap Δy = 0 − 2 = −2.
2. Square and add: 22 + (−2)2 = 4 + 4 = 8.
3. Take the square root: AB = √8 = √(4·2) = 2√2.
4. Numerically 2√2 ≈ 2.828. A sits 2 units along the x-axis and B 2 units up the y-axis, so the chord is the hypotenuse of a 2–2 right triangle — the diagonal of a unit-scaled square.
5. Alternatively, O(0,0), A and B form a right isosceles triangle with legs OA = OB = 2, and the hypotenuse of such a triangle is leg×√2 = 2√2.
6. Hence the blank is 2√2.
  A(2,0), B(0,2)
  d = sqrt( (2-0)^2 + (0-2)^2 )
    = sqrt( 4 + 4 ) = sqrt 8
    = 2 sqrt 2  (approx 2.83)
⚡ JEE Trick For a point (a,0) on the x-axis and (0,a) on the y-axis the distance is always a√2 — the diagonal of a square of side a. More generally any intercept pair (a,0),(0,b) gives √(a2+b2), the length of the line’s segment between the axes. Recognising these saves running the full Pythagoras each time.
Answer: 2√2.
1 (iv)Fill in the blanks: A, B, C, D are the vertices of a ______ if the diagonals AC and BD bisect each other.Parallelogram (diagonals bisect)A quadrilateral is a parallelogram if and only if its diagonals bisect each other🟡 SimilarParallelogram
Concept A quadrilateral is a parallelogram exactly when its two diagonals cut each other into equal halves. The “bisect each other” condition forces both pairs of opposite sides to be equal and parallel.
Formula diagonals AC and BD bisect each other ⇔ mid-point of AC = mid-point of BD. Equal mid-points mean the common point splits each diagonal 1 : 1, the defining property of a parallelogram.
Solution
1. In quadrilateral ABCD the diagonals are AC and BD.
2. If they bisect each other they share a common mid-point M.
3. Then AM = MC and BM = MD, so triangles ABM and CDM are congruent (SAS).
4. Hence AB = CD and AB ∥ CD — one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel.
5. That is precisely the test for a parallelogram. A rectangle, rhombus and square are special parallelograms, but the bare “bisect each other” condition names only the parallelogram.
6. Conversely, in any parallelogram the diagonals do bisect each other, so the condition is an exact characterisation — necessary and sufficient. That two-way equivalence is why “diagonals bisect” is the quickest coordinate test for a parallelogram.
7. So the blank is “parallelogram”.
  mid(AC) = ((xA+xC)/2, (yA+yC)/2)
  mid(BD) = ((xB+xD)/2, (yB+yD)/2)
  equal  ->  diagonals bisect  ->  parallelogram
⚡ JEE Trick “Diagonals bisect each other” = parallelogram; add “diagonals equal” → rectangle; add “diagonals perpendicular” → rhombus; both → square. The coordinate test is always the single equation mid(AC) = mid(BD).
Answer: Parallelogram.

Concept Three standard formulae to recall: internal section (a plus-sign average), distance from the origin (bare Pythagoras), and external section (the same section formula with a minus sign because one weight is taken negative).
Formula internal k:ℓ → ((x2k+x1ℓ)/(k+ℓ), …); external k:ℓ → ((x2k−x1ℓ)/(k−ℓ), …). External division = internal division in ratio k : (−ℓ), which flips both signs in the denominator and numerator.
Solution
1. (A) Internal division in k : ℓ gives ((x2k+x1ℓ)/(k+ℓ), (y2k+y1ℓ)/(k+ℓ)) — the “+” option, which is (r).
2. (B) Distance of P(x, y) from O(0, 0) is √((x−0)2+(y−0)2) = √(x2+y2) — option (p).
3. (C) External division in k : ℓ gives ((x2k−x1ℓ)/(k−ℓ), (y2k−y1ℓ)/(k−ℓ)) — the “−” option, which is (q).
4. So A→r, B→p, C→q. The only difference between internal and external is the sign in front of ℓ; memorise one and flip for the other.
5. Quick check: for the mid-point (k = ℓ) the internal form gives ((x1+x2)/2, …) as expected.
  A -> r  (internal, + sign)
  B -> p  (distance from origin)
  C -> q  (external, - sign)
⚡ JEE Trick Read the denominator: k + ℓ means internal, k − ℓ means external. A negative ratio always signals external division, where P lies on the line AB but outside the segment.
Answer: A→r, B→p, C→q.

Concept For a right-angled triangle the orthocentre — the common point of the three altitudes — is the vertex that holds the right angle. The altitudes dropped from the other two vertices are the two legs themselves (each leg is perpendicular to the other), and those legs meet precisely at the right-angle corner, so no equation-solving is needed once the right angle is located.
Formula in a right triangle, orthocentre = the right-angle vertex. The two legs are perpendicular, so each leg is itself an altitude; they intersect at the right-angle corner.
Solution
1. Vertices are O(0, 0), A(3, 0) on the x-axis and B(0, 4) on the y-axis.
2. OA lies along the x-axis and OB along the y-axis, so the angle at O is 90°.
3. The triangle is right-angled at O, hence the orthocentre is O itself = (0, 0).
4. So (p, q) = (0, 0), giving p = 0 and q = 0. Altitude from A is the x-axis, altitude from B is the y-axis; both pass through O.
5. Then r = p + q + 2 = 0 + 0 + 2 = 2.

Concept A point in a plane can be fixed in two common ways: by perpendicular distances (x, y) from two axes, or by a distance-and-direction pair (r, θ) from a fixed pole and ray. The second description is the polar system.

Solution

2. The pair (r, θ) uses a radial distance r and an angle θ; this is the polar description.
3. “Linear”, “directional” and “axial” coordinates are not standard names for this pair, so they are distractors.

The area of the pentagon ABCDE is (a) 2m(m+n) (b) m(m+3n) (c) m(2m+3n) (d) 2m(m+3n)Reflections and polygon area (shoelace)Reflection of a Point on Y-Axis - Coordinates of the Image | Coordinate Geometry | ACT | JEE🟡 Similarbm(m+3n)
Concept Each reflection sends a point to a predictable image, so we track A through the four reflections to get B, C, D, E, then compute the pentagon’s area by the shoelace formula. The coordinates come out in terms of m and n only.
Formula reflect in y=x: (p,q)→(q,p); in y-axis: (p,q)→(−p,q); in x-axis: (p,q)→(p,−q); shoelace area = (1/2)|Σ(xiyi+1−xi+1yi)|. Applying the four rules in order generates a symmetric pentagon straddling all four quadrants.
Solution
1. A(m, n). B = reflect in y=x = (n, m).
2. C = reflect B in y-axis = (−n, m). D = reflect C in x-axis = (−n, −m). E = reflect D in y-axis = (n, −m).
3. Pentagon A(m,n), B(n,m), C(−n,m), D(−n,−m), E(n,−m), taken in this order around the boundary.
4. Shoelace terms: (m·m−n·n) + (n·m−(−n)m) + ((−n)(−m)−(−n)m) + ((−n)(−m)−n(−m)) + (n·n−m(−m)).
5. = (m2−n2) + 2mn + 2mn + 2mn + (n2+m2) = 2m2 + 6mn. The three middle edges each contribute 2mn; the first and last combine to 2m2.
6. Area = (1/2)(2m2 + 6mn) = m2 + 3mn = m(m + 3n) — option (b).
  A(m,n) B(n,m) C(-n,m) D(-n,-m) E(n,-m)
  shoelace sum = 2m^2 + 6mn
  area = m^2 + 3mn = m(m + 3n)
⚡ JEE Trick Chain the reflection rules mechanically — swap for y=x, flip one sign for an axis — then shoelace in boundary order. Because m, n > 0 the vertices wind consistently, so the shoelace sum needs no absolute-value juggling and gives m(m+3n) cleanly.
Answer: (b) m(m + 3n).

Concept We track P through the four reflections to get Q, R, S, T, then attempt the shoelace area. Unlike Q1, the reflection sequence here (y=x, x-axis, y-axis, y=x) produces a pentagon that crosses itself, so the “area” is ambiguous and the printed options do not match an honest computation.
Formula reflect in y=x: (p,q)→(q,p); x-axis: (p,q)→(p,−q); y-axis: (p,q)→(−p,q). A self-intersecting polygon’s shoelace sum reports a net (signed) area, not the enclosed region, which is why no clean option results.
Solution
1. P(a, b). Q = reflect in y=x = (b, a).
2. R = reflect Q in x-axis = (b, −a). S = reflect R in y-axis = (−b, −a). T = reflect S in y=x = (−a, −b).
3. Join P(a,b), Q(b,a), R(b,−a), S(−b,−a), T(−a,−b) in this order.
4. Shoelace sum = (a2−b2) − 2ab − 2ab + (b2−a2) + 0 = −4ab, giving a net area of (1/2)|−4ab| = 2ab.
5. But 2ab is not among the options; moreover the path is crossed (edge TP meets edge QR), so the figure is not a simple pentagon and no single option is correct. The companion Q1, with a different reflection order, gives a genuine simple pentagon m(m+3n) — the intended type of answer.
6. The key marks (b) b(a+3b); we keep that letter but flag the item as defective — it cannot be reproduced from the stated construction.
  P(a,b) Q(b,a) R(b,-a) S(-b,-a) T(-a,-b)
  shoelace sum = -4ab  ->  net area 2ab
  2ab is not an option ; path self-intersects
  (key (b) kept, item flagged defective)
⚡ JEE Trick Before trusting a “polygon area”, plot the vertices and check the join order does not cross itself. A crossed path makes the shoelace report a net area (here 2ab), not the visible region — a reliable sign the problem (or its key) is mis-set. The clean sibling Q1 shows what a well-posed version looks like.
Answer: key (b) b(a+3b) — ⚠ flagged: the stated reflections give a self-intersecting pentagon (net area 2ab), so no option is truly correct.
3Points P(0, 0), X(cosθ, sinθ), Y(cosφ, sinφ) lie on the unit circle and form a right-angled triangle. The right angle does not occur at the origin. Which of the following values can |sin((θ−φ)/2)| take? Concept P is the origin, which is the centre of the unit circle, and X, Y lie on that circle. So PX = PY = 1 — triangle PXY is isosceles with apex at P. An isosceles triangle cannot have a right angle at either base vertex, so a right angle “not at the origin” is impossible.
Formula PX = PY = 1 (radii) ⇒ base angles at X and Y are equal. Equal sides opposite equal angles; two equal angles cannot both be 90°.
Solution
1. P(0,0) is the centre; X(cosθ, sinθ) and Y(cosφ, sinφ) are on the unit circle, so PX = PY = 1.
2. Triangle PXY is therefore isosceles with the apex angle at P and equal base angles at X and Y.
3. A right angle “not at the origin” would have to be at X or at Y.
4. But the base angles at X and Y are equal; if one were 90° the other would be too, giving an angle sum > 180° — impossible.
5. Hence no such right-angled triangle exists, so |sin((θ−φ)/2)| cannot take any of the listed special values. The only possible right angle in this configuration is at the centre P, which the question explicitly forbids.
6. Therefore the answer is (d) none of these.
  PX = PY = 1  ->  isosceles, apex at P
  base angles at X, Y equal -> neither can be 90
  right angle off-centre impossible  ->  (d) none
⚡ JEE Trick Centre + two circle points always makes an isosceles triangle (two radii equal), so the right angle can only sit at the centre. Forbid that and the configuration is impossible — the answer is “none”, no computation needed.
Answer: (d) none of these.

5Statement-I: For the points (x, −1), (2, 1) and (4, 5) to be collinear, x = 1. Statement-II: If the area of the triangle with vertices (x, 0), (1, 1) and (0, 2) is 4 sq. units, then x = −6. Which is correct? (a) Both I and II are true (b) I true, II false (c) I false, II true (d) Both falseAssertion–Reason: collinearity and areaFind the value of x for which the points (x, – 1), (2, 1) and (4, 5) are collinear.🟢 ExactbStatement-I true, Statement-II false
Concept Evaluate each statement. Statement-I is a straightforward collinearity (slope) condition giving a unique x. Statement-II claims a single value x = −6 for an area condition that in fact has two solutions, so its stated conclusion is incomplete and therefore false.
Formula collinear ⇔ equal slopes; area = (1/2)|…| = 4 gives a |linear| = constant with two roots. An area condition is a modulus equation, so it generally yields two x-values, not one.
Solution
1. Statement-I: (x,−1), (2,1), (4,5). Slope (2,1)–(4,5) = 4/2 = 2; slope (x,−1)–(2,1) = 2/(2−x).
2. Equate: 2/(2−x) = 2 ⇒ 2−x = 1 ⇒ x = 1. So Statement-I is true.
3. Statement-II: area of (x,0),(1,1),(0,2) = (1/2)|x(1−2)+1(2−0)+0(0−1)| = (1/2)|2−x| = 4.
4. So |2 − x| = 8 ⇒ x = −6 or x = 10. The modulus gives two values; the statement names only −6, omitting x = 10.
5. Since x is not uniquely −6, Statement-II’s “then x = −6” is false as a complete conclusion.
6. Statement-I true, Statement-II false ⇒ option (b).
  I: 2/(2-x) = 2  ->  x = 1   (TRUE)
  II: |2 - x| = 8  ->  x = -6 or 10
      "x = -6" alone is incomplete  (FALSE)
  answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick An area-equals-constant condition is |linear| = k ⇒ two answers. If a statement gives just one of them as “the” value, it is false by omission — always solve the modulus both ways before judging such a claim.
Answer: (b) Statement-I true, Statement-II false.
6If the determinant |x1 y1 1; x2 y2 1; x3 y3 1| = |a1 b1 1; a2 b2 1; a3 b3 1|, then the two triangles with vertices (x1,y1), (x2,y2), (x3,y3) and (a1,b1), (a2,b2), (a3,b3) are (a) equal in area (b) similar (c) congruent (d) none of theseDeterminant equals twice the signed areaUsing determinants, find the area of the triangle with vertices (-3, 5), (3, -6) and (7, 2).🟡 Similaraequal in area
Concept The determinant with rows (xi, yi, 1) equals twice the signed area of the triangle with those vertices. If two such determinants are equal, the two triangles have equal (signed, hence also unsigned) areas — but nothing forces them to be similar or congruent.
Formula |x1 y1 1; x2 y2 1; x3 y3 1| = 2 × (signed area). Equal determinants ⇒ equal twice-areas ⇒ equal areas; shape is unconstrained.
Solution
1. For any triangle, the 3×3 determinant of its vertices (with a column of 1s) equals 2×(its signed area).
2. The two given determinants are equal, so 2×area(first) = 2×area(second).
3. Dividing by 2, area(first) = area(second).
4. So the triangles are equal in area — option (a). Equal area says nothing about matching side lengths or angles, so “similar” and “congruent” need not hold.
5. Counter-example: a 1×8 and a 2×4 rectangle’s half-triangles have equal area but are neither similar nor congruent.
6. Hence only “equal in area” is guaranteed — option (a).
  det = 2 * (signed area)
  det1 = det2  ->  area1 = area2
  shape unconstrained -> only "equal in area"
⚡ JEE Trick Read the vertex determinant as 2×area. Equality of determinants transfers to equality of areas only — resist the pull toward “congruent”. Same area, any shape, is the whole content — the determinant sees area, not form. Note a negative determinant simply means the vertices were listed clockwise; the magnitude is what equals twice the area.
Answer: (a) equal in area.

Concept For an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, the centroid coincides with the circumcentre (the circle’s centre). The centroid is the average of the three vertices, so that average equals the centre (the origin here), forcing both coordinate sums to vanish.
Formula centroid = ((Σ a cosθi)/3, (Σ a sinθi)/3) = centre (0,0). Equilateral ⇒ centroid = circumcentre; the circumcentre of a circle of these points is its own centre, the origin.
Solution
1. The vertices (a cosθi, a sinθi) lie on a circle of radius a centred at the origin.
2. For an equilateral triangle the centroid and circumcentre coincide, so the centroid is at the origin.
3. Centroid x-coordinate = (a cosθ1 + a cosθ2 + a cosθ3)/3 = 0 ⇒ cosθ1 + cosθ2 + cosθ3 = 0.
4. Centroid y-coordinate = (a sinθ1 + a sinθ2 + a sinθ3)/3 = 0 ⇒ sinθ1 + sinθ2 + sinθ3 = 0.
5. So both (a) and (b) hold. The tangent/cotangent sums (c), (d) are not forced to vanish. The angles differ by 120°, so cosines and sines each sum to zero, but tan/cot do not have that symmetry in general.
