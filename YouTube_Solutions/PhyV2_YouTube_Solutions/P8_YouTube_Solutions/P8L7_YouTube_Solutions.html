1Starting with an initial speed v₀, a mass m slides down a curved frictionless track arriving at the bottom with a speed v. From what height h did it start? — (Level-1)Energy conservation on a smooth curveBlock slides down a curved frictionless track — energy method🟡 Similarh = (v² − v₀²) / 2g
Concept The track is curved, so the normal reaction changes direction continuously and its magnitude is never given — a force–acceleration (kinematics) attack is hopeless. But the normal force is always ⟂ to the velocity, so it does zero work; the track is frictionless, so gravity is the only force doing work. That makes total mechanical energy constant, and the answer depends only on the two end states — the shape of the curve is irrelevant.
Formula Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf with K = ½mv² (J; m in kg, v in m/s) and U = mgh (J; g in m/s², h in m). Both sides are energies in joules — kg·(m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J, and kg·(m/s²)·m = J ✓ — so the equation is dimensionally sound.
Solution
1. Choose the bottom of the track as the zero of potential energy. Then Uf = 0 and Ui = mgh (J).Only differences in U matter, so any datum works; the bottom is the algebraically cheapest choice.
2. Check the forces: gravity (does work), normal reaction N (⟂ to displacement everywhere ⇒ W = 0), friction (absent). So mechanical energy is conserved.
3. Write it out: ½mv₀² + mgh = ½mv² + 0.
4. Cancel the mass m (kg) from every term — it appears in all four.This is why no mass is given in the question; the result is mass-independent.
5. ½v₀² + gh = ½v² ⇒ gh = ½(v² − v₀²).
6. h = (v² − v₀²) / (2g)Units: (m/s)² / (m/s²) = m ✓. Also note h > 0 requires v > v₀, which is exactly what sliding down must produce.

2Calculate the velocity of the bob of a simple pendulum at its mean position if it is able to rise to a vertical height of 10 cm. Given: g = 980 cm s⁻². — (Level-2)Pendulum, KE ↔ PEVelocity of the bob of a simple pendulum rising 10 cm🟢 Exact1.40 m s⁻¹ (140 cm s⁻¹)
Concept A pendulum bob is acted on by gravity and by the string tension. The tension is always along the string, i.e. perpendicular to the (circular) velocity, so it does no work. Hence all the kinetic energy at the mean (lowest) position is converted into gravitational potential energy at the extreme position, where the bob is momentarily at rest. The string length never enters — only the rise h does.
Formula ½mv² = mgh ⇒ v = √(2gh) — v in m/s, g in m/s², h in m (or keep everything in CGS: cm/s, cm/s², cm). √(m/s² × m) = √(m²/s²) = m/s ✓.
Solution
1. The book gives CGS data: g = 980 cm s⁻², h = 10 cm. Work in CGS and convert at the end (or convert first: g = 9.8 m/s², h = 0.10 m).
2. At the mean position the bob has only KE (h = 0); at the extreme it has only PE (v = 0). Tension does zero work throughout ⇒ energy conserved.
3. ½mv² = mgh ⇒ the mass m cancels ⇒ v = √(2gh).
4. Substitute (CGS): v = √(2 × 980 × 10) = √19600 = 140 cm s⁻¹.
5. Convert: 140 cm/s = 1.40 m s⁻¹.Cross-check in SI: √(2 × 9.8 × 0.10) = √1.96 = 1.4 m/s ✓ — the two systems agree, confirming no unit slip.

3A sphere of mass 4 kg strikes a spring whose force constant is 100 N m⁻¹ with a speed of 2 m s⁻¹. Find (i) the maximum compression of the spring, (ii) the kinetic energy of the sphere when the spring is compressed by 0.1 m. — (Level-3)KE → spring PEMaximum compression in a spring — spring–block system (JEE/NEET)🟡 Similar(i) 0.4 m  (ii) 7.5 J
Concept A spring is a conservative energy store: the work you do compressing it is recoverable as ½kx². Since the surface is smooth and the motion horizontal, gravity contributes nothing, so the sphere's kinetic energy is traded entirely for spring potential energy. "Maximum compression" is precisely the instant the sphere is momentarily at rest — all K has become Uspring.
Formula K = ½mv² (J; m kg, v m/s) and Uspring = ½kx² (J; k in N/m, x in m). Conservation: ½mv² = ½kx²max, and at an intermediate compression x: K(x) = ½mv² − ½kx². N/m × m² = N·m = J ✓, so both stores are in joules and may be added.
Solution
1. Initial kinetic energy: K = ½ × 4 kg × (2 m/s)² = ½ × 4 × 4 = 8 J.
2. (i) At maximum compression the sphere is momentarily at rest, so all 8 J sits in the spring:
  ½ k x²max = 8 ⇒ ½ × 100 × x²max = 8 ⇒ 50 x²max = 8.
3. x²max = 8/50 = 0.16 m² ⇒ xmax = 0.4 m.Units: √(J / (N/m)) = √(N·m·m/N) = m ✓.
4. (ii) At x = 0.1 m the spring stores U = ½ × 100 × (0.1)² = ½ × 100 × 0.01 = 0.5 J.
5. Energy is conserved, so the kinetic energy left over is K = 8 J − 0.5 J = 7.5 J.Sanity: 0.1 m is only a quarter of xmax, and U ∝ x², so only (1/4)² = 1/16 of the energy — 0.5 J of 8 J — has been absorbed. Most of the KE is still there ✓.
   v = 2 m/s
   ┌────┐ →              k = 100 N/m
   │ 4kg│  ─────/\/\/\/\/\/\/─────│wall
   └────┘
   ├── x ──┤

   K₀ = ½(4)(2)²          = 8 J
   (i)  ½kx²  = 8   ⇒ x = √(16/100) = 0.4 m

⚡ JEE Trick Learn the compact form xmax = v√(m/k) — here 2√(4/100) = 2 × 0.2 = 0.4 m in one step, no squaring. 4Two inclined frictionless tracks, one gradual and the other steep, meet at A from where two stones are allowed to slide down from rest, one on each track. Will the stones reach the bottom at the same time? Will they reach there with the same speed? Explain. Given θ₁ = 30°, θ₂ = 60° and h = 10 m, what are the speeds and times taken by the two stones? [Take g = 10 m s⁻²] — (Level-4)Same speed, different timesTwo inclined frictionless tracks meet at A — NCERT Work, Energy & Power🟢 ExactSame speed 14.14 m s⁻¹; t₁ = 2√2 s ≈ 2.83 s, t₂ = 2√(2/3) s ≈ 1.63 s
Concept This is the classic "energy is path-independent, time is not" question. Because both tracks are frictionless and both stones fall through the same height h, energy conservation forces the speeds at the bottom to be equal. But the accelerations differ (a = g sinθ) and the path lengths differ (L = h/sinθ), so the times differ — the steeper track wins.
Formula Speed: v = √(2gh) (m/s). Along-incline acceleration: a = g sinθ (m/s²). Slope length: L = h / sinθ (m). Kinematics from rest: L = ½ a t² ⇒ t = (1/sinθ)·√(2h/g) (s). √(m ÷ m/s²) = √(s²) = s ✓, and the 1/sinθ factor is dimensionless.
Solution
1. Same speed? Yes. Normal reaction ⟂ velocity (no work), no friction, so ½mv² = mgh for both stones ⇒ v = √(2gh), independent of θ.
2. v = √(2 × 10 × 10) = √200 = 10√2 = 14.14 m s⁻¹ for both stones.
3. Same time? No. Derive t: L = ½at² with L = h/sinθ and a = g sinθ ⇒ h/sinθ = ½ (g sinθ) t² ⇒ t² = 2h/(g sin²θ) ⇒ t = (1/sinθ)√(2h/g).
4. Common factor: √(2h/g) = √(2×10/10) = √2 s.
5. Gradual track, θ₁ = 30°, sin30° = ½: t₁ = √2 / (1/2) = 2√2 s ≈ 2.83 s.
6. Steep track, θ₂ = 60°, sin60° = √3/2: t₂ = √2 ÷ (√3/2) = 2√2/√3 = 2√(2/3) s ≈ 1.63 s.t₁/t₂ = sin60°/sin30° = √3 — the gradual stone takes √3 ≈ 1.73 times longer, matching 2.83/1.63 ✓.

5The ends of a spring are attached to blocks of mass 3 kg and 2 kg. The 2 kg mass must be compressed further by at least ______, so that when it is released, the 3 kg block may be lifted off the ground. — (Level-5)Spring launch, lift-off conditionEnds of spring attached to 3 kg and 2 kg blocks — compress further to lift 3 kg🟢 Exact2.5 cm
Concept Two separate ideas are stitched together. (1) The initial 1 cm compression is a statics fact — it calibrates the spring constant k from the 2 kg weight. (2) The lift-off condition for the lower block is a statics fact too: the spring must be stretched enough that its upward pull on the 3 kg block equals 3g. Between them sits an energy calculation: the extra compression must store enough energy to carry the 2 kg block from the compressed state up to that critical extension.
Formula Equilibrium: k x₀ = m₂ g (N; k in N/m, x₀ in m). Lift-off: k e = m₃ g ⇒ e in m. Energy from the released position (compression x) to the extension e: ½kx² = ½ke² + m₂ g (x + e) — every term in joules. The travel of the 2 kg block from compression x to extension e is (x + e) m, so its gravity term is m₂g(x+e) J ✓.
Solution
1. Calibrate k. In equilibrium the spring (compressed 1 cm = 0.01 m) supports the 2 kg block: k(0.01) = 2 × 10 ⇒ k = 2000 N/m.Take g = 10 m/s² — the answer 2.5 cm comes out exactly with it. (With g = 9.8, k = 1960 N/m and every length scales identically, giving the same 2.5 cm.)
2. Lift-off condition for the 3 kg block: the spring must pull up with 3g = 30 N ⇒ extension e = 30/2000 = 0.015 m = 1.5 cm.Below this the normal reaction from the floor is still positive, so the block stays put.
3. Let the total compression at release be x (m). The 2 kg block travels a distance (x + e) upward to reach the critical extension, arriving with (at least) zero speed.
4. Energy: ½kx² = ½ke² + m₂g(x + e)
  1000x² = 1000(0.015)² + 20(x + 0.015)
5. 1000x² = 0.225 + 20x + 0.300 ⇒ 1000x² − 20x − 0.525 = 0.
6. x = [20 ± √(400 + 4·1000·0.525)] / 2000 = [20 ± √2500]/2000 = (20 + 50)/2000 = 0.035 m = 3.5 cm (reject the negative root).
7. The spring was already compressed 1 cm, so the further compression required is 3.5 − 1.0 = 2.5 cm.

1What does the principle of conservation of mechanical energy state? (a) Total energy always increases in a system (b) Only potential energy is conserved (c) Mechanical energy remains constant if no external force acts (d) Kinetic energy is always conserved — (Level-1)Statement of the principleConservation of Mechanical Energy — Class 11 Work, Power, Energy🟡 SimilarcMechanical energy remains constant if no external force acts
Concept Mechanical energy means KE + gravitational/elastic PE only — not heat, not sound, not chemical energy. It is conserved when the only forces doing work are conservative ones. The book states the condition as "no external forces act", and carefully explains that weight does not count as an external force (it is already accounted for inside U = mgh).
Formula E = K + U = ½mv² + mgh (+ ½kx²) = constant — all terms in joules (m in kg, v in m/s, g in m/s², h and x in m, k in N/m). Every term reduces to kg·m²/s² = J, so they may legitimately be summed to a single conserved number.
Solution
1. Test (a): "total energy always increases" — this violates the conservation of energy itself. Wrong.
2. Test (b): "only potential energy is conserved" — false; PE alone changes freely (a falling ball loses PE every instant). It is the sum K + U that is fixed. Wrong.
3. Test (d): "kinetic energy is always conserved" — false for the same reason: the falling ball gains KE. KE alone is conserved only in an elastic collision, a different statement. Wrong.
4. Test (c): mechanical energy K + U stays constant provided no external (i.e. non-conservative / applied) force does work — no friction, no engine, no push. This is exactly the book's statement. Correct.
5. Note the book's three explicit riders: (i) there is no friction, (ii) no "extra" force such as an engine or a person pushing, (iii) weight does NOT count as an external force.Weight is already inside U = mgh, so counting it twice would be an error.

        ↓ ball falls
   K ▁▂▄▆█      U █▆▄▂▁
   ─────────    ─────────
   E = K + U = ████████  (unchanged)

   valid only if:  no friction
                   no engine / applied push
                   (weight is NOT "external")
⚡ JEE Trick Screen every option in one pass by asking "does this claim one energy is conserved, or the sum?" Only the sum-statement can be right. Trap: option (d) sounds respectable because of "conservation of kinetic energy" in elastic collisions — but that is a special case of collisions, not the mechanical-energy principle.
Answer: (c) Mechanical energy remains constant if no external force acts.
2Which of the following is not considered an external force in the context of mechanical energy conservation? (a) Friction (b) Engine force (c) Push from a person (d) Gravitational weight — (Level-1)What counts as "external"Conservation of Mechanical Energy — full concept & derivation (Ch-6)🟡 SimilardGravitational weight
Concept The word "external" here is bookkeeping, not geography. Gravity is conservative, so its work has already been packaged into the potential-energy term mgh. Friction, an engine and a human push are all forces whose work is not stored in any U term — they add or remove mechanical energy, so they must be declared "external".
Formula Work–energy split: Wconservative + Wother = ΔK (J), and by definition Wconservative = −ΔU, giving Wother = ΔK + ΔU = ΔE. ΔE is in joules; when Wother = 0, E is constant — this single line is the conservation theorem.
Solution
1. Ask of each force: "can its work be written as −ΔU for some potential-energy function?"
2. Friction (a): work depends on the path length, not just the endpoints ⇒ no potential function exists ⇒ external, and it always drains E.
3. Engine force (b) and human push (c): supplied from outside (fuel, muscles), arbitrary in magnitude and direction ⇒ external, they pump E in.
4. Gravitational weight (d): W = −mg(hf − hi) depends only on the endpoints ⇒ conservative ⇒ already inside U = mgh.Counting weight as "external" would double-count it: once in U and once as Wother.
5. Hence the force that is not external is gravitational weight — option (d), which is exactly the book's bullet "weight does NOT count as an external force".
   force        path-dependent?   has a U ?   external?
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   friction         YES             no          YES
   engine           YES             no          YES
   person's push    YES             no          YES
   weight (mg)      NO (endpoints)  U = mgh     NO  ← answer
⚡ JEE Trick One-line test: if you can write a potential energy for it, it is internal/conservative — gravity (mgh), spring (½kx²), electrostatics (kq₁q₂/r). Everything else (friction, viscous drag, engines, hands) is external. Trap: (a) friction feels "internal" because it acts at the contact surface, but its path-dependence disqualifies it instantly.
Answer: (d) Gravitational weight — it is conservative and is already represented by the mgh term.
3In problems based on mechanical energy conservation, where should potential energy be taken as zero? (a) At infinity (b) At the highest point (c) At the point where calculations are easy (d) Always at the surface — (Level-1)Choice of PE datumConservation of Mechanical Energy (Lecture 7) — Jha Sir, JEE/NEET🟡 SimilarcAt the point where calculations are easy
Concept Potential energy is defined only up to an arbitrary additive constant; physics depends on ΔU, never on U itself. Shifting the datum by a height h₀ adds the same mgh₀ to every state, and it cancels out of every equation. So the datum is a free choice — pick whatever kills the most terms.
Formula U = mgh + C (J), where C is any constant (J). Conservation uses ΔU = Uf − Ui = mg(hf − hi). C appears in both Uf and Ui and subtracts away, so joules on both sides are unaffected by the choice.
Solution
1. Suppose you shift the datum down by h₀ (m). Then every height becomes h + h₀ and every U becomes mgh + mgh₀.
2. In Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf, the extra mgh₀ (J) sits on both sides and cancels identically ⇒ the physics is unchanged.
3. Therefore (a), (b) and (d) are all legal but none is required. Each is merely convenient in a special context — infinity for gravitation/electrostatics, the surface for projectile problems.
4. Option (c) states the general rule the book gives: "Decide where to take potential energy as zero. This makes it easier … that we always measure from the same place."The one non-negotiable requirement is consistency: the same datum for the initial and the final state.
5. Hence (c).
   choose datum at the bottom          choose datum at the top
   ────────────────────────           ────────────────────────
   U_top = mgh                        U_top = 0
   U_bot = 0                          U_bot = −mgh
   ΔU    = −mgh                       ΔU    = −mgh   ← identical
⚡ JEE Trick Put the datum at the lowest point the body actually reaches: then every U is ≥ 0 and no sign errors creep in. In spring problems, put it at the block's final position so one whole term vanishes. Trap: (d) "always at the surface" is a school habit, not a law — in a mine-shaft or a pulley problem it makes the algebra worse.
Answer: (c) At the point where calculations are easy (the datum is arbitrary — only consistency matters).

5In a spring-block system, the force acting on the block is: (a) Only gravitational force (b) Frictional force (c) Only spring force (d) Centripetal force — (Level-1)Ideal spring–block systemSpring–block system — work, power and energy for JEE/NEET🟡 SimilarcOnly spring force
Concept The book's model (used to derive ΔKE = ½k(x₁² − x₂²)) is a block on a horizontal frictionless table attached to an ideal spring. Vertically, weight and normal reaction cancel exactly; horizontally there is no friction and no applied push. So the only force that does work — the only force in the equation of motion along x — is the spring force.
Formula Hooke's law F = −kx (N; k in N/m, x in m measured from the natural length), and the work done by the spring moving from x₁ to x₂: W = ½k(x₁² − x₂²) (J). By the work–energy theorem W = ΔKE ⇒ ½m(v₂² − v₁²) = ½k(x₁² − x₂²). (N/m)·m² = J on the right and kg·(m/s)² = J on the left ✓.
Solution
1. Draw the free body: weight mg down, normal reaction N up, spring force −kx horizontal.
2. Vertical: N − mg = 0 ⇒ N = mg. These two cancel and, being ⟂ to the motion, do zero work.
3. Horizontal: the surface is frictionless, so there is no friction term; nothing else touches the block.

Concept The word "slowly" is the key: the process is quasi-static, so the kinetic energy is zero at both ends and ΔK = 0. The agent's work therefore appears entirely as stored energy — some of it as the upper ball's gravitational PE, the rest as elastic PE in the stretched thread. Formula Quasi-static work: Wagent = ΔUgravity + ΔUelastic = m g Δh + ½ k e² (J; m in kg, g in m/s², Δh and e in m, k in N/m). kg·m/s²·m = J and (N/m)·m² = J ✓, so the two stores add cleanly.
Solution
1. How far does the upper ball rise? The thread goes from 0.40 m to 1.00 m while the lower ball stays put, so the upper ball rises Δh = 0.60 m.
2. Gravitational PE gained by the upper ball: ΔUg = m g Δh = 0.06 × 10 × 0.60 = 0.36 J.
3. Elastic PE stored in the thread (using k = 1 N/m from Q5, extension e = 0.60 m):
  ΔUe = ½ k e² = ½ × 1 × (0.60)² = ½ × 0.36 = 0.18 J.
4. The lower ball has not moved and has no kinetic energy, so it stores nothing.
5. Raised "slowly" ⇒ ΔK = 0 ⇒ the agent's work equals the total stored energy:
  Wagent = 0.36 + 0.18 = 0.54 J ⇒ option (a).Note the decoys: (b) 0.36 J is the gravity term alone and (c) 0.18 J is the elastic term alone — the question wants their sum.

Concept An Atwood machine solved by energy rather than by Newton's laws. Two facts do all the work: (i) the string is inextensible, so both masses move with the same speed v — the kinetic energy is ½(m₁+m₂)v², not ½m₁v²; (ii) as m₁ descends h, m₂ rises the same h, so the net PE released is only (m₁ − m₂)gh. Put those together and the answer writes itself.
Formula (m₁ − m₂) g h = ½ (m₁ + m₂) v² (J; m in kg, g in m/s², h in m, v in m/s) ⇒ v = √(2gh(m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)). Inside the root: (m/s²)(m)(kg/kg) = m²/s², whose square root is m/s ✓.
Solution
1. Because the string is inextensible and the pulley ideal, when m₁ falls h the lighter mass m₂ rises exactly h, and at every instant |v₁| = |v₂| = v.
2. Energy released by the descending mass: +m₁ g h (J).
3. Energy consumed by the rising mass: −m₂ g h (J).
4. Net gravitational energy released: (m₁ − m₂) g h (J), which is positive because m₂ < m₁.
5. This appears as the kinetic energy of both blocks (the pulley and string are massless, tension is internal and does no net work):
  (m₁ − m₂)gh = ½(m₁ + m₂)v².
6. Solve: v = √(2gh(m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)) ⇒ option (a).Two limiting checks: if m₂ = 0 it reduces to v = √(2gh), plain free fall ✓; if m₂ → m₁ it gives v → 0, a balanced Atwood that does not move ✓.
              ○ pulley

       ┌───┐ │ ┌────┐
       │m₂ │ │ │ m₁ │   (m₂ < m₁)
       └───┘ │ └────┘
         ↑ h      ↓ h
       ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  floor

   net PE released = (m₁ − m₂)gh
   KE gained       = ½(m₁ + m₂)v²      ← BOTH masses move
   v = √( 2gh(m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) )

⚡ JEE Trick Equivalently derive it in one line from a = g(m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂) and v² = 2ah — same answer, and it shows why the mass factor appears exactly once inside the root. Always run the two limiting checks (m₂ = 0 and m₂ = m₁) on symbolic options; here they kill (b), (c) and (d) instantly. Trap: option (b) √(2gh) forgets that m₂ is being lifted; options (c) and (d) misplace which mass sits in the numerator.

Concept The stored energy sets a hard ceiling on the speed: nothing can move faster than the value obtained when all of ½kx² has become kinetic energy. 10Multiple Correct Answer Question. A block hangs freely from the end of a spring. A boy then slowly pushes the block upwards so that the spring becomes strain free. The gain in gravitational potential energy of the block during this process is not equal to: (a) the work done by the boy against the gravitational force acting on the block (b) the loss of energy stored in the spring minus the work done by the tension in the spring (c) the work done on the block by the boy plus the loss of energy stored in the spring (d) the work done on the block by the boy minus the work done by the tension in the spring plus the loss of energy stored in the spring (e) the work done on the block by the boy minus the work done by the tension in the spring — (Level-6)Energy accounting, quasi-staticA block hangs freely from a spring; a boy slowly pushes it up until the spring is strain free🟢 Exacta,b,d,eonly (c) is equal to the PE gain — a, b, d, e are not
Concept This is a pure energy-bookkeeping question, and the way to win it is to compute all four quantities explicitly in terms of one symbol, then test each statement arithmetically. The physical content is that the boy does not do all the lifting — the stretched spring is pulling upward too, and it hands over its stored energy as it relaxes. In fact the spring and the boy contribute exactly half each.
Formula Initial extension (equilibrium): x₀ = mg/k (m). Gravitational gain: ΔUg = m g x₀ (J). Work by the spring on the block as it relaxes: WT = −ΔUspring = +½ k x₀² (J). Quasi-static (ΔK = 0): Wboy + WT + Wgravity = 0 (J). Every term is in joules; the block is moved "slowly", so no kinetic energy is created or destroyed ✓.
Solution
1. Initially the block hangs in equilibrium, so kx₀ = mg ⇒ x₀ = mg/k, and the block must be raised exactly x₀ for the spring to reach its natural length.
2. ΔUg = mg x₀ — call this quantity Q for brevity.
3. Energy stored in the spring initially: ½k x₀² = ½(mg/x₀)x₀² = ½mg x₀ = Q/2. It ends at zero, so the loss of spring energy = Q/2.
4. Work done by the tension (spring force) on the block: the spring pulls up while the block moves up, so this is positive and equals the energy the spring gives away: WT = +Q/2.
5. Work done by the boy: from Wboy + WT + Wgravity = 0 with Wgravity = −Q:
  Wboy = Q − WT = Q − Q/2 = Q/2. The boy supplies only half the lift; the spring supplies the other half.
6. Now test each statement against ΔUg = Q:
  (a) work done by the boy = Q/2 ≠ Q ⇒ not equal ✓ (in the answer).
  (b) loss of spring energy − WT = Q/2 − Q/2 = 0 ≠ Q ⇒ not equal ✓.
  (c) Wboy + loss of spring energy = Q/2 + Q/2 = Q ⇒ EQUAL — so (c) is excluded.
  (d) Wboy − WT + loss = Q/2 − Q/2 + Q/2 = Q/2 ≠ Q ⇒ not equal ✓.
  (e) Wboy − WT = Q/2 − Q/2 = 0 ≠ Q ⇒ not equal ✓.
7. The statements that are not equal to the gain in gravitational PE are therefore (a), (b), (d) and (e).The single true accounting is (c): the block's rise in PE is paid for jointly by the boy and by the spring's released energy — Q/2 + Q/2 = Q.
