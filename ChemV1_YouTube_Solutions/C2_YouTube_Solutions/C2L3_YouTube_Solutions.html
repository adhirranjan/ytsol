1Minimum frequency of incident radiation to just eject an electron is called?Threshold frequencyA-CHAR🟡 SimilarbThreshold frequency
Concept In the photoelectric effect an electron is only knocked out of a metal if the incoming photon carries enough energy to overcome the metal's binding energy, called the work function W₀. Because a photon's energy is fixed by its frequency (E = hν), there is a single minimum frequency below which no photon — however many arrive — can free an electron. That cut-off frequency is given its own name.
Formula hν₀ = W₀, so ν₀ = W₀ ÷ h: the threshold frequency ν₀ (in Hz, s⁻¹) is the work function W₀ (J) divided by Planck's constant h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s. At ν = ν₀ the photon energy exactly equals W₀, so the electron just escapes with zero kinetic energy.
Solution
1. Emission needs photon energy ≥ work function: hν ≥ W₀.Anything less leaves the electron still bound to the metal.
2. The smallest frequency that still satisfies this is the equality hν = W₀, i.e. ν = ν₀.
3. This special minimum frequency is defined as the threshold frequency ν₀ — matching option (b).
4. Rule out distractors: "critical", "resonance" and "cut-off potential" are not the standard term for this minimum ejection frequency.
⚡ JEE Trick Tie the three "minimum" quantities together: minimum frequency = threshold frequency ν₀; the matching maximum wavelength = threshold wavelength λ₀ = c/ν₀; and hν₀ = W₀. Trap: below ν₀ raising the intensity (more photons) still gives zero emission — energy per photon, not photon count, is what must clear W₀.
Answer: (b) Threshold frequency.

3Why does photoelectric current stop rising even as intensity increases (saturation)?Saturation currentA-CHAR🟡 SimilarcAll emitted electrons are already being collected by the anode.
Concept Photoelectric current is set by two things: how many electrons the light ejects per second, and what fraction of them actually reach the collecting anode. As the anode voltage (or intensity) is pushed up, the current first climbs but then flattens to a constant saturation current. Saturation is reached when the collector is already gathering every electron the surface emits — there are simply no more to collect.
Key relation i = (electrons emitted per second) × e, and emission rate ∝ intensity for a fixed frequency. Once all emitted electrons are collected, raising the accelerating voltage cannot increase the count, so i stays fixed at i_sat.
Solution
1. For light of fixed frequency, the number of photoelectrons released per second is fixed by the intensity.
2. As collecting voltage rises, a larger share of those electrons reaches the anode, so current grows — at first.
3. Beyond a point, 100% of the emitted electrons are already reaching the anode; none are being lost.You cannot collect more than are emitted, so the current has nowhere higher to go.
4. Hence the current levels off (saturates) — option (c). It is not because emission stops or the metal is exhausted.
⚡ JEE Trick Saturation current is set by intensity (photon arrival rate), while stopping potential is set by frequency (energy per photon) — keep the two axes separate. Trap: a higher intensity raises the saturation level (more electrons/second) but does not change the shape of the plateau — the plateau still means "all emitted electrons collected."
Answer: (c) All emitted electrons are already being collected by the anode.

1What is a quantum of energy?Planck's quantumA-QUANTUM🟡 Similar—
Concept Before Planck, energy was assumed to flow continuously, in any amount. Planck's radical proposal was that a body emits or absorbs radiation not smoothly but in tiny, indivisible packets. Each such smallest packet of radiant energy is called a quantum (plural: quanta); for light the quantum is the photon. This idea of discreteness is the seed of all quantum theory and directly explains the photoelectric effect.
Key relation E = hν = hc/λ: the energy of one quantum is Planck's constant h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s times the frequency ν (Hz), equivalently hc/λ. Energy is exchanged only in whole-number multiples of this quantum — Etotal = nhν, with n a positive integer.
Solution
1. State the postulate: radiant energy is emitted/absorbed in discrete packets, not continuously.This was Planck's fix for black-body radiation, which classical physics could not explain.
2. Define the packet: the smallest such indivisible amount is one quantum, of energy E = hν.
3. Note the consequence: a system can only gain or lose energy in integer multiples nhν — never a fraction of a quantum.
4. For light specifically, this quantum is a photon, whose energy grows with frequency.
⚡ JEE Trick "Quantum" = smallest indivisible packet; "photon" = that packet for light. Remember E = hν scales with frequency, so a UV quantum carries more energy than a visible one. Trap: energy comes only in whole quanta (nhν) — an atom cannot absorb 1.5 photons' worth.
Key point: a quantum is the smallest discrete packet of energy that can be emitted or absorbed, of magnitude E = hν = hc/λ; for electromagnetic radiation it is the photon.
2Explain the photoelectric effectPhotoelectric effectA-PEE🟡 Similar—
Concept The photoelectric effect is the ejection of electrons from a metal surface when light of sufficiently high frequency shines on it. It could not be explained by the wave picture of light and was the decisive evidence for light's particle nature: light delivers energy in discrete photons, each interacting one-to-one with a single electron. Einstein's explanation of it won him the Nobel Prize.
Key relation hν = W₀ + K_max (Einstein's equation), with W₀ = hν₀ the work function and K_max the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electron. A single photon gives all its energy hν to one electron; part (W₀) frees it, the rest becomes kinetic energy.
Solution — key observations
1. Emission occurs only if ν ≥ ν₀ (threshold frequency); below ν₀ no electrons come out no matter how intense the light.Each photon must individually carry at least W₀; low-frequency photons are too weak.
2. The maximum KE of emitted electrons rises with frequency ν, and is independent of intensity.
3. The number of electrons (photocurrent) rises with intensity (more photons per second), but intensity does not change their KE.
4. Emission is essentially instantaneous — no time lag — because the one-photon/one-electron transfer is immediate.
⚡ JEE Trick Split every photoelectric fact along two axes: frequency → energy/KE/threshold; intensity → number/current. Trap: the classical wave prediction (brighter light ⇒ more KE, with a time lag) is exactly what experiment disproves — do not mix it into the explanation.
Key point: the photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal when struck by light of frequency ≥ threshold; it obeys hν = W₀ + K_max and demonstrates the particle (photon) nature of light.
3Calculate the energy associated with a radiation of wavelength 10 ÅPhoton energy E=hc/λA-PHOTON🟡 Similar—
Concept A photon's energy is set entirely by its wavelength through E = hc/λ — shorter wavelength means higher energy. Here the wavelength is given in ångström (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m), so the only real work is a careful unit conversion followed by one division. 10 Å lies in the X-ray region, so we should expect a fairly large photon energy.
Formula E = hc ÷ λ: h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.0×10⁸ m s⁻¹, λ in metres, E in joules. hc packages the two constants; dividing by λ gives energy — small λ ⇒ large E. To convert to eV, divide joules by 1.602×10⁻¹⁹.
Solution
1. Convert wavelength: λ = 10 Å = 10 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 1.0×10⁻⁹ m.
2. Form the constant hc = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(3.0×10⁸) = 1.988×10⁻²⁵ J·m.6.626 × 3.0 = 19.88, and 10⁻³⁴×10⁸ = 10⁻²⁶, giving 19.88×10⁻²⁶ = 1.988×10⁻²⁵.
3. Divide by λ: E = 1.988×10⁻²⁵ ÷ 1.0×10⁻⁹ = 1.988×10⁻¹⁶ J.10⁻²⁵ ÷ 10⁻⁹ = 10⁻¹⁶; the coefficient 1.988 is unchanged since λ's coefficient is 1.
4. Convert to eV: E = 1.988×10⁻¹⁶ ÷ 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.24×10³ eV ≈ 1.24 keV.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm: then E(eV) = 1240 ÷ λ(nm). Here λ = 1 nm ⇒ E ≈ 1240 eV instantly — no long constant multiplication. Trap: forgetting 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m (not 10⁻⁹) throws the answer off by a factor of 10.

4Explain the line emission spectrum of hydrogenHydrogen spectrumA-HSPEC🟡 Similar—
Concept When hydrogen gas is energised (heated or by electric discharge), its electrons jump to higher energy levels and then fall back, emitting light. Crucially the emitted light is not a continuous rainbow but a set of sharp, discrete coloured lines at fixed wavelengths. This "line spectrum" is direct proof that the electron's energy inside the atom is quantised — only certain energy levels are allowed.
Key relation ΔE = Ehigher − Elower = hν = hc/λ, and the wavenumbers follow 1/λ = RH(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) (Rydberg). Each line corresponds to one specific downward jump n₂ → n₁, so its wavelength is fixed by the energy gap between just those two levels.
Solution
1. Energy input promotes the electron from the ground level to higher allowed levels (excitation).
2. The excited electron is unstable and drops back to a lower level, releasing the energy difference as one photon.Energy is conserved: the photon carries exactly ΔE between the two levels.
3. Because only fixed levels exist, only fixed ΔE values — hence fixed wavelengths — appear: a spectrum of discrete lines, not a continuum.
4. Transitions grouped by the final level form series: n→1 Lyman (UV), n→2 Balmer (visible), n→3 Paschen (IR), etc.
⚡ JEE Trick Discrete lines ⇒ quantised levels; the series is named by the lower level (n₁ = 1 Lyman, 2 Balmer, 3 Paschen). Only the Balmer series (n₁=2) is visible. Trap: a line emission spectrum shows bright lines on a dark background; do not confuse it with a continuous or absorption spectrum.
Key point: the hydrogen line spectrum is a set of discrete wavelengths emitted as electrons fall between quantised energy levels (ΔE = hc/λ, Rydberg 1/λ = RH(1/n₁²−1/n₂²)), proving energy levels are quantised.
