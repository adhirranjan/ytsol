1In a vertical circular motion, when is the kinetic energy maximum? (a) At the top of the circle (b) At the midpoint (c) At the bottom of the circle (d) It remains constant — (Level-1)Where KE is maximum11 chap 4 || Circular Motion 06 || Motion in a Vertical Circle IIT JEE / NEET || Critical Velocity🟡 SimilarcAt the bottom of the circle
Concept In a vertical circle the bob feels only tension, directed along the string, and gravity. Tension is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does zero work — every change of speed is gravity's doing. Mechanical energy is therefore conserved, so KE is largest exactly where PE is smallest: the lowest point.
Formula WT = 0 since T ⊥ v, hence ½mv² + mgh = ½mvb² with h from the lowest point, i.e. KE(h) = ½mvb² − mgh. Conservation is legal only because the one force with a component along v is gravity, which is conservative.
Solution
1. T lies along the radius, v is tangential, so T·v = 0 — tension can never change the speed.This is why a vertical circle is non-uniform circular motion: gravity alone speeds it up and slows it down.
2. Energy from the bottom: ½mv² + mgh = ½mvb² ⇒ KE(h) = ½mvb² − mgh.
3. KE(h) is a line of slope −mg, strictly decreasing, so maximum KE sits at minimum h.A monotonic function takes its extremes at the ends of the range 0 ≤ h ≤ 2r — no calculus needed.
4. h = 0: KE = ½mvb² (largest). h = 2r: KE = ½mvb² − 2mgr (smallest).
5. Equivalently vb² − vt² = 4gr > 0 always, so the bottom speed beats the top speed however fast the bob is launched.
6. Options at the critical launch vb = √(5gr): bottom 2.5mgr, side 1.5mgr, top 0.5mgr — (a) is the minimum, (b) is intermediate, (d) needs constant KE, so (c) stands.

 bottom     0      0           2.50 mgr   <-- MAX

 top        2r     2mgr        0.50 mgr   <-- min

 KE falls monotonically as h rises => max at h = 0
⚡ JEE Trick Option (d) is the planted trap: KE is constant for horizontal uniform circular motion, but a vertical circle always has a tangential component of gravity. The mental test is vb² − vt² = 4gr, a fixed non-zero gap. For any "where is X maximum" vertical-circle question: bottom for KE, speed and tension; top for PE.
Answer: (c) At the bottom of the circle.

Concept Tension in a vertical circle must supply the centripetal force mv²/r and cope with the radial part of gravity. At the bottom gravity pulls away from the centre so the string does both jobs and T is maximum; at the top gravity points towards the centre and helps, so T is minimum. Energy conservation links the two speeds and the ratio comes out mass- and radius-free.
Formula Bottom Tmax − mg = mvb²/r; top Tmin + mg = mvt²/r; energy vt² = vb² − 4gr. The sign flip on mg is the whole problem — at the top the weight already points at the centre.
Solution
1. Given vb² = 7gr > 5gr, so the loop is completed and the string stays taut throughout.Below 5gr the string would go slack part-way and Tmin would be 0, not a top-point value.
2. Tmax = mvb²/r + mg = m(7gr)/r + mg = 7mg + mg = 8mg.
3. Top speed by energy (rise 2r): vt² = 7gr − 4gr = 3gr.Tension does no work, so only the height drop enters — the path in between is irrelevant.
4. Tmin = mvt²/r − mg = 3mg − mg = 2mg.
5. Ratio Tmax : Tmin = 8mg : 2mg = 4 : 1; m, g and r all cancel.
6. Audit with the universal identity Tmax − Tmin = 6mg: 8mg − 2mg = 6mg ✓.That 6mg gap holds for every vertical circle whatever the launch speed, so it checks both answers at once.

Answer: 4 : 1, with Tmax = 8mg at the lowest point and Tmin = 2mg at the highest.
3The bob of a pendulum at rest is given a sharp hit to impart a horizontal velocity u = √(3lg) where l is the length of the pendulum. Find the angle rotated by the string before it becomes slack. — (Level-3)Angle turned before the string goes slackThe bob of a stationary pendulum is given a sharp hit to impart it a horizontal speed of sqrt(3gl) - angle before string goes slack🟢 Exactπ − cos−1(1/3), i.e. cosθ = −1/3
Concept A string can pull but never push, so it goes slack the instant the required tension would turn negative. With u² = 3gl the launch sits in the band 2gl < u² < 5gl: the bob climbs past the horizontal but cannot reach the top, so T hits zero in the upper quadrant and the bob becomes a projectile. Locating that instant means solving T = 0 alongside energy conservation.
Formula v² = u² − 2gl(1 − cosθ) and T = mv²/l + mg cosθ, θ measured from the lowest point. cosθ carries its own sign, +1 at the bottom and −1 at the top, so one formula covers the whole circle.
Solution
1. Band test: 2gl < 3gl < 5gl, so a slack point above the horizontal really exists.Under 2gl the bob just oscillates and over 5gl it loops — in both cases T never vanishes.
2. At angle θ, h = l(1 − cosθ), so v² = 3gl − 2gl(1 − cosθ) = gl(1 + 2cosθ).
3. Slack means T = 0, so mv²/l = −mg cosθ, i.e. v² = −gl cosθ.A positive v² forces cosθ < 0, so the algebra itself insists the release is above the horizontal.
4. Equate: gl(1 + 2cosθ) = −gl cosθ ⇒ 1 + 3cosθ = 0 ⇒ cosθ = −1/3.
5. Hence θ = cos−1(−1/3) = π − cos−1(1/3) ≈ 109.5°, about 19.5° above the horizontal.
6. Check: v² = gl/3 > 0 and h = 4l/3 < 2l, so the bob is genuinely short of the top.Both checks fail if the sign of cosθ is mishandled, so they are worth ten seconds.

1A body of mass m attached to a thread is revolved along a vertical circle of radius r. If its velocity at the topmost point of the circle is v, the tension of the thread at the instant will be: (a) mg − mv²r (b) mv²/r (c) (mv²/r) − mg (d) (mv²/r) + mgTension at the topmost pointTension of a string at highest point in vertical circular motion🟡 Similarc(mv²/r) − mg
Concept At the top of a vertical circle the centre lies directly below the body, so both the tension and the weight point the same way — downward, towards the centre. Newton's second law along the radius therefore adds them, and together they must equal the centripetal requirement mv²/r. Tension is whatever is left over once gravity has done its share, which is why it is the smallest of the whole circle there.
Formula Radial law at the top: T + mg = mv²/r, hence T = mv²/r − mg. Only radial forces enter this equation; the tangential component of gravity is zero at the top, so nothing else appears.
Solution
1. Draw the free body at the topmost point: weight mg downward, tension T along the string towards the centre, which is also downward.A string can only pull, so T must point from the body towards the fixed support — here that direction is straight down.
2. Net inward force = T + mg, and it must equal the centripetal requirement: T + mg = mv²/r.
3. Solve: T = mv²/r − mg, which is option (c).
4. Physical validity: T ≥ 0 demands v² ≥ gr, so vmin = √(gr) at the top — the familiar looping condition.A negative T would mean the string is pushing, which it cannot; instead it goes slack and the body leaves the circle.
5. Dimension check: mv²/r has units kg·m²s−2/m = N, matching mg, so the subtraction is legal.
6. Option scan: (d) mv²/r + mg is the bottom-point result, where gravity opposes the centripetal direction; (b) mv²/r ignores gravity entirely, true only for a horizontal circle; (a) as printed, mg − mv²r, is not even dimensionally a force and reverses the sign anyway.

          v     v      i.e. towards the centre
        (  centre O  )

⚡ JEE Trick Never memorise "plus mg" or "minus mg"; decide each time by asking whether gravity points towards the centre. At the top it does, so it helps and T shrinks; at the bottom it points away, so T grows. Option (d) is the deliberate bait for anyone who reflexively writes the lowest-point formula, and the two differ by exactly 2mg at equal speed.

2A stone is whirled in a vertical circle. In what position of the stone is the tension in the string maximum? Concept Tension in a vertical circle is raised by two independent effects, and both peak at the bottom. Speed is greatest at the lowest point because the stone has fallen through the largest drop, so mv²/r is largest there; and at the bottom gravity points away from the centre, so the string must supply the centripetal force and hold the weight as well. Two maxima at the same place make the answer unambiguous.
Formula At angle θ from the lowest point, T = mv²/r + mg cosθ with v² = vb² − 2gr(1 − cosθ). Combining the two eliminates v and leaves T as a function of θ alone, so the maximum reads off directly.
Solution
1. Substitute the energy result into the radial law: T = m[vb² − 2gr(1 − cosθ)]/r + mg cosθ.
2. Collect terms: T = mvb²/r − 2mg + 3mg cosθ.Everything but 3mg cosθ is constant, so position enters only through cosθ.
3. cosθ is largest, +1, at θ = 0 — the lowest point — giving Tmax = mvb²/r + mg.
4. It is smallest, −1, at θ = 180°, so the top carries Tmin and Tmax − Tmin = 3mg[1 − (−1)] = 6mg.That 6mg is independent of the launch speed, a handy audit of any pair of answers.
5. Horizontal, θ = 90°: cos90° = 0, so T = mv²/r lies strictly between the two — (a) is out.
6. Options: (b) the top is the minimum; (a) horizontal is intermediate; (d) needs cosθ constant, true only for a horizontal circle; so (c) stands.Constant tension demands that gravity have no radial component — impossible in a vertical plane.

Concept The phrase that decides this question is "with constant angular velocity". Centripetal force is not an extra force but the net inward force required to keep the bob on its circle, and its size is fixed entirely by m, ω and r. The string length r is fixed and ω is stated constant, so the requirement mω²r is the same number at every point of the circle — only its direction turns, always pointing at the centre.
Formula Fc = mv²/r = mω²r, with v = ωr. The mω²r form is the useful one here because ω is what the question holds constant; the mv²/r form tempts you to hunt for a varying speed that this problem has removed by decree.
Solution
1. Given: r = L (string length, fixed) and ω = constant by hypothesis.
2. Fc = mω²r = mω²L — every symbol on the right is a constant, so Fc is the same at the top, the bottom and everywhere between. Hence (c).
3. What does vary is the tension: T = mω²L + mg cosθ measured from the top, so Tbottom − Ttop = 2mg.Gravity is partly radial, so the string only has to make up the difference between the constant Fc and gravity's radial share — that difference changes, the requirement does not.
4. (b) "maximum at lowest point" is the trap: that is true of the tension, and of Fc in a freely whirled circle where the speed varies. Constant ω is externally driven, so it does not apply here.
5. (a) "maximum at highest point" is false in every version — the top is where speed and tension are least, never greatest.
6. (d) "zero" would require ω = 0, i.e. no rotation at all, contradicting the stated motion.

Concept A bob on an inextensible string has three regimes, fixed by two speeds at the lowest point. Slow: it stops below the horizontal and returns, string taut. Fast: it runs the whole loop. Between: the string cannot push, so tension dies above the horizontal and the bob turns projectile. Each row of Column I is a speed to file into a box.
Formula vosc = √(2gl), vloop = √(5gl) , v² = v0² − 2gh, Ttop = mvtop²/l − mg. √(2gl) just reaches the horizontal; √(5gl) keeps T ≥ 0 at the top.
Solution
1. Thresholds for l = 1 m, g = 10: √(2gl) = 4.47 m/s and √(5gl) = 7.07 m/s.
2. (a) v0 = 2 < 4.47: rises only h = v0²/2g = 0.2 m. String taut, T never zero, never horizontal. → q.
3. (b) v0 = 4 < 4.47: h = 0.8 m, still short of l = 1 m — same verdict. → q.4 m/s feels large, but the cut-off is 4.47 — a deliberate near-miss.
4. (c) v0 = 6 lies between: at the horizontal v² = 36 − 2(10)(1) = 16 > 0, so it gets there (r), and just past it the tension dies and it leaves the circle (p); the path is no longer circular, so q is out. → p, r.
5. (d) v0 = 8 > 7.07: it completes the loop (s), passes the horizontal on the way (r), and the string never slackens, so the path is always circular (q).
6. Is p true for (d)? vtop² = 64 − 4(10)(1) = 24, so Ttop = 24 − 10 = 14 N ≠ 0 — p is out. → q, r, s.Tension vanishes only between the two thresholds.

9What should be the minimum initial velocity of ball u (in m/s) with which it must be thrown such that it just reaches the ceiling of room after impact from the floor? (Room height 5 m, ball projected downward from a point 1.2 m below the ceiling, e = 0.5.)Bounce with restitution to reach the ceilingBouncing Ball Cases for JEE & NEET | Class 11 Physics in Minutes | Physics Galaxy🟡 Similar18 m/s
Concept Three separate stages, chained by speed at the floor: a downward-accelerated fall, an instantaneous collision that scales the speed by e, and a free rise. The two lengths differ: the ball falls only 5 − 1.2 = 3.8 m but must climb the full 5 m, and that asymmetry plus the halved rebound speed forces a large launch speed.
Formula v² = u² + 2gs for each free-flight leg and vrebound = e·vapproach at the floor. The floor is immovable, so the coefficient of restitution is simply the ratio of the two speeds at the point of contact.
Solution
1. Geometry: the launch point is 1.2 m below the ceiling of a 5 m room, so the drop to the floor is 5 − 1.2 = 3.8 m.The throw is downward, so there is no rise before the impact.
2. Falling leg: v1² = u² + 2g(3.8) = u² + 2(10)(3.8) = u² + 76.
3. Rising leg, worked backwards: "just reaches the ceiling" means arriving 5 m up with zero speed, so v2² = 2g(5) = 100 → v2 = 10 m/s."Just reaches" pins the velocity at the top to exactly zero.
4. Collision: v2 = e·v1 → 10 = 0.5·v1 → v1 = 20 m/s.
5. Back-substitute into step 2: 20² = u² + 76 → u² = 400 − 76 = 324 → u = 18 m/s.
6. Forward check: u = 18 → v1 = √(324 + 76) = 20 → v2 = 10 → rise = v2²/2g = 100/20 = 5 m, exactly the ceiling.e = 0.5 destroys 1 − e² = 75% of the kinetic energy, hence the violent head start.

⚡ JEE Trick Both distances are booby-trapped. Using 5 m for the fall gives u = √300 = 17.3 m/s; using 3.8 m for the rise (as if the ball only had to return to its launch point) gives u = 15.1 m/s. Read the picture, not the numbers. Third slip: e = 0.5 scales the speed, not the energy or the height.
Answer: u = 18 m/s.

Concept A sphere on a thread of length l is released from rest with the thread horizontal; θ is measured from the vertical. Tension is always perpendicular to v, so it does no work: energy gives the speed, radial Newton gives the tension. The acceleration splits into centripetal v²/l and tangential g sinθ — all four options probe those two.
Formula v² = 2gl cosθ, an = v²/l, at = g sinθ, T − mg cosθ = mv²/l. Radial Newton uses v²/l, never at.
Solution
1. The sphere drops h = l cosθ, so v² = 2gh = 2gl cosθ.Tension does no work, so energy is conserved.
2. an = v²/l = 2g cosθ, at = g sinθ ⇒ |a| = g√(4cos²θ + sin²θ) = g√(1 + 3cos²θ) → (a) IN.sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ folds this into 1 + 3cos²θ.
3. Radial: T − mg cosθ = 2mg cosθ ⇒ T = 3mg cosθ → (b) IN; at θ = 90°, T = 0.
4. (c) Vertical part of a = ancosθ − atsinθ = 2g cos²θ − g sin²θ = 0 ⇒ 3cos²θ = 1, θ = cos−1(1/√3) → (c) IN.Horizontal total ⇔ the vertical shares of an and at cancel.
5. (d) vy = v sinθ ⇒ vy² = 2gl(cosθ − cos³θ); d/d(cosθ) = 1 − 3cos²θ = 0 ⇒ cosθ = 1/√3, the same angle as step 4.vy peaks where dvy/dt = 0 — zero vertical acceleration.
6. There T = 3mg cosθ = 3mg/√3 = √3 mg ≈ 1.73mg, not mg → (d) OUT. Correct set: (a), (b), (c).
