1For a constant hydraulic stress on an object, the fractional change in the object’s volume (ΔV/V) and its bulk modulus (B) are related as: (a) ΔV/V ∝ B (b) ΔV/V ∝ 1/B (c) ΔV/V ∝ B2 (d) ΔV/V ∝ B−2 (Level-1)Hydraulic stress vs bulk modulusFor a constant hydraulic stress on an object, the fractional change in the object's volume and its bulk modulus 🟢 ExactbΔV/V ∝ 1/B
Concept Bulk modulus B is defined as the hydraulic (volume) stress divided by the volumetric strain it produces: B = −ΔP/(ΔV/V). The minus sign simply records that volume shrinks as pressure rises. Rearranged, the fractional change in volume is ΔV/V = −ΔP/B — and with the hydraulic stress ΔP held constant, the only thing left varying on the right is B, sitting in the denominator. So ΔV/V falls as B grows. A stiff material (large B, like steel) is squashed very little; a soft one (small B, like rubber) is squashed a lot. The whole MCQ is the definition of B wearing a proportionality disguise.
Formula B = −ΔP/(ΔV/V) ⇒ ΔV/V = −ΔP/B ∝ 1/B. Only B is free to change once ΔP is fixed, and it lives in the denominator — the signature of an inverse proportion.
Solution
1. Write the defining equation of bulk modulus, B = −ΔP/(ΔV/V), the minus sign recording that volume drops as pressure is applied.
2. Make the fractional change the subject: ΔV/V = −ΔP/B.
3. “Constant hydraulic stress” fixes ΔP as a number, say k, so ΔV/V = −k/B.This single word is the hinge of the question: it removes ΔP from the comparison and leaves B alone.
4. A relation of the form y = k/B is an inverse proportion, so ΔV/V ∝ 1/B. Doubling B halves the compression.
5. Reject the distractors: (a) ΔV/V ∝ B would make stiffer bodies compress more — physically backwards; (c) B2 and (d) B−2 invent a power the definition simply does not contain.
6. Limit check: a perfectly rigid body has B → ∞, so 1/B → 0 and ΔV/V → 0 — no squeeze at all, exactly as a rigid body demands.
⚡ JEE Trick The load-bearing phrase is “constant hydraulic stress” — it pins ΔP so the whole comparison lives in B. Anyone who misremembers the definition upside-down (B = strain/stress) is lured straight to (a). Anchor on one sentence instead: stiffer means less squashed, so compression must fall as B rises, and only 1/B does that.
Answer: (b) ΔV/V ∝ 1/B.
2A steel wire of length 20 cm is stretched to increase its length by 0.2 cm. Find the lateral strain in the wire if the Poisson’s ratio for steel is 0.19. (Level-2)Lateral strain from Poisson’s ratioA 3 cm long copper wire is stretched to increase its length by 0.3 cm. Find the lateral strain 🟡 SimilarLateral strain = 1.9 × 10−3 (0.0019)
Concept When a wire is stretched it does two things at once: it grows longer (longitudinal strain) and gets thinner (lateral strain). Poisson’s ratio σ is the fixed bridge between the two — the magnitude of the lateral strain is always σ times the longitudinal strain, for this material 0.19 times. So the problem is a one-line multiply once the longitudinal strain is known. Notice the data never needs force, area or Young’s modulus: the longitudinal strain is handed to us directly as Δl/l.
Formula lateral strain = σ × (Δl/l), from σ = (lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain). Poisson’s ratio is defined as the ratio of the two strains, so the lateral one is just σ multiplied by the longitudinal one.
Solution
1. Longitudinal strain = extension/original length = Δl/l = 0.2 cm / 20 cm = 0.01 (dimensionless).Both lengths are in cm, so the units cancel before any conversion — no need to switch to metres.
2. Poisson’s ratio links the strains: lateral strain = σ × longitudinal strain.
3. Substitute: lateral strain = 0.19 × 0.01 = 0.0019.
4. Write it in powers of ten: 0.0019 = 1.9 × 10−3.
5. Sign sense: the lateral strain is a contraction (the diameter shrinks), so strictly it carries a minus sign; the question asks only for its size, 1.9 × 10−3.The defining minus in σ = −(lateral)/(longitudinal) is what makes σ a positive number for ordinary materials.
6. Reasonableness: σ = 0.19 < 0.5, so the lateral strain is smaller than the longitudinal one — 0.0019 vs 0.01, which it is.
⚡ JEE Trick The only trap is units: keep extension and length in the same unit (both cm here) and the strain is a pure number — converting one to metres and forgetting the other gives a factor-of-100 blunder. After that it is a single multiply by σ. If an answer comes out larger than the longitudinal strain, σ has been divided instead of multiplied.
Answer: lateral strain = 0.19 × 0.01 = 1.9 × 10−3 (0.0019).
3The average depth of a lake is 2500 m. Calculate the fractional compression ΔV/V of water at the bottom of the lake. (Bulk modulus of water = 2.0 × 109 N/m2, density of water = 1000 kg/m3, g = 9.8 m/s2) (Level-3)Fractional compression at depthThe fractional compression (dV/V) of water at the depth of 2.5 km below the sea level is ___ % 🟢 ExactΔV/V = 1.225 × 10−2 (≈ 1.23%)
Concept Water at the bottom of a deep lake is squeezed by the weight of the column above it. That extra (gauge) pressure is the hydraulic stress, and the bulk modulus converts it into a fractional volume change. The job splits cleanly in two: first find the pressure at depth from ρgh, then divide by the bulk modulus to get ΔV/V. Atmospheric pressure at the surface is ignored because only the increase in pressure with depth compresses the water further.
Formula P = ρgh and ΔV/V = P/B. The first gives the hydrostatic pressure of a fluid column of height h; the second is the bulk-modulus definition solved for volumetric strain.
Solution
1. Pressure at the bottom: P = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 2500.Use the density of water, g, and the full depth h = 2500 m — this is the pressure the deep water feels in excess of the surface.
2. Evaluate: P = 1000 × 9.8 × 2500 = 2.45 × 107 N/m2.
3. Convert pressure to fractional compression with the bulk modulus: ΔV/V = P/B.
4. Substitute: ΔV/V = (2.45 × 107)/(2.0 × 109) = 1.225 × 10−2.
5. As a percentage that is about 1.23% — small, as expected for a nearly incompressible liquid under a few hundred atmospheres.2.45 × 107 Pa is roughly 240 atm, yet water’s volume changes by barely one part in eighty.
6. Units check: (N/m2)/(N/m2) is dimensionless, correct for a strain.
⚡ JEE Trick Two slips lurk here. First, use only the gauge pressure ρgh — do not add the 1 atm of surface air, since the question asks for the extra compression at depth. Second, keep B in the same SI units as P so the ratio is a clean pure number; mixing a pressure in atm with a modulus in N/m2 wrecks the answer. The final strain should always come out well below 1 for water.
Answer: ΔV/V = P/B = 1.225 × 10−2 (≈ 1.23%).
4A square lead slab of side 50 cm and thickness 5.0 cm is subjected to a shearing force (on its narrow face) of magnitude 9.0 × 104 N. The lower edge is riveted to the floor. How much is the upper edge displaced if the shear modulus of lead is 5.6 × 109 Pa? (Level-4)Shear displacement of a slabA square lead slab of side 50 cm and thickness 10 cm subject to a shearing force on its narrow face 🟡 SimilarΔx = 3.21 × 10−4 m (≈ 0.32 mm)
Concept A tangential force on the top face with the bottom riveted down shears the slab: the top slides sideways while the bottom stays put, tilting the vertical faces. The shear (rigidity) modulus links the shear stress F/A to the shear strain Δx/L, where A is the face the force acts on and L is the height over which the slide develops. The one thing to get right is geometry — which face is A and which length is L.
Formula η = (F/A)/(Δx/L) ⇒ Δx = FL/(Aη). Shear modulus is tangential stress over shear strain; solving for the sideways slip Δx gives the displacement directly.
Solution
1. Identify the sheared face: the force acts on the narrow face of area A = side × thickness = 0.50 m × 0.05 m = 0.025 m2.The “narrow face” is the 50 cm × 5 cm face; the riveted lower narrow face is parallel to it.
2. The height across which the top slides is the other side, L = 0.50 m.
3. Shear stress: F/A = (9.0 × 104)/0.025 = 3.6 × 106 N/m2.
4. Shear strain: (F/A)/η = (3.6 × 106)/(5.6 × 109) = 6.43 × 10−4.
5. Displacement: Δx = strain × L = 6.43 × 10−4 × 0.50 = 3.21 × 10−4 m ≈ 0.32 mm.
6. Cross-check with Δx = FL/(Aη) = (9.0 × 104 × 0.50)/(0.025 × 5.6 × 109) = 3.21 × 10−4 m — identical. (The printed key reads 3.21 × 10−5 m; the mantissa matches but the exponent is an evident misprint — the physical answer is 3.21 × 10−4 m.)
⚡ JEE Trick The classic error is mixing up A and L. The force’s own face is the area A; the perpendicular dimension over which the slide builds is L. Halving the thickness (5 cm instead of the familiar 10 cm NCERT slab) halves A, doubles the stress and doubles the displacement — so 0.16 mm becomes 0.32 mm. Keep every length in metres before dividing.
Answer: Δx = FL/(Aη) = 3.21 × 10−4 m (≈ 0.32 mm).
5What is the density of water at a depth where the pressure is 80.0 atm, given that its density at the surface is 1.03 × 103 kg m−3? (For water, B = 2.18 × 109 N/m2) (Level-5)Density of water under pressureWhat is the density of water at a depth where pressure is 80.0 atm, given its density at the surface 🟢 Exactρ′ ≈ 1.034 × 103 kg m−3
Concept Mass is conserved when water is squeezed, so as its volume shrinks its density must rise. The pressure compresses the volume by a fraction ΔV/V = ΔP/B; the same fraction (to first order) is how much the density climbs. The surface density is the starting point, and the deep-water density is just a little higher — water is so stiff that even 80 atm changes it by under half a percent.
Formula ΔV/V = ΔP/B and, from ρ = m/V, ρ′ = ρ/(1 − ΔV/V) ≈ ρ(1 + ΔP/B). Density is inversely proportional to volume at fixed mass, so a fractional volume loss becomes an equal fractional density gain.
Solution
1. Convert the pressure: ΔP = 80.0 atm = 80.0 × 1.013 × 105 = 8.104 × 106 N/m2.The modulus is in N/m2, so the pressure must be too before they are divided.
2. Fractional volume change: ΔV/V = ΔP/B = (8.104 × 106)/(2.18 × 109) = 3.72 × 10−3.
3. Density rises by the same fraction: ρ′ = ρ(1 + ΔP/B) = 1.03 × 103 × (1 + 3.72 × 10−3).
4. Evaluate: ρ′ = 1.03 × 103 × 1.00372 = 1033.8 ≈ 1.034 × 103 kg m−3.
5. Exact form check: ρ′ = ρ/(1 − ΔV/V) = 1030/(1 − 0.00372) = 1033.8 kg m−3 — the approximation and the exact ratio agree to four figures.Because ΔV/V is tiny, 1/(1−x) ≈ 1+x and the two routes coincide.
6. Sanity: the density increased by only about 4 kg m−3 under 80 atm — water is nearly incompressible, so a sub-percent change is exactly what we expect.
⚡ JEE Trick Remember that a volume decrease means a density increase — the fraction is the same but the sign flips. Students who subtract instead of add push the density below the surface value, which is impossible under compression. Convert atm to N/m2 first (multiply by 1.013 × 105), and expect a change of only a few kg m−3.
Answer: ρ′ = ρ(1 + ΔP/B) ≈ 1.034 × 103 kg m−3.

1Calculate the change in volume of a lead block of volume 1 m3 subjected to a pressure of 10 atmospheres. Also calculate the compressibility of lead. (One atmosphere = 1.013 × 105 N/m2, B = 8 × 109 N/m2)Volume change & compressibility of leadFind the change in volume of a lead block of volume 2 m^3 using bulk modulus 🟡 SimilarΔV = 1.266 × 10−4 m3; k = 1.25 × 10−10 m2/N
Concept A uniform pressure on a block is a hydraulic stress, and the bulk modulus B tells us how much volume it loses. Two quantities are asked. The change in volume follows from ΔV = PV/B; the compressibility is simply the reciprocal of the bulk modulus, k = 1/B, a material constant independent of the block’s size. The only care needed is converting the 10-atmosphere pressure into N/m2 first.
Formula B = P/(ΔV/V) ⇒ ΔV = PV/B, and compressibility k = 1/B. Compressibility is defined as the reciprocal of the bulk modulus — the ease with which a material yields volume per unit pressure.
Solution
1. Convert the pressure: P = 10 atm = 10 × 1.013 × 105 = 1.013 × 106 N/m2.B is quoted in N/m2, so P must be in the same units before dividing.
2. Change in volume: ΔV = PV/B = (1.013 × 106 × 1)/(8 × 109).
3. Evaluate: ΔV = 1.266 × 10−4 m3 — a contraction, so strictly −1.266 × 10−4 m3.
4. Compressibility: k = 1/B = 1/(8 × 109) = 1.25 × 10−10 m2/N.
5. Consistency: ΔV = k × P × V = 1.25 × 10−10 × 1.013 × 106 × 1 = 1.266 × 10−4 m3 — same number by the reciprocal route.Using k instead of B must reproduce the volume change, a quick self-check.
6. Scale sense: a 1 m3 lead block loses only about 0.13 cm3 under 10 atm — lead is stiff in volume even though it is soft in shear.
⚡ JEE Trick Do not forget to turn “10 atmospheres” into pascals — leaving P as 10 silently divides the answer by 105. And keep the two asks separate: ΔV depends on the block’s volume, but compressibility 1/B does not — it is a property of lead alone.
Answer: ΔV = PV/B = 1.266 × 10−4 m3; compressibility = 1/B = 1.25 × 10−10 m2/N.
2Find the increase in pressure required to decrease the volume of mercury by 0.001%. (Bulk modulus of mercury = 2.8 × 1010 N/m2)Pressure to compress mercuryFind the increase in pressure required to decrease the volume of mercury by 0.001% 🟢 ExactΔP = 2.8 × 105 N/m2
Concept This is the bulk-modulus relation run in reverse: instead of finding the volume change from a pressure, we are told the volume change and asked for the pressure that caused it. Because B = ΔP/(ΔV/V), the required pressure is simply the bulk modulus multiplied by the fractional volume change. The 0.001% must first be written as a plain decimal fraction.
Formula B = ΔP/(ΔV/V) ⇒ ΔP = B × (ΔV/V). Rearranging the definition of bulk modulus isolates the pressure increase needed for a given fractional compression.
Solution
1. Write the fractional change as a decimal: ΔV/V = 0.001% = 0.001/100 = 1 × 10−5.“Per cent” means divide by 100, so 0.001% is 10−5, not 10−3 — the single most common slip in this problem.
2. Apply the formula: ΔP = B × (ΔV/V) = 2.8 × 1010 × 1 × 10−5.
3. Evaluate: ΔP = 2.8 × 105 N/m2.
4. In atmospheres: 2.8 × 105 / 1.013 × 105 ≈ 2.76 atm — a modest pressure for so tiny a squeeze, because mercury’s bulk modulus is large.
5. Units: (N/m2) × (dimensionless) = N/m2, a pressure, as required.The fractional strain is unitless, so ΔP carries the units of B.
6. Reasonableness: a stiff liquid like mercury needs a sizeable pressure for even a ten-thousandth fractional change — 2.8 × 105 Pa fits that expectation.
⚡ JEE Trick The whole difficulty is the percentage. 0.001% is 10−5: write it as 0.001 divided by 100 and you cannot go wrong. Treating it as 0.001 (i.e. 10−3) inflates the answer by a factor of 100. Once the fraction is right, it is a one-line multiply by B.
Answer: ΔP = B × (ΔV/V) = 2.8 × 105 N/m2.

(Given: Ysteel = 2 × 1011 N/m2, σ = 0.291)Lateral strain in a stretched wireWhen a uniform metallic wire is stretched, the lateral strain produced 🟡 SimilarLateral strain = 1.455 × 10−5 (0.1455 × 10−4)
Concept Stretching a wire with a force makes it longer (longitudinal strain) and thinner (lateral strain), and Poisson’s ratio σ links the two. Answer: lateral strain = σ(F/A)/Y = 1.455 × 10−5.

Answer: Δx = FL/(Aη) = 3 × 10−3 m = 3 mm.

2. Its contribution to the restoring torque is (stress) × (area 2πr dr) × (lever arm r) = η(rθ0/L)(2πr dr)(r).
3. Integrate from 0 to R: τ0 = (2πηθ0/L) ∫0R r3 dr = (2πηθ0/L)(R4/4).
4. Simplify: τ0 = (πηR4θ0)/(2L) — the standard torsion formula.
5. Solve for the modulus: η = 2Lτ0/(πR4θ0).
6. Dimensional check: [N·m][m]/([m4][rad]) = N/m2, correct for a modulus (the radian is dimensionless).The strong R4 dependence means a slightly thicker cylinder is dramatically harder to twist.
⚡ JEE Trick The R4 comes from integrating r3 (one r from the strain, one from the lever arm, plus r dr from the ring area) — forgetting one of these powers gives R2 or R3 and a wrong formula. Just invert the standard torsion law τ = πηR4θ/2L for η.
Answer: η = 2Lτ0/(πR4θ0).
6A thin uniform rod of mass M and length L is free to rotate in a vertical plane about a horizontal axis passing through one of its ends. The rod is released from the horizontal position. Calculate the shear stress developed at the centre of the rod immediately after it is released. The cross-sectional area of the rod is A. (For the moment of inertia you may treat it as very thin.)Shear stress in a released rodShear Stress at the middle of a rod 🟡 SimilarShear stress = Mg/16A
Concept The instant the horizontal rod is released it begins to rotate about its pinned end with angular acceleration α. Different parts of the rod need different downward (tangential) accelerations, and the only way the outer half can be forced to accelerate correctly is for the inner half to exert a transverse shear force on it at the mid-section. That internal shear force, divided by the cross-sectional area, is the shear stress asked for.
Formula α = (MgL/2)/(ML2/3) = 3g/2L; for the outer half, Mg/2 + V = (M/2)α(3L/4) ⇒ shear stress = V/A. Torque over moment of inertia gives α; Newton’s law on the outer half isolates the shear force V at the centre.
Solution
1. Angular acceleration at release: α = torque/I = (Mg × L/2)/(ML2/3) = 3g/(2L).Weight acts at the centre L/2; the rod’s moment of inertia about the end is ML2/3.
2. Consider the outer half (centre to tip): mass M/2, its centre of mass at 3L/4 from the pivot.
3. Tangential acceleration of that CM: a = α(3L/4) = (3g/2L)(3L/4) = 9g/8.
4. Newton’s law on the outer half (downward positive): weight Mg/2 plus the shear V from the inner half equals mass × a: Mg/2 + V = (M/2)(9g/8) = 9Mg/16.The outer half needs a larger downward force than its own weight provides, so the inner half must pull it down through the shear.
5. Solve for V: V = 9Mg/16 − Mg/2 = 9Mg/16 − 8Mg/16 = Mg/16.
6. Shear stress = V/A = Mg/(16A).Dividing the internal shear force by the cross-section gives the stress on the mid-section.
⚡ JEE Trick Isolate the outer half and apply F = ma to it: its CM needs acceleration α(3L/4), which exceeds what gravity alone supplies, so the shear force makes up the difference. The neat cancellation 9/16 − 8/16 = 1/16 is the whole answer — use the CM at 3L/4, not L/2.
Answer: shear stress at the centre = V/A = Mg/16A.
7A rigid cylindrical container has inner radius r. A cork having radius r + Δr and length L is to be fitted so as to close the container. A uniform pressure (ΔP) is needed on the curved cylindrical surface of the cork. Poisson’s ratio of the cork is almost zero, and its bulk modulus is B. (a) Calculate ΔP. (b) After the cork is fitted, how much force will be needed to pull it out of the container? The coefficient of friction between the container and the cork is μ.Cork compression & pull-out forceA rigid cylindrical container has inner radius r. A cork of radius r+dr and length L is fitted 🟢 Exact(a) ΔP = 2BΔr/r; (b) F = 4πμBLΔr
Concept The cork is slightly too fat (radius r + Δr) for the hole (radius r), so pushing it in squeezes its radius down by Δr. With Poisson’s ratio nearly zero, the radial squeeze does not change the cork’s length, so the volume change is purely radial. The bulk modulus converts that volumetric strain into the pressure ΔP on the curved wall. That pressure then sets the normal force, and friction against it gives the pull-out force.
Formula ΔV/V = 2Δr/r (σ ≈ 0), ΔP = B(ΔV/V) = 2BΔr/r; friction F = μ(ΔP)(2πrL). Area ∝ r2 gives ΔV/V = 2Δr/r; the normal force is the pressure over the curved surface 2πrL.
Solution
1. Radial strain: the radius is compressed from r + Δr to r, so Δr/r (the length stays fixed because σ ≈ 0).
2. Volume strain: V ∝ r2L with L fixed, so ΔV/V = 2(Δr/r).Only the cross-section changes; its area goes as r2, giving the factor of 2.
3. Pressure from the bulk modulus: ΔP = B(ΔV/V) = 2BΔr/r — this is answer (a).
4. Normal force on the cork’s curved surface: N = ΔP × (curved area) = ΔP × 2πrL.
5. Maximum static friction (the pull-out force): F = μN = μ(ΔP)(2πrL) = μ(2BΔr/r)(2πrL).
6. The r cancels: F = 4πμBLΔr — answer (b).The factor of r in the area cancels the 1/r in ΔP, leaving a tidy r-free force.
⚡ JEE Trick Poisson’s ratio ≈ 0 is the key simplification: the length does not change, so the volume strain is just 2Δr/r (two radial directions). The pull-out force comes from friction over the curved surface 2πrL; watch the r cancel between ΔP (∝ 1/r) and the area (∝ r) to leave 4πμBLΔr.
Answer: (a) ΔP = 2BΔr/r; (b) F = 4πμBLΔr.
8A closed steel cylinder is completely filled with water at 0°C. The water is made to freeze at 0°C. Calculate the rise in pressure on the cylinder wall. It is known that the density of water at 0°C is 1000 kg/m3 and the density of ice at 0°C is 910 kg/m3. The bulk modulus of ice at 0°C is nearly 9 × 109 Pa.Pressure rise when water freezesA closed steel cylinder completely filled with water at 0 C - the water is made to freeze 🟢 Exact≈ 8.1 × 108 Pa
Concept When the trapped water freezes it tries to expand (ice is less dense than water), but the rigid sealed cylinder forbids any volume increase. So the newly formed ice is compressed back to the water’s volume, and the pressure needed for that compression is what pushes on the walls. The required pressure is the bulk modulus of ice times the fractional compression prevented.
Formula ΔV/Vice = 1 − ρice/ρwater, P = Bice × (ΔV/Vice). The ice would occupy m/ρice but is held at m/ρwater; the strain is referred to the ice’s natural (uncompressed) volume.
Solution
1. For mass m, the natural ice volume is m/910 and the confined volume is m/1000 (that of the original water).
2. The compression is ΔV = m/910 − m/1000, taken relative to the ice volume m/910.
3. Fractional compression: ΔV/Vice = (m/910 − m/1000)/(m/910) = 1 − 910/1000 = 0.09.Referring the strain to the ice’s own volume gives the clean figure 90/1000 = 0.09.
4. Pressure from the bulk modulus of ice: P = Bice × 0.09 = (9 × 109) × 0.09.
5. Evaluate: P = 8.1 × 108 Pa.
6. Context: 8.1 × 108 Pa is about 8000 atmospheres — far beyond what pipes can withstand, which is exactly why water pipelines burst in winter.Comparing with 105 Pa (1 atm) shows the freezing pressure is enormous.
⚡ JEE Trick Reference the strain to the ice volume (the uncompressed state), giving 90/1000 = 0.09 rather than 90/910. That choice is what the key uses, and it matches how bulk modulus is defined (strain relative to the relaxed volume). The huge result explains burst winter pipes — a built-in sanity note.
Answer: P = Bice(1 − ρice/ρwater) = 9 × 109 × 0.09 ≈ 8.1 × 108 Pa.

Concept The balloon is a thin spherical shell. At the bursting altitude the inside (helium) pressure exceeds the outside pressure, and that pressure difference sets up a tensile stress in the shell. The shell bursts when this stress hits the material’s maximum. Three sub-results feed in: the internal pressure from the isothermal gas law, the shell thickness from its mass and density, and the thin-shell stress formula.
Formula stress σ = ΔP·R/(2t), with t = m/(ρ0·4πR2) and (4/3)πR3 = 1.25V0. A thin sphere under pressure difference ΔP carries stress ΔP·R/(2t); the thickness follows from spreading the shell mass over its surface.
Solution
1. Internal pressure (isothermal): P0V0 = Pin(1.25V0) ⇒ Pin = 0.8P0. External = P0/2, so ΔP = 0.8P0 − 0.5P0 = 0.3P0.
2. Shell thickness: t = (shell volume)/(area) = (m/ρ0)/(4πR2) = m/(4πR2ρ0).
3. Thin-shell stress: σ = ΔP·R/(2t) = ΔP·R·(4πR2ρ0)/(2m) = ΔP·ρ0·2πR3/m.Substituting t cancels one power of R and brings in the density.
4. Express 2πR3 via the volume: (4/3)πR3 = 1.25V0 ⇒ 2πR3 = 1.5 × 1.25V0 = 1.875V0.
5. Assemble: σ = (0.3P0)·ρ0·(1.875V0)/m = 0.5625 P0V0ρ0/m = (9/16)P0V0ρ0/m.
6. Match αP0V0ρ0/(βm) = (9/16)P0V0ρ0/m: α = 9, β = 16, so α + β = 25.
⚡ JEE Trick Substituting the thickness turns R/t into ρ0·2πR3/m, and 2πR3 = 1.5 × (balloon volume). Keep that factor 1.5; dropping it is the usual slip.
Answer: σ = (9/16)P0V0ρ0/m ⇒ α + β = 9 + 16 = 25.
