1A horizontal platform is rotating with uniform angular velocity around the vertical axis passing through its centre. At some instant of time a viscous fluid of mass ‘m’ is dropped at the centre and is allowed to spread out and finally fall. The angular velocity during this period (a) Decreases continuously (b) Decreases initially and increases again (c) Remains unaltered (d) Increases continuously — (Level-1)Changing I at zero external torqueConservation of Angular Momentum for IIT JEE / NEET — Rotational Motion 05🟡 SimilarbDecreases initially and increases again ⚠
Concept The platform turns on a vertical axis and every force in the story — gravity, the normal reaction, the viscous drag between fluid and platform — is either along the axis or internal to the platform–fluid system. So no external torque acts about the axis and the total angular momentum of platform + fluid is fixed. What is not fixed is how that angular momentum is shared, because the fluid keeps moving outward and its moment of inertia keeps growing. Angular speed is therefore a slave to the instantaneous moment of inertia of whatever is still riding on the platform.
Formula L = Iω = constant ⇒ ω ∝ 1/I. Valid because τext = dL/dt = 0 about the vertical axis. Ifluid = Σmiri². Moving the same mass outward raises I quadratically.
Solution
1. Drop stage: the blob lands at r = 0, so Ifluid = m(0)² = 0 and the platform’s I0ω0 is untouched — the angular speed does not jump at the instant of contact.
2. Spreading stage: the fluid creeps out to radius r, so I = I0 + mr² and ω = I0ω0/(I0 + mr²), which falls monotonically as r grows.
3. Numerically, with I0 = mR² for illustration: at r = R/2, ω = ω0/1.25 = 0.80ω0; at r = R, ω = ω0/2. Viscous drag is the internal agent that slows the platform.
4. Shedding stage (the key’s reading): once the fluid runs off the rim it is no longer part of I, so I drops back to I0 and, if L of the platform alone were restored, ω would climb back → “decreases then increases”, option (b).
5. My working: the departing fluid leaves the rim with tangential speed ωR, i.e. carrying angular momentum mR²ω with it. The platform’s own L = I0ω is not returned, so ω simply freezes at its lowest value. This is the discrepancy behind the ⚠.
6. Options (c) and (d) die immediately: I demonstrably changes, and it only ever increases while the fluid is on board, so ω can never rise above ω0.
7. Option (a) “decreases continuously” would need I to keep growing after the fluid has gone, which is impossible; so (b) is the least wrong choice on offer.

⚡ JEE Trick The tempting trap is to treat “I goes back down” as automatically meaning “ω goes back up”. That inference is only legal when the same angular momentum is redistributed; mass that flies off the rim exports its share of L instead. Whenever a problem says mass leaves the body, check whether it leaves with a tangential velocity — if it does, the remaining body’s ω is unchanged.
Answer: (b) Decreases initially and increases again — per the key; my working gives decreases, then stays constant. ⚠
2As shown in figure (a conical pendulum: a bob of mass m hangs on a string from a point O on a vertical axis, the string making an angle θ with the axis, and the bob sweeps a horizontal circle whose centre o lies on the same axis). Find the point on the axis about which angular momentum of bob remains constant. — (Level-2)Torque-free point of a conical pendulumAngular Momentum — Basic Introduction, Torque, Inertia, Conservation of Angular Momentum🟡 SimilarAbout the point o — the centre of the bob’s horizontal circle, where the axis pierces the plane of the circle
Concept Angular momentum is constant about a point only if the net torque about that same point vanishes; the choice of origin is everything here. The bob feels exactly two forces: its weight mg, straight down, and the string tension T directed from the bob towards the suspension point O. About a general point both leave a non-zero torque, so we must hunt for the origin at which the two torques cancel. Because the motion is a steady horizontal circle, the vertical force balance already fixes T cosθ = mg, and that is the equality that makes the cancellation happen.
Formula τ = r⃗ × F⃗, dL⃗/dt = τ⃗net. L is constant about a point precisely when the vector sum of torques about it is zero. T cosθ = mg, T sinθ = mω²r. Standard conical-pendulum equilibrium along and perpendicular to the axis.
Solution
1. Put the origin at o, the centre of the circle, at the same height as the bob. The position of the bob is then purely radial: r⃗ = r r̂, with r = ℓ sinθ.
2. Torque of gravity about o: τ⃗g = (r r̂) × (−mg k̂) = −mgr (r̂ × k̂), a horizontal vector of magnitude mgr.
3. Split the tension into T sinθ along −r̂ and T cosθ along +k̂. The radial part is parallel to r⃗, so it gives zero torque about o. r̂ × r̂ = 0 — a central force never torques about the point it points at.
4. Torque of the vertical part: τ⃗T = (r r̂) × (T cosθ k̂) = r T cosθ (r̂ × k̂) = mgr (r̂ × k̂), using T cosθ = mg.
5. Add them: τ⃗net = −mgr(r̂×k̂) + mgr(r̂×k̂) = 0. Hence L⃗ about o is a genuine constant — magnitude mωr² and direction along the axis, both fixed. This is why the key answers “about o”.
6. Contrast with the suspension point O: there r⃗ = ℓ (along the string), tension gives zero torque, but gravity gives τ = m g ℓ sinθ ≠ 0. So about O the magnitude of L is constant while its direction sweeps a cone — L is not conserved there.
7. Any other point on the axis, at height h above o, adds an uncancelled term of size mgr × (h/r) type; only h = 0 kills it. The unique torque-free point is the circle’s own centre.
 origin      torque of mg     torque of T      net
 ------------------------------------------------
 O (top)     mg*l*sin(th)     0                != 0
 o (centre)  mg*r             T cos(th)*r      0
 axis, h     mg*r             mg*r  (tilted)   != 0
 ------------------------------------------------
 at o:  T cos(th) = mg  =>  the two cancel
 so L about o = m*w*r^2 along the axis = const
⚡ JEE Trick Students reflexively answer “the point of suspension”, because that is where the string is tied and where the tension torque vanishes — but gravity does not vanish there. The correct instinct is to look for the origin where the two torques are equal and opposite, and the vertical equilibrium condition T cosθ = mg hands it to you: the centre of the circle. Note also that at o the angular momentum is constant in both magnitude and direction, while at O only the magnitude survives.
Answer: About the point o — the centre of the bob’s horizontal circle on the axis.
3The initial angular velocity of a circular disc of mass M is ω1. Then two small spheres of mass ‘m’ are attached gently to two diametrically opposite points on the edge of the disc. What is the final angular velocity of the disc? — (Level-3)Masses added to a spinning discRotational Motion 08 — Conservation of Angular Momentum (IIT JEE / NEET)🟡 SimilarMω1/(M + 4m)
Concept The word gently is doing all the work: it means the spheres are placed with no impulsive push and no external torque about the spin axis. Whatever friction or adhesion grabs them is internal to the disc-plus-spheres system, so the total angular momentum about the axis is the same an instant before and an instant after they stick. The moment of inertia, however, jumps, because two point masses now sit at the full radius R where they contribute the most possible. Angular speed must drop in exactly the ratio that keeps Iω fixed.
Formula Idisc = MR²/2. Uniform disc about its central perpendicular axis. Ipoint = mR² each. A point mass at distance R from the axis. I1ω1 = I2ω2. Zero external torque about the axis.
Solution
1. Before: I1 = MR²/2, so the angular momentum is L = I1ω1 = MR²ω1/2.
2. After: both spheres sit at the rim, one at each end of a diameter, so I2 = MR²/2 + mR² + mR² = MR²/2 + 2mR². Diametrically opposite only matters for balance; each still contributes mR².
3. Factor the radius out: I2 = (R²/2)(M + 4m). Writing it this way makes R cancel in the next line.
4. Conserve: ω2 = I1ω1/I2 = [(MR²/2)ω1] / [(R²/2)(M + 4m)] = Mω1/(M + 4m).
5. Sanity check with numbers: M = 4 kg, m = 1 kg, ω1 = 8 rad/s gives ω2 = 4(8)/(4+4) = 4 rad/s — two 1 kg spheres on a 4 kg disc exactly halve the speed, which is right because 2mR² = 2R² equals MR²/2 = 2R².
6. Limits: m → 0 returns ω2 = ω1; m → ∞ sends ω2 → 0. Both are the physically obvious behaviour, so the algebra is trustworthy.
7. Note that kinetic energy is not conserved: KE = L²/2I falls by the factor M/(M+4m), the loss going into the friction that spins the spheres up.

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is writing the disc’s inertia as MR² instead of MR²/2; that single slip turns the answer into Mω1/(M+2m) and matches a distractor in nearly every version of this question. Second trap: “gently” is a technical word — it licenses angular-momentum conservation but forbids energy conservation, so never equate the two kinetic energies here.
Answer: ω2 = Mω1/(M + 4m).
4What will be the duration of the day, if the Earth suddenly shrinks to 1/64 of its original volume, mass remaining unchanged? Moment of inertia of sphere = (2/5) × mass × (radius)². — (Level-4)Earth shrinks — length of dayIf earth suddenly shrinks to 1/8th of its original volume, mass same — duration of day🟡 Similar1.5 hour
Concept A shrinking Earth pulls itself inward under its own gravity, which is an internal force and exerts no torque about the spin axis. Total angular momentum L = Iω is therefore locked, so squeezing the mass closer to the axis must be paid for by spinning faster. The only external handle is the volume, and volume fixes the radius through V ∝ R³. Once R is known, I ∝ R² follows and the period T = 2π/ω drops in the same proportion as I.
Formula I = (2/5)MR². Solid sphere about a diameter, as the question supplies. V = (4/3)πR³ ⇒ R ∝ V1/3. Converts the volume shrink into a radius shrink. Iω = const, T = 2π/ω ⇒ T ∝ I. Zero external torque about the rotation axis.
Solution
1. Radius after shrinking: R′/R = (V′/V)1/3 = (1/64)1/3 = 1/4, so R′ = R/4.
2. New moment of inertia: I′/I = (R′/R)² = (1/4)² = 1/16, since M is unchanged — I′ = I/16.
3. Conserve angular momentum: Iω = I′ω′ ⇒ ω′ = ω(I/I′) = 16ω. The Earth would spin sixteen times faster.
4. Convert to period: T′ = 2π/ω′ = (2π/ω)/16 = T/16 = 24 h / 16 = 1.5 hour = 90 minutes.
5. Cross-check by the direct route: T′ = T (R′/R)² = 24 × (1/16) = 1.5 h. T ∝ R² is the one-line shortcut worth memorising for every “Earth contracts” question.
6. Rotational KE would rise: KE = L²/2I, so KE′/KE = I/I′ = 16. The gravitational potential energy released during the collapse supplies it — no conservation law is broken.
7. Beware the numerical decoys: 1/64 is a volume ratio, so 64 is a cube, not a square. Reading it as a radius ratio would give T′ = 24/4096 s, absurdly small.

 -----------------------------------------

 radius     R         R/4         1/4

 omega      w         16 w        16
 period T   24 h      1.5 h       1/16
 -----------------------------------------
 rule of thumb:  T' / T = (R'/R)^2
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit side by side. First, the cube root: examiners deliberately quote a volume so that students who square 1/64 instead of cube-rooting it land on a wrong option. Second, the direction: I falls, so ω rises and T falls — a shrinking Earth gives a shorter day, never a longer one. Memorise T ∝ R² at fixed mass and every variant of this problem becomes a one-liner.
Answer: 1.5 hour (90 minutes).
5Two discs are rotating about their fixed centres C1 & C2 as shown in figure (disc 1: mass m1, radius r1; disc 2: mass m2, radius r2; both spun to the same angular speed ω0 in the same sense, rims touching, r1 > r2 at t = 0). Find the workdone by friction on the system of discs till the slipping stops. — (Level-5)Rim friction between two spinning discsRotational dynamics: A two disk problem🟡 SimilarW = −m1m2(r1+r2)²ω0² / [4(m1+m2)]
Concept Both centres are pinned, so the axles can supply any reaction they like and the total angular momentum is not conserved — this is the single biggest difference from the usual “disc dropped on disc” problem. What is usable is the friction impulse J at the contact: it is one and the same force pair, so it delivers a torque J r1 to one disc and J r2 to the other. Slipping ends when the two rims share a common contact-point velocity, which for externally touching wheels means they must end up counter-rotating like gears. The energy lost is then the friction force times the relative sliding, integrated over the whole process.
Formula Ii = miri²/2. Uniform disc about its centre. IΔω = −r J. Angular impulse of the single contact impulse J on each disc. Wfr = −∫u dJ with u the relative sliding speed. Kinetic-friction dissipation is force × relative slip, not force × path of either body.
Solution
1. Take each ω positive in its original sense. Contact-point speeds are v1 = ω1r1 and v2 = −ω2r2, so the sliding speed is u = ω1r1 + ω2r2, initially u0 = ω0(r1+r2).
2. Angular impulse on each disc: ω1 = ω0 − r1J/I1 = ω0 − 2J/(m1r1) and likewise ω2 = ω0 − 2J/(m2r2). The r² in I cancels one r from the torque arm, leaving 1/(mr).
3. Substituting into u: u = u0 − 2J(1/m1 + 1/m2) = u0 − 2J/μ where μ = m1m2/(m1+m2) is the reduced mass. The radii have vanished from the dynamics entirely.
4. Slipping stops at u = 0, i.e. the total friction impulse is Jf = μu0/2 = μω0(r1+r2)/2.
5. Heat generated = ∫f u dt = ∫0Jf u dJ = u0Jf − Jf²/μ = μu0²/2 − μu0²/4 = μu0²/4.
6. Hence Wfriction on the system = −μu0²/4 = −m1m2(r1+r2)²ω0²/[4(m1+m2)], independent of the friction coefficient.
7. Check the symmetric case m1 = m2 = m, r1 = r2 = r: μ = m/2, u0 = 2ω0r, so W = −mω0²r²/2 — exactly the whole initial KE of 2 × ½(mr²/2)ω0², and indeed both discs come to rest. A perfect stress test of the formula.
 mu = m1 m2 / (m1 + m2)      u0 = w0 (r1 + r2)
 -------------------------------------------------
 u(J)  = u0 - 2J/mu          slip ends at u = 0
 Jf    = mu u0 / 2
 heat  = int u dJ = mu u0^2 / 4
 -------------------------------------------------
 W_fr = - m1 m2 (r1+r2)^2 w0^2 / [4 (m1 + m2)]
 check m1=m2=m, r1=r2=r  ->  W = - m w0^2 r^2 / 2
⚡ JEE Trick The fatal instinct is to write I1ω0 + I2ω0 = (I1+I2)ω — illegal here, because the fixed axles are external supports that happily absorb angular momentum. Work instead with the shared friction impulse J and the rolling condition ω1r1 = −ω2r2. Also note that μ here is a reduced mass, not a friction coefficient; μfriction cancels out because it only sets how long the process takes, not how much slip has to be eaten.
Answer: W = −m1m2(r1+r2)²ω0²/[4(m1+m2)] — the printed ICAD key lists no answer for this question, so the value above is entirely my own derivation.

1A spherical water balloon is revolving at 60 rpm. In the course of time, 48.8% of its water leaks out. With what frequency will the remaining balloon revolve now? Neglect all non-conservative forces. — (Level: Board)Mass loss from a spinning sphereRotational Motion L-10 — Conservation of Angular Momentum (Class 11)🟡 Similarn′ = (1.25)5 × 1 = 3.052 rps (≈ 183 rpm)
Concept Water has fixed density, so losing mass from a spherical balloon shrinks its radius as the cube root of the surviving mass fraction. The problem tells us to neglect non-conservative effects, which is the instruction to treat the angular momentum of the remaining ball as unchanged while it contracts. Both M and R drop, and since I = (2/5)MR² the moment of inertia falls faster than the mass alone would suggest — it falls as M5/3. The spin rate rises by exactly the reciprocal of that factor.
Formula I = (2/5)MR². Solid sphere of water about a diameter. M ∝ R³ at fixed density ⇒ R ∝ M1/3. Volume, not radius, is what the leaked fraction really tells us. Iω = const ⇒ n′/n = I/I′. Frequency n = ω/2π scales the same way as ω.
Solution
1. Surviving mass fraction: M′/M = 1 − 0.488 = 0.512. Recognise it — 0.512 = (0.8)³, which is why the number 48.8% was chosen.
2. Radius: R′/R = (M′/M)1/3 = (0.512)1/3 = 0.8, i.e. the ball contracts to four-fifths of its radius.
3. Moment of inertia: I′/I = (M′/M)(R′/R)² = 0.512 × 0.64 = 0.32768 = (0.8)5. The exponent 5 = 3 (mass) + 2 (radius squared) is the signature of a constant-density sphere.
4. Initial frequency: n = 60 rpm = 1 rev/s. Conserving angular momentum, n′ = n (I/I′) = 1/(0.8)5 = (1.25)5.
5. Evaluate: (1.25)5 = 1.5625 × 1.5625 × 1.25 = 2.4414 × 1.25 = 3.052 rev/s, i.e. about 183 rpm.
6. Check the arithmetic the other way: 1/0.32768 = 3.0518. Matching two independent routes to four figures confirms both the cube root and the exponent.
7. Physical caveat worth stating: real leaking water carries angular momentum away, so this “neglect non-conservative forces” model is an idealisation — the board question wants Iω of the remaining ball held constant, and the key’s (1.25)5 confirms that reading.

⚡ JEE Trick Most students stop at I ∝ R² and forget that M is shrinking too, landing on 1/0.64 = 1.5625 rps. The clean habit is: for a body of fixed density, I ∝ M5/3 ∝ R5, so a leak of fraction f gives n′/n = (1−f)−5/3. The oddly specific 48.8% is a giveaway that the setter engineered (0.8)³ — spotting that saves you the cube root.
Answer: 3.052 rps, i.e. (1.25)5 rev/s ≈ 183 rpm.

1The motion of planets in the solar system is an example of the conservation of (a) Mass (b) Linear momentum (c) Angular momentum (d) Energy — (Level-1)Central forces and Kepler’s second lawLaw of Conservation of Angular Momentum and its Applications — Class 11 Physics🟡 SimilarcAngular momentum
Concept Gravity from the Sun is a central force: it always points from the planet straight back at the Sun. Taking the Sun as origin, the position vector r⃗ and the force F⃗ are therefore antiparallel, so their cross product — the torque — is identically zero at every instant of the orbit. Zero torque means dL⃗/dt = 0, so the planet’s angular momentum about the Sun is a strict constant of the motion, in both magnitude and direction. That single fact is what forces the orbit to stay in one plane and what Kepler had already spotted empirically as his law of equal areas.
Formula τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗ = 0 when F⃗ ∥ r⃗. Definition of a central force; gravity qualifies exactly. L⃗ = r⃗ × p⃗ = constant. Direct consequence of dL⃗/dt = τ⃗net. dA/dt = L/2m. Kepler’s second law is just constant L in disguise.
Solution
1. Write the torque of the Sun’s pull about the Sun: τ⃗ = r⃗ × (−GMm/r²) r̂ = −(GMm/r²)(r⃗ × r̂) = 0, because r⃗ × r̂ = 0.
2. Hence L⃗ is constant. Its fixed direction confines the orbit to a plane; its fixed magnitude gives the equal-area law dA/dt = L/2m. Both halves of Kepler’s second law fall out of one statement.
3. Test option (b): linear momentum p⃗ = mv⃗ changes continuously — at perihelion and aphelion the planet moves in opposite directions, so p⃗ has reversed. The Sun exerts a real external force, so p⃗ cannot be conserved.
4. Test option (a): mass conservation is true but trivial — it holds for a rock on a table too, and says nothing special about planetary motion. It is not what the orbit “exemplifies”.
5. Test option (d): total mechanical energy E = ½mv² − GMm/r is conserved for an ideal two-body orbit, which makes (d) the strongest distractor. But energy conservation is shared with every conservative force, including a spring; it does not explain the plane or the equal areas. The question asks what planetary motion is a standard example of, and the textbook illustration of L-conservation is exactly a planet speeding up near the Sun.
6. Quantitative signature: at perihelion rpvp = rava. For Earth, ra/rp ≈ 1.034, so vp/va ≈ 1.034 — Earth runs about 3.4% faster in January.
7. Therefore (c) is the answer; the key prints (c) and my derivation agrees.

   equal areas        (magnitude of L)

⚡ JEE Trick Energy is the distractor that catches good students, because mechanical energy really is conserved in an ideal orbit. The discriminator is what the phrase “is an example of” points at: the observable peculiarity of planetary motion — fast near the Sun, slow far away, always in one plane — is the fingerprint of constant L, not of constant E. Linear momentum is never conserved when a single external force acts.
Answer: (c) Angular momentum.
2The angular momentum of a system of particles is conserved (a) When no external force acts upon the system (b) When no external torque acts on the system (c) When no external impulse acts upon the system (d) When axis of rotation remains same — (Level-1)Condition for conserving LConservation of Angular Momentum for IIT JEE / NEET — Rotational Motion 05🟡 SimilarbWhen no external torque acts on the system
Concept The rotational analogue of Newton’s second law reads τ⃗ext = dL⃗/dt for a system of particles about a fixed point in an inertial frame. Internal forces between the particles come in Newton’s-third-law pairs acting along the same line, so their torques cancel exactly and they can never change L⃗. That leaves the external torque as the one and only agent capable of changing angular momentum. Setting it to zero is therefore the necessary and sufficient condition, and nothing else in the option list is equivalent to it.
