1Let A(4, −2), B(1, 1) and C(9, −3) be the vertices of a triangle ABC. Then the maximum area of the parallelogram AFDE, formed with vertices D, E and F on the sides BC, CA and AB of the triangle ABC respectively, is ______. [JEE Main 2025(2 Apr. Shift-2)] (Level-8)Max area of parallelogram inscribed in a triangleLet A(4, –2), B(1, 1) and C(9, –3) be the vertices of a triangle ABC. Then the maximum area of the🟢 Exact3
Concept A parallelogram AFDE inscribed in triangle ABC, sharing the vertex A and carrying the other three vertices on the sides (F on AB, E on CA, D on BC), is largest when its sides are the mid-lines of the triangle. The well-known extremum result is that such a parallelogram can cover at most half the triangle’s area, so the whole problem reduces to finding the area of ΔABC and halving it.
Formula [Δ] = (1/2)|x1(y2−y3) + x2(y3−y1) + x3(y1−y2)|; max parallelogram = (1/2)[ΔABC]. If F divides AB in ratio t:(1−t), the parallelogram area is 2t(1−t)[Δ], maximised at t = 1/2, giving (1/2)[Δ].
Solution
1. List the vertices: A(4, −2), B(1, 1), C(9, −3).
2. Apply the coordinate area formula for ΔABC.
3. x1(y2−y3) = 4(1−(−3)) = 4·4 = 16.
4. x2(y3−y1) = 1((−3)−(−2)) = 1·(−1) = −1.
5. x3(y1−y2) = 9((−2)−1) = 9·(−3) = −27.
6. Sum = 16 − 1 − 27 = −12, so [ΔABC] = (1/2)|−12| = 6. The modulus discards the sign that only encodes vertex orientation.
7. Hence the maximum parallelogram area = (1/2)(6) = 3 square units.
  [ABC] = 1/2 |16 + (-1) + (-27)|
        = 1/2 |-12| = 6
  max AFDE = 1/2 * [ABC] = 1/2 * 6 = 3
⚡ JEE Trick Any parallelogram inscribed in a triangle with one shared vertex peaks at exactly half the triangle’s area, reached when the mobile vertices hit the mid-points of the sides. Never set up the general parallelogram — compute the triangle’s area and halve it.
Answer: 3 square units.
2Let the area of a ΔPQR with vertices P(5, 4), Q(−2, 4) and R(a, b) be 35 square units. Concept PQ is horizontal (both ends have y = 4), so the altitude from R to PQ is vertical and its x-coordinate equals the orthocentre’s x. That instantly pins a. The area fixes b up to a sign, and the orthocentre’s y-value selects the correct b. The centroid is then the plain average of the three vertices.
Formula [Δ] = (1/2)·base·height; centroid = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). For a horizontal base PQ the height is just |b − 4|, and the vertical altitude through R forces a = x-coordinate of the orthocentre.
Solution
1. PQ lies on y = 4 with length |5 − (−2)| = 7.
2. Area: (1/2)·7·|b − 4| = 35 ⇒ |b − 4| = 10 ⇒ b = 14 or b = −6.
3. Altitude from R ⊥ PQ is vertical, x = a; it meets the orthocentre, so a = 2.
4. Altitude from P(5,4) ⊥ QR: slope QR = (b−4)/4, so its slope = −4/(b−4). At the orthocentre x = 2, giving y = 4 + 12/(b−4).
5. Set y = 14/5: 14/5 − 4 = −6/5 = 12/(b−4) ⇒ b − 4 = −10 ⇒ b = −6.
6. Centroid: c = (5 − 2 + 2)/3 = 5/3, d = (4 + 4 − 6)/3 = 2/3.
7. Hence c + 2d = 5/3 + 2·(2/3) = 5/3 + 4/3 = 9/3 = 3.
  PQ: y=4, length 7 ;  (1/2)(7)|b-4|=35 -> b=-6 or 14
  R-altitude vertical -> a = 2
  y_ortho = 4 + 12/(b-4) = 14/5 -> b = -6
  c = (5-2+2)/3 = 5/3 , d = (4+4-6)/3 = 2/3
  c + 2d = 5/3 + 4/3 = 3
⚡ JEE Trick When two vertices share a y-value the base is horizontal: the third vertex’s altitude is vertical, so its x-coordinate is the orthocentre’s x with no algebra. Use the area for the magnitude of the free coordinate and the given centre to fix its sign.
Answer: (c) 3.
3Let ABC be the triangle such that the equations of lines AB and AC are 3y − x = 2 and x + y = 2, respectively, and the points B and C lie on the x-axis. If P is the orthocentre of the triangle ABC, then the area of the triangle PBC is equal to: (a) 4 (b) 8 (c) 10 (d) 6 [JEE Main 2025(7 Apr. Shift-1)] (Level-6)Orthocentre and area of a triangle from line equationsLet ABC be the Triangle such that the equations of the lines AB and AC be 3y-x=2 and x+y=2 | JEE pyq🟢 Exactd6
Concept B and C are where the two given lines cut the x-axis, so set y = 0 in each. A is where the lines meet. Because BC lies along the x-axis, the orthocentre is easy: the altitude from A is vertical, and the altitude from B is perpendicular to AC. Their intersection is P, and the area of ΔPBC is base BC times the height |yP|.
Formula perp. slopes: m·m′ = −1; [Δ] = (1/2)·BC·|yP|. With BC on the x-axis the altitude from the apex is vertical, and any triangle on that base has height equal to the vertical distance of the opposite vertex.
Solution
1. B on AB (3y − x = 2) with y = 0: −x = 2 ⇒ B(−2, 0).
2. C on AC (x + y = 2) with y = 0: x = 2 ⇒ C(2, 0).
3. A = AB ∩ AC: from x = 2 − y, 3y − (2 − y) = 2 ⇒ 4y = 4 ⇒ A(1, 1).
4. Altitude from A ⊥ BC (x-axis) is the vertical line x = 1.
5. Altitude from B ⊥ AC: slope AC = −1, so this altitude has slope 1 through B: y = x + 2. Two altitudes are enough to locate the orthocentre.
6. Intersect x = 1 with y = x + 2: y = 3, so orthocentre P(1, 3).
7. [PBC]: base BC = |2 − (−2)| = 4, height = |yP| = 3 ⇒ (1/2)(4)(3) = 6.
  B: 3(0)-x=2 -> x=-2 ;  C: x+0=2 -> x=2
  A: 3y-(2-y)=2 -> y=1 -> A(1,1)
  alt A: x=1 ;  alt B: y=x+2  ->  P(1,3)
  [PBC] = 1/2 * BC * |y_P| = 1/2 * 4 * 3 = 6
⚡ JEE Trick When the base lies on a coordinate axis, grab the orthocentre with two altitudes only: one is the vertical through the apex, the other is the perpendicular from a base vertex to the opposite side. Area then needs no distance formula — just base along the axis and the apex’s height.
Answer: (d) 6.
4Let k be an integer such that the triangle with vertices (k, −3k), (5, k) and (−k, 2) has area 28 sq units. Then, the orthocentre of this triangle is at the point: (a) (2, 1/2) (b) (2, −1/2) (c) (1, 3/4) (d) (1, −3/4) [JEE Main 2017] (Level-7)Area condition for k, then orthocentreLet k be an integer such that triangle with vertices (k, −3k), (5, k) and (−k, 2) has area 28 sq.🟢 Exacta(2, 1/2)
Concept First turn the area condition into a quadratic in k and keep the integer root. With k fixed the three vertices are known numbers; two of them share y = 2, so that side is horizontal and the orthocentre falls out from a vertical altitude and one perpendicular altitude.
Formula 2[Δ] = |x1(y2−y3)+x2(y3−y1)+x3(y1−y2)|. Equating this to 2×28 = 56 gives |5k2+13k+10| = 56; only the integer solution is admissible.
Solution
1. Vertices (k, −3k), (5, k), (−k, 2). Expand the area sum.
2. k(k−2) + 5(2+3k) + (−k)(−4k) = 5k2 + 13k + 10.
3. Area 28 ⇒ |5k2 + 13k + 10| = 56.
4. 5k2 + 13k − 46 = 0 ⇒ (k − 2)(5k + 23) = 0 ⇒ k = 2 (integer). The other branch 5k2+13k+66 = 0 has no real root.
5. So the vertices are A(2, −6), B(5, 2), C(−2, 2); BC is horizontal (y = 2).
6. Altitude from A is vertical: x = 2. Altitude from B ⊥ AC: slope AC = (2−(−6))/(−2−2) = −2, so slope 1/2 through B: y − 2 = (1/2)(x − 5).
7. At x = 2: y = 2 + (1/2)(−3) = 1/2, so orthocentre (2, 1/2).
  area sum = 5k^2 + 13k + 10 ,  |.| = 56
  5k^2 + 13k - 46 = 0 -> (k-2)(5k+23)=0 -> k=2
  A(2,-6), B(5,2), C(-2,2)
  alt A: x=2 ;  alt B: y-2=(1/2)(x-5)
  x=2 -> y = 1/2  ->  (2, 1/2)
⚡ JEE Trick Area questions with an unknown vertex coordinate are quadratics in disguise — the integer/natural constraint usually kills one root. Once the points are numeric, spot any horizontal or vertical side to get one altitude for free before touching slopes.
Answer: (a) (2, 1/2).
5The number of points, having both coordinates as integers, that lie in the interior of the triangle with vertices (0, 0), (0, 41) and (41, 0) is: (a) 820 (b) 780 (c) 901 (d) 861 [JEE Main 2015] (Level-7)Lattice points interior to a triangleThe number of points, having both coordinates are integers, that lie in the interior of the triangle🟢 Exactb780
Concept The triangle has its two legs on the coordinate axes and its hypotenuse on the line x + y = 41. “Interior” is strict: a lattice point counts only if it stays off all three boundary segments, i.e. x ≥ 1, y ≥ 1 and x + y ≤ 40 (strictly below the hypotenuse). Grouping the admissible points by the fixed value of the sum x + y turns the two-dimensional count into a one-dimensional arithmetic series that sums in a single step.
Formula for fixed s = x + y, #{x ≥ 1, y ≥ 1} = s − 1; total = ∑(s − 1). Interior means all three strict inequalities x > 0, y > 0, x + y < 41, i.e. x + y runs from 2 up to 40.
Solution
1. Region: x > 0, y > 0, x + y < 41 with x, y integers.
2. So x ≥ 1, y ≥ 1 and x + y ≤ 40.
3. Fix s = x + y. Then x runs 1 .. s−1, giving s − 1 points for each s.
4. s ranges from 2 (x = y = 1) up to 40.
5. Total = ∑s=240 (s − 1) = 1 + 2 + … + 39.
6. = 39·40/2 = 780. Equivalently C(40, 2) = 780, the number of ways to drop two of 40 gaps.
7. Points on the axes or on the hypotenuse are boundary, not interior, so they are excluded — the count stays 780.
  interior: x>=1, y>=1, x+y<=40
  for x+y=s : (s-1) points , s = 2 .. 40
  total = 1+2+...+39 = 39*40/2 = 780
⚡ JEE Trick For the right triangle with legs n on the axes, the strictly-interior lattice count is (n−1)(n−2)/2. Here n = 41 gives 40·39/2 = 780 in one stroke — and it equals C(n−1, 2), a handy cross-check.
Answer: (b) 780.
6The x-coordinate of the incentre of the triangle that has the coordinates of mid-points of its sides as (0, 1), (1, 1) and (1, 0) is: (a) 2 + √2 (b) 2 − √2 (c) 1 + √2 (d) 1 − √2 [JEE Main 2013] (Level-6)Incentre of a triangle from its mid-point triangleThe x-coordinate of the incentre of the triangle that has the coordinates of mid points of its sides🟢 Exactb2 − √2
Concept The mid-point triangle is given, so first recover the real vertices (each vertex is the sum of two mid-points minus the third). The recovered triangle is right-angled and isosceles, after which the incentre’s x-coordinate is the side-weighted average of the vertices’ x-coordinates.
Formula vertex = Mi + Mj − Mk; Ix = (a·xA + b·xB + c·xC)/(a + b + c). a, b, c are the side lengths opposite A, B, C; the incentre is their weighted centroid.
Solution
1. Mid-points P(0,1), Q(1,1), R(1,0). Recover vertices.
2. A = P + Q − R = (0, 2); B = Q + R − P = (2, 0); C = R + P − Q = (0, 0).
3. Side lengths: a = BC = 2, b = CA = 2, c = AB = √(22+22) = 2√2.
4. a is opposite A(0,2), b opposite B(2,0), c opposite C(0,0). Opposite-side weighting is what distinguishes the incentre from the centroid.
5. Ix = (2·0 + 2·2 + 2√2·0)/(2 + 2 + 2√2) = 4/(4 + 2√2).
6. = 2/(2 + √2); multiply top and bottom by (2 − √2): 2(2 − √2)/(4 − 2) = 2 − √2.
7. So the x-coordinate of the incentre is 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586.
  A=P+Q-R=(0,2), B=Q+R-P=(2,0), C=R+P-Q=(0,0)
  a=BC=2, b=CA=2, c=AB=2sqrt2
  I_x = (2*0+2*2+2sqrt2*0)/(4+2sqrt2)
      = 4/(4+2sqrt2) = 2/(2+sqrt2) = 2 - sqrt2
⚡ JEE Trick Rebuild a triangle from its mid-points with “sum two, subtract the third”. For the incentre weight each vertex by the length of the opposite side — mixing that up with the equal-weight centroid is the classic trap on this exact problem.
Answer: (b) 2 − √2.
7Let O(0, 0), P(3, 4), Q(6, 0) be the vertices of the triangle OPQ. The point R inside the triangle OPQ is such that the triangles OPR, PQR, OQR are of equal area. The coordinates of R are: (a) (4/3, 3) (b) (3, 2/3) (c) (3, 4/3) (d) (4/3, 2/3) [IIT-JEE 2007] (Level-9)Equal-area interior point is the centroidIIT JEE CONIC SECTIONS Let O(0,0), P(3,4), and Q(6,0) be the vertices of triangle OPQ🟢 Exactc(3, 4/3)
Concept If an interior point R splits a triangle into three sub-triangles OPR, PQR and OQR of equal area, then each sub-triangle must be exactly one-third of the whole. The unique point with that balancing property is the centroid: a sub-triangle on a given side has area proportional to R’s perpendicular distance from that side, and making all three equal is precisely the condition that R is the average of the three vertices. So the whole question reduces to a one-line centroid calculation.
Formula centroid G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). [OPR] = [PQR] = [OQR] = (1/3)[OPQ] holds exactly and only when R = G.
Solution
1. Vertices O(0, 0), P(3, 4), Q(6, 0).
2. Equal areas of OPR, PQR, OQR ⇒ R is the centroid of ΔOPQ.
3. xR = (0 + 3 + 6)/3 = 9/3 = 3.
4. yR = (0 + 4 + 0)/3 = 4/3.
5. So R = (3, 4/3). R lies inside ΔOPQ, consistent with “point inside the triangle”.
6. Check [OPQ]: base OQ = 6 on the x-axis, height = 4 ⇒ area 12; each sub-triangle = 4 = (1/3)(12). Confirms the equal-area split.
7. Hence the coordinates of R are (3, 4/3).
  equal three areas  ->  R = centroid
  x = (0+3+6)/3 = 3
  y = (0+4+0)/3 = 4/3
  R = (3, 4/3)
⚡ JEE Trick “Interior point making the three corner triangles equal in area” is code for the centroid — no area equations needed, just average the vertices. The same point also makes the three medians’ sub-triangles equal, a quick sanity anchor.
Answer: (c) (3, 4/3).
8Three distinct points A, B and C are given in the 2-dimensional coordinates plane such that the ratio of the distance of any one of them from the point (1, 0) to the distance from the point (−1, 0) is equal to 1/3. Then, the circumcentre of the triangle ABC is at the point: (a) (5/4, 0) (b) (5/2, 0) (c) (5/3, 0) (d) (0, 0) [AIEEE 2009] (Level-9)Apollonius circle; circumcentre of ABCThree distinct points A, B and C are given in the 2-dimensional coordinate plane such that the ratio🟢 Exacta(5/4, 0)
Concept Every point whose distances to two fixed points are in a constant ratio (not 1) lies on an Apollonius circle. All three of A, B, C satisfy the same ratio condition, so they all lie on one such circle. That circle is therefore the circumcircle of ΔABC, and its centre is the circumcentre.
Formula PA/PB = k ⇒ PA2 = k2PB2. Squaring the ratio clears the roots and gives the equation of a circle; its centre is read straight off.
Solution
1. Let P(x, y) with dist to (1,0) over dist to (−1,0) = 1/3.
2. 9[(x−1)2 + y2] = (x+1)2 + y2.
3. 9x2 − 18x + 9 + 9y2 = x2 + 2x + 1 + y2.
4. 8x2 + 8y2 − 20x + 8 = 0 ⇒ x2 + y2 − (5/2)x + 1 = 0.
5. Compare with x2 + y2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0: 2g = −5/2, 2f = 0. Centre of such a circle is (−g, −f).
6. Centre = (5/4, 0); since A, B, C lie on this circle, the circumcentre is (5/4, 0).
7. Radius2 = (5/4)2 − 1 = 9/16 > 0, so the circle is real. Confirms three distinct points can indeed lie on it.
  9[(x-1)^2+y^2] = (x+1)^2+y^2
  8x^2+8y^2-20x+8=0
  x^2+y^2-(5/2)x+1=0
  centre (5/4, 0)  ->  circumcentre
⚡ JEE Trick A fixed distance-ratio (≠ 1) always traces an Apollonius circle; the three unknown points are red herrings — their common circle is the circumcircle, so just find the centre of that locus. The two foci (1,0) and (−1,0) are inverse points, both on the x-axis, so the centre must be on the x-axis too.
Answer: (a) (5/4, 0).
9Let A(h, k), B(1, 1) and C(2, 1) be the vertices of a right angled triangle with AC as its hypotenuse. If the area of the triangle is 1, then the set of values which ‘k’ can take is given by: (a) {1, 3} (b) {0, 2} (c) {−1, 3} (d) {−3, −2} [AIEEE 2007] (Level-8)Right-angle and area conditions on a vertexLet A(h, k), B(1, 1) and C(2, 1) be the vertices of a right angled triangle with AC as its hypotenuse🟢 Exactc{−1, 3}
Concept In a right-angled triangle the hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle, so naming AC the hypotenuse places the right angle at the remaining vertex B. That makes the two legs BA and BC perpendicular. Since BC is horizontal here, BA must be vertical, which forces A to lie directly above or below B and pins h. The area then fixes how far A sits from B, and the ± from that distance delivers two symmetric values of k.
Formula BA·BC = 0 (right angle at B); [Δ] = (1/2)|BA||BC|. For a right angle the legs are BA and BC, so the area is half their product — no sine needed.
Solution
1. B(1, 1), C(2, 1): vector BC = (1, 0), length 1.
2. Right angle at B ⇒ BA ⊥ BC: BA·BC = (h−1)(1) + (k−1)(0) = 0.
3. So h − 1 = 0 ⇒ h = 1; thus A = (1, k), directly above/below B.
4. Leg BA has length |k − 1|. A and B share the x-coordinate 1, so BA is vertical.
5. Area = (1/2)|BA||BC| = (1/2)|k − 1|(1) = 1.
6. Hence |k − 1| = 2 ⇒ k − 1 = ±2 ⇒ k = 3 or k = −1.
7. So the set of admissible k is {−1, 3}. Both keep A off line BC, so a genuine right triangle forms in each case.
  BC=(1,0), |BC|=1 ;  right angle at B
  BA.BC=0 -> (h-1)=0 -> h=1 , A=(1,k)
  (1/2)|k-1|*1 = 1 -> |k-1| = 2
  k = 3  or  k = -1   ->  {-1, 3}
⚡ JEE Trick “Hypotenuse AC” immediately locates the right angle at B — set the dot product of the two legs from B to zero. With one leg horizontal, the perpendicular leg is vertical, so the area collapses to (1/2)·base·height and the ± gives both answers at once.
Answer: (c) {−1, 3}.
10If a vertex of a triangle is (1, 1) and the mid-points of two sides through this vertex are (−1, 2) and (3, 2), then the centroid of the triangle is: (a) (1/3, 7/3) (b) (1, 7/3) (c) (−1/3, 7/3) (d) (−1, 7/3) [AIEEE 2005] (Level-5)Centroid from a vertex and two mid-pointsIf a vertex of a triangle is (1, 1) and the midpoints of two sides through this vertex are (−1, 2)🟢 Exactb(1, 7/3)
Concept The two given mid-points are the mid-points of the two sides that pass through the known vertex A. A side is a segment, and a mid-point is the average of its two ends, so each mid-point together with the shared vertex A fixes the opposite end of that side by the “double the mid, subtract the known end” rule. Recover both far vertices this way, and the centroid is simply the average of all three vertices — no section formula or distance work is needed anywhere.
Formula if M is the mid-point of AB then B = 2M − A; G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). Mid-point M = (A+B)/2 rearranges to B = 2M − A, component-wise.
Solution
1. Vertex A(1, 1). Let the mid-points of AB and AC be (−1, 2) and (3, 2).
2. B = 2(−1, 2) − (1, 1) = (−2 − 1, 4 − 1) = (−3, 3).
3. C = 2(3, 2) − (1, 1) = (6 − 1, 4 − 1) = (5, 3).
4. Vertices: A(1, 1), B(−3, 3), C(5, 3). Both new vertices sit at y = 3, as a check both mid-points have y = 2 = (1+3)/2.
5. Centroid x = (1 − 3 + 5)/3 = 3/3 = 1.
6. Centroid y = (1 + 3 + 3)/3 = 7/3.
7. Hence the centroid is (1, 7/3).
  B = 2(-1,2)-(1,1) = (-3, 3)
  C = 2( 3,2)-(1,1) = ( 5, 3)
  G_x = (1-3+5)/3 = 1
  G_y = (1+3+3)/3 = 7/3   ->  (1, 7/3)
⚡ JEE Trick Recover a missing endpoint instantly with B = 2M − A — no section formula. Shortcut: the centroid also equals A/3 plus two-thirds of the average of the two mid-points, so you can reach (1, 7/3) without even naming B and C.
Answer: (b) (1, 7/3).
