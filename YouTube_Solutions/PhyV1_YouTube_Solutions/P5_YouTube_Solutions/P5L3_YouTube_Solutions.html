1What is the sine law in the context of triangle ABC? (a) sinA+sinB+sinC=0 (b) sinA/a = sinB/b = sinC/c (c) sinA·sinB·sinC = abc (d) sinA = sinB − sinCSine rule (law of sines)Prove a/sinA = b/sinB = c/sinC = 2R — Sine Law, Class 11 Trigonometry🟢 Exactbsin A / a = sin B / b = sin C / c
Concept The Law of Sines says that in any triangle ABC each side is proportional to the sine of the angle opposite it, and the common ratio equals the diameter 2R of the circumscribed circle. It is what you reach for when you know an angle–opposite-side pair plus one more angle or side. The three distractors are structural look-alikes — a sum, a product, a difference — each of which fails either dimensionally or on a simple test case, so recognising the correct equal-ratio shape is the whole battle here.
Formula a/sinA = b/sinB = c/sinC = 2R, equivalently sinA/a = sinB/b = sinC/c. Taking reciprocals term-by-term of an equal-ratio chain preserves the equality, so the two forms are the same statement — option (b) is just the reciprocal form. Here a, b, c are side lengths; sines are dimensionless; the ratio side/sinθ carries the unit of length.
Solution
1. Drop the altitude h from vertex C onto AB. In the two right triangles it makes, h = b·sinA and also h = a·sinB.Same height, read off from either base angle.
2. Equate: b·sinA = a·sinB ⇒ sinA/a = sinB/b.
3. Repeat with the altitude from A ⇒ sinB/b = sinC/c. Chaining gives sinA/a = sinB/b = sinC/c — option (b).
4. Test (a) sinA+sinB+sinC = 0: every angle of a triangle lies in (0°,180°), so every sine is positive and the sum can never be zero. ✗
5. Test (c) sinA·sinB·sinC = abc: the left side is a pure number, the right side has units length³ — a dimensional mismatch. ✗
6. Test (d) sinA = sinB − sinC: try an equilateral triangle, sin60° = sin60° − sin60° = 0, plainly false. ✗

Concept A body on a smooth (frictionless) horizontal surface has only two horizontal forces on it: the applied 10 N and the unknown F. The vertical forces (weight mg down, normal N up) already cancel by themselves and play no part. "Equilibrium" for a particle means the vector sum of all forces is zero, so along the one direction that matters — the horizontal — the two forces must be equal in size and opposite in direction. The figure shows the 10 N pushing the block to the right and F pushing to the left, i.e. they are collinear and opposed, which is what makes this a one-line problem.
Formula ΣF⃗ = 0 N ⇒ componentwise ΣFx = 0 N and ΣFy = 0 N. A vector equation is only satisfied when every component vanishes separately, so one vector equation gives two scalar equations here. Forces in N; the mass m is irrelevant because equilibrium means a = 0 m s⁻², so ΣF = ma = m × 0 = 0 N whatever m is.
Solution
1. Assumptions: surface smooth ⇒ friction f = 0 N; the block is a particle; the two forces are horizontal and opposite (as drawn).Stating this matters — with friction there would be a whole range of F values, not one.
2. Vertical direction: N − mg = 0 N ⇒ N = mg.This just fixes N; it contains no information about F, so we set it aside.
3. Horizontal direction, taking rightwards as +x: the 10 N acts along +x, F acts along −x.Fix a sign convention before adding, or the two forces would wrongly sum to 20 N.
4. Write ΣFx = 0 N: (+10 N) + (−F) = 0 N.
5. Solve: F = 10 N.The mass never enters; the answer is independent of m.
6. Dimensional check: both terms are forces [M L T⁻²], and the result carries the unit N. ✓

Concept A block on a smooth incline tends to slide down because gravity has a component along the slope. To hold it still, the applied force F must be aimed up the plane and exactly cancel that component. The trick is to resolve the weight mg into two perpendicular pieces — one along the incline (mg sinθ, the part that slides the block) and one perpendicular to it (mg cosθ, the part the surface pushes back on via the normal force N). Because the plane is smooth, no friction can help, so F alone must do the job.
Formula Along the plane: F = mg sinθ; perpendicular to it: N = mg cosθ. Units: m in kg, g in m s⁻², so mg is in kg m s⁻² = N; sinθ and cosθ are dimensionless. Choosing axes along and perpendicular to the slope (rather than true horizontal/vertical) puts N entirely on one axis, so it never contaminates the F equation. Data: m = 10 kg, θ = 53°, g = 10 m s⁻², sin53° = 0.8, cos53° = 0.6.

4A weight W is suspended from the midpoint of a rope with its ends at the same level, so the rope is no longer horizontal. Find the minimum tension required to make the rope completely straightTension in a sagging ropeA weight w is suspended from the midpoint of a rope, whose ends are at the same level · same problem, alternate solution🟢 Exact∞ — not possible
Concept A weight W hangs from the midpoint of a rope whose two ends are pinned at the same height. The load makes the rope sag into a shallow V, and we ask what tension would pull that V flat into a perfectly horizontal straight line. The key physical fact is that a horizontal rope segment has no vertical component of tension, yet the weight is purely vertical — so a truly straight rope could not hold up any weight at all. That contradiction is what forces the answer to be "infinite / impossible".
Formula With each half of the rope making angle θ to the horizontal, vertical equilibrium of the midpoint gives 2T sinθ = W ⇒ T = W / (2 sinθ). Only the vertical components 2·(T sinθ) fight gravity; the horizontal components cancel each other by symmetry. Units: W and T in the same force unit; θ dimensionless.
Solution
1. Free the midpoint: three forces meet there — the two rope tensions T (each along its half) and the weight W straight down.
2. Vertical balance: 2T sinθ = W, so T = W/(2 sinθ). "Straight and horizontal" means the sag angle θ → 0°.
3. As θ → 0°, sinθ → 0, hence T = W/(2 sinθ) → ∞.The denominator collapses to zero while W stays finite, so the tension grows without bound.
4. Physical reading: a horizontal rope offers zero upward pull, so it can never support a downward weight — the rope must sag, however slightly.
5. Numerical feel: θ = 10° needs T ≈ 2.9 W; θ = 1° needs T ≈ 28.6 W; θ = 0.1° needs T ≈ 286 W — climbing to infinity as θ shrinks.
6. Conclusion: the minimum tension to make it completely straight is unattainable — no finite force will do it.

      30°          1.00 W
      10°          2.88 W
       1°         28.6  W
       0°           ∞         <-- answer (not possible)
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a loaded string is asked to be "perfectly horizontal", answer infinite tension on sight — the 1/sinθ blow-up is the signature. Trap: students plug in a convenient θ and quote a finite number; the question is really testing the limit θ → 0°, where no real rope can ever go while carrying a load.
Answer: ∞ — a straight (horizontal) rope cannot support the weight, so the required tension is infinite; not possible.

Concept This is a two-string suspension with the geometry hidden inside a Pythagorean triple. Two pegs A and B sit 13 cm apart on a horizontal line; a single 17 cm thread runs A → body → B, and the two segments meet at 90°. Because the segments are perpendicular and their far ends span 13 cm, triangle ABC is right-angled at the body, so a² + b² = 13². Combined with a + b = 17 cm this pins both segment lengths exactly. Only once the geometry is known can the tensions be found by resolving the weight.
Formula Geometry: a + b = 17 cm and a² + b² = (13 cm)² (Pythagoras, right angle at the body). Statics for two perpendicular strings carrying weight W: Ta = W·(b/13), Tb = W·(a/13), with the identity Ta² + Tb² = W². Each tension is proportional to the opposite segment because the string's angle with the vertical has cosine = (opposite segment)/(hypotenuse AB). Units: lengths in cm (they appear only as ratios, so they cancel), W and T in kg wt.
Solution
1. Assumptions: strings light and inextensible; the body is a particle; pegs A and B lie on the same horizontal line; the two segments are perpendicular (given).
2. Let the segments be a and b, with a + b = 17 cm and, since the angle at the body is 90°, a² + b² = AB² = 13² = 169 cm².
3. Square the sum: (a + b)² = 17² = 289 cm² ⇒ a² + b² + 2ab = 289 cm².
4. Substitute a² + b² = 169 cm²: 169 + 2ab = 289 ⇒ 2ab = 120 cm² ⇒ ab = 60 cm².
5. So a and b are the roots of t² − 17t + 60 = 0 ⇒ t = [17 ± √(289 − 240)]/2 = (17 ± 7)/2 ⇒ t = 12 cm or 5 cm.Check: 5 + 12 = 17 ✓ and 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13² ✓ — the classic 5-12-13 triple.
6. So the shorter segment = 5 cm and the longer segment = 12 cm.
7. Now the tensions. W = 169 kg wt acts vertically down at the body; the two tensions act along the strings toward their pegs and are mutually perpendicular. Resolving horizontally (they must cancel sideways) and vertically (together they must carry W) gives each tension = W × (the other segment)/13:
  • T along the shorter (5 cm) part = W × 12/13 = 169 kg wt × 12/13 = 13 × 12 = 156 kg wt
  • T along the longer (12 cm) part = W × 5/13 = 169 kg wt × 5/13 = 13 × 5 = 65 kg wt

1What is the condition for a body to be in equilibrium under multiple coplanar forces? (a) body moves in a straight line (b) net force and net torque must be zero (c) body rotates at constant speed (d) net acceleration must be non-zeroConditions for equilibrium (coplanar forces)General conditions of Equilibrium of Coplanar Forces🟡 SimilarbThe net force and net torque on the body must be zero
Concept "Equilibrium" for an extended body (not merely a point particle) is a double condition, and that is exactly what this question probes. A body can have zero net force yet still spin up — picture two equal and opposite forces applied at opposite edges of a wheel: they cancel as vectors (ΣF⃗ = 0 N) but form a couple that spins the wheel. So killing translation is not enough; you must kill rotation too. Full static equilibrium therefore needs net force zero AND net torque zero.
Formula ΣF⃗ = 0 N (translational equilibrium ⇒ linear acceleration a⃗ = 0 m s⁻²) and Στ⃗ = 0 N m (rotational equilibrium ⇒ angular acceleration α = 0 rad s⁻²). These follow from Newton's second law in its two forms — F⃗ = ma⃗ (N = kg m s⁻²) and τ⃗ = Iα (N m = kg m² s⁻² per rad s⁻²) — by setting each acceleration to zero. For coplanar forces in the xy-plane this is three scalar equations: ΣFx = 0 N, ΣFy = 0 N, Στz = 0 N m.
Solution
1. Translation: if ΣF⃗ ≠ 0 N then a⃗ = ΣF⃗/m ≠ 0 m s⁻², so the centre of mass accelerates — not equilibrium. Hence ΣF⃗ = 0 N is necessary.
2. Rotation: ΣF⃗ = 0 N alone is not sufficient. Counter-example: forces of +10 N and −10 N applied 0.5 m either side of the centre give ΣF = 0 N, but Στ = 10 N × 0.5 m + 10 N × 0.5 m = 10 N m ≠ 0 — the body spins.This single counter-example is what kills every "net force zero is enough" answer.
3. So we must additionally impose Στ⃗ = 0 N m, which forces α = Στ/I = 0 rad s⁻².
4. Both conditions together ⇒ option (b): "the net force and net torque on the body must be zero."
5. Eliminate the distractors by testing each against the two conditions:
 option                                    verdict
 (a) body must move in a straight line     NO - describes motion, not a condition;
                                                equilibrium allows rest OR uniform velocity
 (b) net force AND net torque zero         YES - Sum F = 0 N and Sum tau = 0 N m
 (c) body rotates at constant speed        NO - that is rotational equilibrium only
                                                (alpha = 0), says nothing about Sum F
 (d) net acceleration must be non-zero     NO - exactly backwards: a = 0 m/s^2 is required
⚡ JEE Trick Say it as "no push, no twist" — ΣF⃗ = 0 N kills translation, Στ⃗ = 0 N m kills rotation. Useful fact: once ΣF⃗ = 0 N, the torque sum is the same about every axis, so you may take moments about whichever point is most convenient (usually where an unknown acts, so it vanishes). Trap: equilibrium does not mean "at rest" — a block sliding at constant velocity is in equilibrium, because a⃗ = 0 m s⁻². That is why (a) and (d) are both wrong for the same underlying reason: they confuse the state of motion with the force condition.
Answer: (b) The net force and net torque on the body must be zero.

Concept Torque (moment of a force) measures a force's turning effect about an axis. What matters is not just how hard you push but where — specifically the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force, called the moment arm. A force aimed straight at the axis produces no turning at all; the same force applied far out on a long spanner turns easily. The distractors pair force with quantities (velocity, time, area) that have nothing to do with rotation.
Formula τ = F × r⊥, or in vector form τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗ with magnitude τ = F r sinθ. r sinθ is exactly the perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action — the moment arm — so the two forms agree. Units: F in N, r in m ⇒ τ in N·m.
Solution
1. Definition: torque = force × moment arm, where the moment arm is the perpendicular distance r⊥ from the axis to the force's line of action.
2. Check units of each option paired with force (N): N×(m/s) = W (power), N×s = N·s (impulse), N×m = N·m (torque ✓), N×m² = odd. Only "perpendicular distance" gives the N·m of torque.
3. Test (a) velocity, (b) time: these appear in power (F·v) and impulse (F·t), not in a static turning effect. ✗
4. Test (d) area: no rotational quantity is force×area (that is pressure inverted). ✗
5. Geometric check: push a door at the hinge (r⊥ = 0) and it will not turn — confirming distance, not force alone, sets the torque.τ = F·0 = 0 even for a large F.
6. So torque is the product of force and the perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation — option (c).

3In Lami's Theorem, how many forces are acting on the particle? (a) Two (b) Four (c) Three (d) FiveLami's theorem — number of forcesEquilibrium of Coplanar Force Systems — 3-Force Body & Lami's Theorem🟡 SimilarcThree
Concept Lami's theorem is a shortcut for one very specific situation: exactly three coplanar, concurrent, non-collinear forces holding a particle in equilibrium. The number three is not arbitrary — it is forced by geometry. Three vectors summing to zero can be laid head-to-tail to close a triangle, and the sine rule of that triangle is Lami's theorem. Two forces would only close a degenerate "triangle" (a straight line); four or more would close a polygon, for which no single sine relation exists.
Formula P / sin α = Q / sin β = R / sin γ, where P, Q, R are the three force magnitudes in N and α, β, γ are the angles (in degrees) opposite each — α is the angle between Q and R, and so on — with α + β + γ = 360°. Each ratio is a force ÷ a dimensionless sine, so every ratio carries the unit N and the three are dimensionally consistent.
Solution
1. Equilibrium of a particle requires P⃗ + Q⃗ + R⃗ = 0 N.The vector sum of all forces acting at the point must vanish.
2. Three vectors summing to zero, drawn head-to-tail, form a closed triangle — the triangle of forces.
3. Apply the sine rule to that triangle: side ÷ sin(opposite angle) is constant. Translating the triangle's interior angles back to the angles between the forces at the particle (each interior angle = 180° − the corresponding angle between forces, and sin(180° − θ) = sin θ) yields P/sin α = Q/sin β = R/sin γ.
4. This construction requires exactly three forces — option (c).
5. Why the other counts fail:
 number of forces   head-to-tail figure    Lami applies?
 (a) Two            a straight line        NO  - degenerate: forces just equal & opposite
 (c) Three          a closed TRIANGLE      YES - sine rule works = Lami's theorem
 (b) Four           a quadrilateral        NO  - no unique sine relation
 (d) Five           a pentagon             NO  - no unique sine relation

 sanity: alpha + beta + gamma = 360 deg  (the three angles fill the turn around the particle)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember "Lami = triangle = three." If a problem hands you four or more concurrent forces, do not force Lami — resolve into components (ΣFx = 0 N, ΣFy = 0 N), which works for any number. Conversely, whenever you see exactly three forces on a knot or peg, Lami is usually the fastest route because it skips resolution entirely. Trap: forgetting the Lami angles sum to 360°, not 180° — the 180° belongs to the force triangle's interior angles, not to the angles at the particle.
Answer: (c) Three.
4Which of the following is NOT a condition for applying Lami's Theorem? (a) forces non-collinear (b) forces concurrent (c) forces in equilibrium (d) forces perpendicularLami's theorem — conditionsProof of Lami's Theorem — Definition & Conditions🟡 SimilardForces must be perpendicular
Concept A "find the odd one out" test of Lami's preconditions. Lami works because three forces summing to zero close a triangle whose sine rule is the theorem. For that argument to run, the forces must (i) lie in one plane (coplanar), (ii) act at one point (concurrent), (iii) be non-collinear so the triangle is genuine rather than squashed flat, and (iv) actually be in equilibrium so the triangle closes. Nowhere does the derivation require the forces to be at 90° — in fact Lami is most useful precisely when they are not.
Formula P / sin α = Q / sin β = R / sin γ with P, Q, R in N and α + β + γ = 360°. Valid iff: three forces · coplanar · concurrent · non-collinear · in equilibrium (ΣF⃗ = 0 N). The angles are entirely free apart from summing to 360°; the formula never assumes any of them is 90°.
Solution
1. Test (a) non-collinear: if all three forces lay along a single line the "triangle" would collapse to a segment of zero area and every sine would be 0 — the theorem becomes meaningless (division by zero). So this is required.
2. Test (b) concurrent: if the forces acted at different points they could produce a net torque and rotate the body even with ΣF⃗ = 0 N. Lami is a particle theorem. So this is required.
3. Test (c) in equilibrium: only if P⃗ + Q⃗ + R⃗ = 0 N does the head-to-tail triangle close; an open figure has no sine rule to apply. So this is required.
4. Test (d) perpendicular: nothing in the derivation constrains the angles beyond α + β + γ = 360°. Concrete counter-example — the standard symmetric case α = β = γ = 120° contains no right angle anywhere, yet Lami holds perfectly. So perpendicularity is NOT a condition.A single valid counter-example is enough to disqualify a claimed "requirement".
5. Therefore the odd one out, and the answer, is (d).

 (a) forces non-collinear         YES            else the force triangle has zero area
 (b) forces concurrent            YES            else a net torque can appear
 (c) forces in equilibrium        YES            else the force triangle does not close
 (d) forces perpendicular         NO   <-- ANSWER  angles are free; only sum = 360 deg

⚡ JEE Trick Checklist for Lami — "3 forces, 1 plane, 1 point, not in a line, adding to zero." Anything outside that list is the odd one out. Note the irony the examiner exploits: if the forces were perpendicular you would not need Lami at all — plain resolution or Pythagoras would be quicker — so perpendicularity is not a requirement but an alternative shortcut. Trap: "coplanar" and "concurrent" sound interchangeable but differ — coplanar = same plane, concurrent = same point of application.
Answer: (d) Forces must be perpendicular — this is NOT a condition for applying Lami's theorem.
5In a triangle of forces, each force vector is arranged: (a) Head to tail (b) Base to apex (c) Tail to head (d) Perpendicular to each otherTriangle of forcesTriangle of Forces: Introduction — 3 Forces in Equilibrium🟢 ExactcTail to head
Concept The triangle of forces is the graphical statement of equilibrium for exactly three concurrent forces: if a particle is in equilibrium under three forces, those forces, drawn as vectors in order, close up into a triangle. "In order" means each vector is drawn starting where the previous one ended — the standard head-to-tail (tip-to-tail) rule of vector addition. Because the forces balance, the third vector's head lands exactly back on the first vector's tail, closing the figure with zero gap.
Formula Equilibrium of three concurrent forces: F⃗1 + F⃗2 + F⃗3 = 0. Drawn head-to-tail this closes a triangle, whose sides (by Lami/sine rule) satisfy F1/sinα = F2/sinβ = F3/sinγ. A closed vector polygon is the geometric picture of "vector sum = 0"; three forces make the smallest such polygon — a triangle.
Solution
1. Vector addition places each next vector with its tail on the head of the one before — the head-to-tail construction.

Formula Geometry: 3² + 4² = 5² ⇒ angle at the body = 90°. Statics: Tshort = W × (long ÷ separation) and Tlong = W × (short ÷ separation), with the identity Tshort² + Tlong² = W².
