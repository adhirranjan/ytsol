1Viscosity of a liquid is defined as … (internal resistance to flow)Viscosity conceptViscosity - States of Matter - Chemistry Class 11🟢 ExactcThe internal resistance offered by a liquid to its flow
Concept When a liquid flows, its layers slide over one another at different speeds. Because the molecules attract their neighbours, a faster layer is dragged back and a slower layer is dragged forward — an internal friction that opposes the relative motion of the layers. Viscosity is the name for exactly this property: the resistance a liquid offers to its own flow. It is not resistance to compression, nor a surface effect — it is a bulk, layer-against-layer effect.
Formula Newton's law of viscosity, F = η·A·(v/x): the internal (viscous) force F is proportional to the area A of the layers and the velocity gradient v/x between them. The constant of proportionality η is the coefficient of viscosity — the very "internal resistance" the question describes; a thick liquid has a large η.
Solution
1. Picture two adjacent liquid layers moving at different speeds; molecular attraction makes each layer tug on the other.This tug always acts to reduce the speed difference — i.e. it opposes flow.
2. Identify that opposition: it is a resistance within the liquid, between its own layers, so it is an internal resistance to flow.
3. Match to the options: (c) "the internal resistance offered by a liquid to its flow" states this exactly.
4. Reject the distractors: resistance to compression describes bulk modulus, and a skin-like pull at the surface describes surface tension — neither is viscosity.
⚡ JEE Trick Fix the one-line tags: viscosity = internal friction opposing flow; surface tension = inward pull at the surface. Any option with the words "internal resistance / friction between layers" is viscosity. Trap: options mentioning "surface" or "compression" are deliberate decoys.
Answer: (c) The internal resistance offered by a liquid to its flow.

Concept Viscosity has two common units: the poise (the cgs unit, from dyn·s·cm⁻²) and the SI unit N·s·m⁻² (= Pa·s). Converting between them is a fixed one-step scaling because the definition itself gives the exchange rate. Knowing that rate — 1 poise = 0.1 SI units — turns every "poise → SI" question into a single multiplication.
Formula η(SI, N·s·m⁻²) = η(poise) × 0.1. 1 poise = 1 dyn·s·cm⁻²; converting 1 dyn = 10⁻⁵ N and 1 cm⁻² = 10⁴ m⁻² gives 10⁻⁵ ÷ 10⁻⁴ = 10⁻¹, i.e. 1 poise = 0.1 N·s·m⁻². N·s·m⁻² and Pa·s are the same unit.
Solution
1. Write the given value and the conversion factor: η = 2.5 poise, and 1 poise = 0.1 N·s·m⁻².
2. Multiply: η = 2.5 × 0.1 N·s·m⁻².Replacing "poise" by "0.1 N·s·m⁻²" and scaling the number is all that is needed.
3. Evaluate: 2.5 × 0.1 = 0.25 N·s·m⁻², which is option (a).

⚡ JEE Trick Poise → SI is just "÷10" (×0.1); SI → poise is "×10." So 2.5 poise "moves the decimal one place left" → 0.25 Pa·s. Trap: don't multiply by 10 (that is the reverse direction) — a poise number always shrinks going to SI.

5Soap film γ=0.040 N·m⁻¹ stretched 8→12 cm² — work done (two surfaces)Surface-energy workwatch?v=zhxZDkAzP_Y🟡 Similarb3.2×10−5 J
Concept Enlarging a liquid surface means creating new surface, and the work needed equals surface tension × the new area created. A soap film has two faces — a front and a back surface both in contact with air — so stretching it makes new area on both, and the area increase must be counted twice. Missing this factor of 2 is the single most common error in film problems.
Formula W = γ · 2·ΔA for a two-surface film, where γ is in N·m⁻¹, ΔA = (final − initial) area in m², and W in joule. Each unit of new surface stores γ joules; a film has two surfaces, hence the ×2. Also 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m².
Solution
1. Area increase on one face: ΔA = 12 − 8 = 4 cm² = 4 × 10⁻⁴ m².cm² → m² is ×10⁻⁴.
2. A film has two surfaces, so total new area = 2 × ΔA = 2 × 4×10⁻⁴ = 8 × 10⁻⁴ m².
3. Work: W = γ × (2ΔA) = 0.040 × 8×10⁻⁴.
4. Evaluate: 0.040 × 8 = 0.32, so W = 0.32 × 10⁻⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ J.

⚡ JEE Trick Say "soap film ⇒ ×2" every time — one flat surface of liquid uses W = γΔA, a film (two faces) uses W = 2γΔA. Trap: the answer without the ×2 would be 1.6×10⁻⁵ J, which is offered precisely to catch you; the word "film" demands the factor of 2.
Answer: (b) 3.2×10⁻⁵ J.

1Describe the properties of a liquid stateLiquid-state propertiesProperties of Liquid State - States of Matter - Chemistry Class 11🟢 Exact—
Concept A liquid is the intermediate state of matter: its intermolecular forces are strong enough to hold the molecules close together (as in a solid) but too weak to lock them into fixed positions (unlike a solid), so the molecules can slide past one another. This single fact — close packing plus free movement — explains every characteristic property of the liquid state.
Key relation Liquid properties follow from strong-but-mobile intermolecular forces: molecules touch (definite volume, near-incompressible) yet wander (fluidity, no fixed shape). Surface tension and viscosity are the direct fingerprints of those attractive forces.
Solution
1. Definite volume, no definite shape: molecules stay close but move freely, so a liquid keeps its volume yet takes the shape of its container.Forces fix the spacing but not the arrangement.
2. Fluidity / flow: layers slide over one another, so liquids flow and can be poured.
3. Very low compressibility: molecules are already nearly in contact, leaving little empty space to squeeze out.
4. Surface tension: unbalanced inward attraction on surface molecules pulls the surface into the smallest area (drops become spherical).
5. Viscosity: internal friction between layers resists flow.
6. Evaporation & vapour pressure: high-energy surface molecules escape, exerting a vapour pressure that rises with temperature; densities are much higher than gases and diffusion is slower.
⚡ JEE Trick Answer such "describe the state" questions along one axis — volume, shape, compressibility, fluidity, surface tension, viscosity, vapour pressure — and tie each to "close packing + free movement." Trap: giving a solid's property (fixed shape) or a gas's (highly compressible) loses marks; the liquid is deliberately in-between.
Key point: a liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape, is nearly incompressible, flows (fluidity), and shows surface tension, viscosity, evaporation and a temperature-dependent vapour pressure — all because its intermolecular forces are strong enough to keep molecules close yet weak enough to let them move.
2Explain the factors affecting vapour pressure of a liquidVapour pressureClass 11 Chemistry Chapter 5 | Vapour Pressure and Boiling Point - States of Matter | CBSE/NCERT🟢 Exact—
Concept Vapour pressure is the pressure of the vapour that stands in equilibrium with its liquid in a closed container. It is set by a competition: molecules escape the surface if they have enough energy, while others return. Anything that helps more molecules escape raises the vapour pressure; anything that holds molecules back lowers it. So the factors are exactly those that change the escaping tendency.
Key relation Vapour pressure ↑ with temperature (more molecules exceed the escape energy) and ↓ with stronger intermolecular forces (higher boiling liquids are less volatile). It does not depend on surface area or the amount of liquid, since equilibrium is dynamic.
Solution
1. Temperature (main factor): raising T gives more molecules the kinetic energy to escape, so vapour pressure rises steeply (roughly exponentially) with temperature.The fraction of molecules above the escape energy grows fast with T.
2. Nature of the liquid / intermolecular forces: weak forces (ether, petrol) ⇒ easy escape ⇒ high vapour pressure (volatile); strong forces (water, glycerol) ⇒ low vapour pressure.
3. Not affected by: surface area or quantity of liquid — at equilibrium the escape and return rates both scale with area, so their balance (the pressure) is unchanged. A dissolved non-volatile solute lowers it (Raoult's law), but for a pure liquid the two factors above govern.
⚡ JEE Trick Only two things move a pure liquid's vapour pressure: temperature (↑ raises it) and strength of intermolecular forces (stronger ⇒ lower). Trap: "larger surface area" or "more liquid" are decoys — they change how fast equilibrium is reached, not the equilibrium pressure itself.
Key point: a liquid's vapour pressure increases with temperature and decreases as its intermolecular forces get stronger (less volatile); it is independent of surface area and the amount of liquid present.
3Define and explain surface tension / short noteSurface tensionSurface Tension - States of Matter - Chemistry Class 11🟢 Exact—
Concept A molecule inside a liquid is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbours, so the forces cancel. A molecule at the surface has neighbours only below and beside it, so it feels a net inward pull. This imbalance makes the surface behave like a stretched elastic skin that tries to shrink to the smallest possible area — the origin of surface tension.
Key relation γ = F ÷ L — surface tension is the force acting per unit length along the surface, units N·m⁻¹ (or dyn·cm⁻¹). Equivalently γ = surface energy per unit area (J·m⁻²). Both views agree because force-per-length and energy-per-area have the same dimensions.
Solution
1. Define: surface tension is the force per unit length acting at right angles on either side of an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface, tending to pull the surface together.
2. Molecular cause: unbalanced inward attraction on surface molecules ⇒ the surface minimises its area.Fewer neighbours above means the resultant force points into the bulk.
3. Consequences: small drops become spherical (least area for a given volume); insects rest on water; capillary rise occurs.
4. Dependence: γ decreases with temperature (falling to zero at the critical temperature) and depends on the nature of the liquid; SI unit N·m⁻¹, cgs dyn·cm⁻¹ (1 N·m⁻¹ = 1000 dyn·cm⁻¹).
⚡ JEE Trick Carry both faces of surface tension: force/length (N·m⁻¹) for wire/film-pull problems and energy/area (J·m⁻²) for work-done problems — they are numerically equal. Trap: surface tension is a surface effect (fewer neighbours at the top), not the bulk internal friction that is viscosity.
Key point: surface tension is the force per unit length (γ = F/L, N·m⁻¹) acting along a liquid surface — caused by the net inward pull on surface molecules — which makes the surface contract to minimum area; it equals the surface energy per unit area and falls with rising temperature.
4Explain the term viscosity / short noteViscosityViscosity - States of Matter - Chemistry Class 11🟢 Exact—
Concept When a liquid flows, imagine it as a stack of thin layers moving at different speeds. Because molecules attract their neighbours, a fast layer drags a slow one forward and the slow layer drags the fast one back — an internal friction between adjacent layers. Viscosity is the measure of this internal resistance to flow; "thick" liquids (honey, glycerol) have high viscosity, "thin" ones (water, ether) have low viscosity.
Key relation Newton's law: F = η·A·(v/x), so the coefficient of viscosity η = F ÷ [A·(v/x)]. Units: poise (dyn·s·cm⁻²) in cgs, N·s·m⁻² (Pa·s) in SI, with 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s. η is the force needed per unit area to keep unit velocity gradient — a direct measure of internal resistance.
Solution
1. Define: viscosity is the internal resistance offered by a liquid to its flow, arising from friction between its layers.
2. Coefficient of viscosity η: the tangential force per unit area required to maintain unit velocity gradient between two layers (from F = η·A·v/x).
3. Temperature effect: for liquids viscosity decreases as temperature rises (molecules gain energy and slip past each other more easily); for gases it increases.Opposite trends because gas viscosity comes from molecular collisions, not attraction.
4. Units: 1 poise = 0.1 N·s·m⁻²; SI unit is Pa·s.
⚡ JEE Trick Tag viscosity as "internal friction between layers" and remember its unit chain: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s. Trap: liquids get less viscous on heating while gases get more viscous — do not assume both behave the same.
Key point: viscosity is the internal resistance to flow caused by friction between a liquid's layers; measured by the coefficient η from F = η·A·(v/x), with units poise (cgs) or Pa·s (SI, 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s), and it decreases with rising temperature for liquids.

1Nature of intermolecular forces in liquids vs gases & solidsIntermolecular forcesIntermolecular Forces | States of Matter | CBSE Class 11 Chemistry🟢 ExactcBetween gases and solids
Concept The three states of matter form a ladder of intermolecular-force strength. In gases the molecules are far apart and the forces are negligible; in solids they are packed and locked by very strong forces. A liquid sits in between — its forces are strong enough to keep molecules touching (definite volume) but weak enough to let them move (fluidity). So the liquid's intermolecular forces are intermediate between those of gases and solids.
Key relation gas < liquid < solid in intermolecular-force strength (and in order/closeness of packing). Force strength tracks how close and how ordered the molecules are; the liquid is the middle rung.
Solution
1. Rank the forces: gases (weakest) → liquids → solids (strongest).
2. Place the liquid: its forces exceed a gas's (molecules stay together) but are less than a solid's (molecules still move).This is why a liquid has fixed volume yet no fixed shape.
3. Therefore the intermolecular forces in a liquid are between those of gases and solids — option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the one-line ladder gas < liquid < solid for force strength, packing, and density — it answers a whole family of "compare the states" MCQs. Trap: options claiming a liquid's forces are "stronger than a solid's" or "same as a gas's" contradict the ladder.
Answer: (c) Between gases and solids.

Concept In a closed container, molecules leave the liquid surface (evaporation) and some vapour molecules return (condensation). 2. The steady pressure of this vapour, in equilibrium with the liquid at that temperature, is the vapour pressure.

Answer: (c) Pressure by vapours in equilibrium (with the liquid at a given temperature).
3What causes cooling during evaporation?Evaporation coolingA-VP🟡 SimilarcLoss of high-energy molecules
Concept Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. Only the fastest, most energetic molecules have enough energy to break free from the surface and evaporate. When they leave, the molecules that stay behind are on average slower, so the average kinetic energy — and hence the temperature — of the remaining liquid drops. That is why evaporation cools the liquid (and the surface it sits on).
Key relation average KE ∝ temperature; removing the high-KE molecules lowers the average KE. Evaporation is selective — it skims off the energetic tail of the speed distribution, cooling the rest.
Solution
1. Molecules have a spread of speeds; only the highest-energy ones escape the surface.They alone can overcome the intermolecular attraction holding them in the liquid.
2. Their departure lowers the average kinetic energy of the molecules left behind.
3. Lower average KE means lower temperature — the liquid cools. This is the loss of high-energy molecules, option (c).
4. Reject distractors invoking absorbed light or chemical change — evaporative cooling is purely the escape of energetic molecules.
⚡ JEE Trick Evaporative cooling = "the fastest molecules leave, so the average slows down." This is why sweating cools you and a wet cloth feels cold. Trap: it is not that the liquid "absorbs cold"; it is the selective escape of high-energy molecules that lowers the average KE.
Answer: (c) Loss of high-energy molecules.
4Boiling point is defined as the temperature at which …Boiling pointClass 11 Chemistry Chapter 5 | Vapour Pressure and Boiling Point - States of Matter | CBSE/NCERT🟢 ExactbVapour pressure equals external pressure
Concept As a liquid is heated its vapour pressure rises. Boiling begins when vapour bubbles can form inside the liquid and grow, and a bubble can only survive if the vapour pressure inside it can push back against the pressure of the atmosphere (and liquid) around it. So boiling occurs exactly when the liquid's vapour pressure becomes equal to the external (atmospheric) pressure — that temperature is the boiling point.
Key relation Boiling point = temperature at which vapour pressure = external (atmospheric) pressure. Only then can vapour bubbles resist the surrounding pressure and rise, i.e. the liquid boils throughout its bulk, not just at the surface.
Solution
1. Heating raises vapour pressure until, at some temperature, it matches the pressure pushing down on the liquid.Below that, any bubble is crushed by the higher external pressure.
2. At that temperature vapour bubbles form in the bulk and escape — the liquid boils.
3. Hence boiling point = temperature where vapour pressure equals external pressure, option (b).
4. Corollary: lower the external pressure (e.g. on a mountain) and the boiling point falls, since the vapour pressure needs to reach only a smaller value.
⚡ JEE Trick Boiling is a "vapour pressure = surrounding pressure" condition — that is why a pressure cooker (higher pressure) raises the boiling point and high altitude (lower pressure) lowers it. Trap: "vapour pressure equals zero" or "liquid turns to gas at the surface" describe evaporation, not boiling.
Answer: (b) Vapour pressure equals external pressure.
