1Which statement correctly describes the number of molecular orbitals formed during LCAO? (a) Always more than the number of atomic orbitals (b) Always less than the number of atomic orbitals (c) Equal to the number of combining atomic orbitals (d) Depends on orbital energiesLCAO — orbital countAtomic orbitals of bonded atoms combine to form molecular orbitals — how many molecular orbitals are formed?🟢 ExactcEqual to the number of combining atomic orbitals
Concept LCAO is a linear-algebra statement dressed up as chemistry. Each atomic orbital is a basis vector; combining n of them produces exactly n independent linear combinations, no more and no fewer. Orbitals are never created or destroyed by mixing — they are only re-labelled. Physically, the total number of places an electron can live must be conserved, so the count going in must equal the count coming out. The energies redistribute (half go down, half go up), but the census does not change.
Key relation number of MOs = number of combining AOs, and they split into equal halves: n/2 bonding + n/2 antibonding. For a homonuclear pair, ψ = ψA + ψB is the bonding combination and ψ* = ψA − ψB the antibonding one — two inputs, two outputs.
Solution
1. Write the two allowed combinations for two 1s orbitals: ψ = ψA + ψB and ψ* = ψA − ψB.Any other combination such as 2ψA + 2ψB is just a multiple of the first — not independent, so it does not count.
2. Count: 2 AOs in ⇒ 2 MOs out, one lowered in energy, one raised.
3. Scale it up: H₂ (2 AOs → 2 MOs), and for the second period 2s + 2px + 2py + 2pz on each of two atoms = 8 AOs → 8 MOs (4 bonding, 4 antibonding). The rule never breaks.
4. Eliminate the distractors: (a) and (b) would mean orbitals appear from nowhere or vanish, violating the completeness of the basis; (d) confuses how many MOs form with how much they split — energy matching controls the size of the splitting, not the number.

2What happens when two atomic orbitals try to overlap but their phases do not match? (a) A bonding molecular orbital is formed (b) An antibonding molecular orbital is formed (c) They do not combine because the overlap is not proper (d) A π-bond is formedOut-of-phase overlapBonding and antibonding molecular orbitals from in-phase and out-of-phase overlap🟡 SimilarbAn antibonding molecular orbital is formed
Concept Electron waves interfere exactly like any other waves. When two atomic orbitals meet with the same sign of ψ (in phase), the amplitudes add — constructive interference — and electron density piles up between the nuclei: that is the bonding MO. When they meet with opposite signs (out of phase), the amplitudes subtract — destructive interference — density is scooped out from between the nuclei and a nodal plane appears: that is the antibonding MO. The crucial point is that a phase mismatch does not abolish the interaction; it simply changes which of the two MOs you are describing.
Key relation In phase: ψ = ψA + ψB ⇒ ψ² = ψA² + ψB² + 2ψAψB density raised by +2ψAψB in the internuclear region ⇒ bonding. Out of phase: ψ* = ψA − ψB ⇒ ψ*² = ψA² + ψB² − 2ψAψB density lowered by the same 2ψAψB ⇒ antibonding, with ψ* = 0 at the midpoint.
Solution
1. Read the phrase "phases do not match" as ψA and ψB carrying opposite signs where they meet.
2. Subtracting the wave functions gives ψ* = ψA − ψB, whose square is smaller than ψA² + ψB² by 2ψAψB.Less electron glue between the nuclei ⇒ the nuclei repel ⇒ the orbital lies above the parent AOs in energy.
3. At the midpoint ψA = ψB by symmetry, so ψ* = 0 — a nodal plane, the fingerprint of an antibonding MO.
4. Kill the wrong options: (a) needs matched phases; (c) is the classic trap — the orbitals do combine, and both MOs form simultaneously from the same pair of AOs; (d) confuses phase (which decides bonding vs antibonding) with geometry of overlap (which decides σ vs π).
 phase relation      combination        density between nuclei     MO type
 same sign (+ , +)   ψA + ψB            ψA²+ψB² + 2ψAψB  (more)    BONDING
 opposite (+ , -)    ψA - ψB            ψA²+ψB² - 2ψAψB  (less)    ANTIBONDING   <-- answer (b)
                                        ψ* = 0 at midpoint -> nodal plane
 NOTE : both MOs are always produced together; nothing is "refused"
⚡ JEE Trick Keep two switches separate and you will never miss a bonding/antibonding question: SIGN decides bonding vs antibonding, SHAPE of overlap decides σ vs π vs δ. Same sign + head-on = σ; opposite sign + head-on = σ*; same sign + sideways = π; opposite sign + sideways = π*. Trap: option (c) — "no combination" only happens for zero net overlap (s with px perpendicular), which is a symmetry failure, not a phase mismatch.
Answer: (b) An antibonding molecular orbital is formed.
3Which of the following overlaps can produce a σ molecular orbital? (a) p–p sidewise overlap (b) s–p sideways overlap (c) p–p head-on overlap (d) p–d sideways overlapσ overlap geometryOrbital overlap: how head-on overlap gives σ and sideways gives π🟡 Similarcp–p head-on overlap
Concept A σ molecular orbital is defined by symmetry, not by which orbitals made it: the electron cloud must be cylindrically symmetric about the internuclear axis, so that spinning the molecule about that axis leaves the orbital unchanged. That happens only when the two lobes point straight at each other along the axis — head-on (axial) overlap. Sideways (lateral) overlap puts the density above and below the axis with a nodal plane containing the axis, which is the definition of π. Because head-on overlap concentrates both lobes into the same small region, σ overlap is also the largest, which is why σ bonds are the strongest.
Key relation σ ⇔ axial (head-on) overlap ⇔ symmetric about the internuclear axis ⇔ 0 nodal planes containing the axis; π ⇔ lateral (sideways) overlap ⇔ 1 nodal plane containing the axis. Take z as the internuclear axis: 2pz–2pz is head-on (σ), while 2px–2px and 2py–2py are sideways (π).
Solution
1. Set the internuclear axis as z and test each option against the cylindrical-symmetry rule.
2. (a) p–p sidewise — this is px with px: density lies above and below the axis, with the xy-plane... rather the nodal plane contains the axis ⇒ π, not σ.
3. (b) s–p sideways — an s orbital is spherical and a perpendicular p orbital has one + lobe and one − lobe; the two halves of the overlap cancel exactly ⇒ net overlap zero ⇒ non-bonding, no MO at all.
4. (c) p–p head-on — pz with pz along z: the lobes meet nose to nose, the result is symmetric about z ⇒ σ. ✔
5. (d) p–d sideways — py with dxy gives a π overlap (this is exactly the p–d figure in the theory), not σ.

Answer: (c) p–p head-on overlap.
4A molecular orbital shows a node between the two nuclei. Which statement about this orbital is correct? (a) It is lower in energy than the atomic orbitals (b) It results from constructive interference (c) It corresponds to an antibonding orbital (d) It increases electron density between nucleiNode ⇒ antibondingNodal plane in bonding vs antibonding molecular orbitals (MOT)🟢 ExactcIt corresponds to an antibonding orbital
Concept A node between the nuclei is the single most reliable diagnostic in MO theory. It can only arise from ψA − ψB, because a sum of two positive-going waves can never be zero in the middle. So node ⇒ subtraction ⇒ destructive interference ⇒ density removed from the bonding region ⇒ nuclear repulsion is unshielded ⇒ the orbital sits above the parent AOs. Every one of the other three options is a property of the bonding partner, so recognising the node settles all four at once.
Key relation ψ* = ψA − ψB; at the midpoint ψA = ψB ⇒ ψ* = 0 and ψ*² = 0. Zero probability density on a whole plane means the electron simply never appears between the nuclei — it cannot glue them together, so it pushes them apart. Energy: E(ψ*) > E(AO) > E(ψ).
Solution
1. Identify what makes a node: only the difference ψA − ψB vanishes at the midpoint. Therefore the orbital came from destructive interference.
2. Destructive interference removes the density 2ψAψB from between the nuclei, so the internuclear region is depleted, not enriched — this kills option (d).
3. With no shielding charge in between, the two positive nuclei repel more strongly ⇒ the orbital's energy is raised above the atomic orbitals — this kills option (a).
4. "Constructive interference" is the opposite process and would give no node — this kills option (b).
5. The orbital that has a node, is depleted between the nuclei and lies higher in energy is by definition the antibonding MO ⇒ option (c).

 node between nuclei         NO                     YES                <-- given
 interference                constructive           destructive
 density between nuclei      increased (+2ψAψB)     decreased (-2ψAψB)

 ⇒ the described orbital is ANTIBONDING                                 <-- answer (c)
⚡ JEE Trick Learn the four-property chain as one unit — node ⇔ subtraction ⇔ less density ⇔ higher energy — and any one clue in a question hands you the other three for free. Trap: a "node" that contains the internuclear axis (as in π 2px) does not mean antibonding; only a node lying across the axis, between the nuclei, does.
Answer: (c) It corresponds to an antibonding orbital.
5[MULTIPLE CORRECT] For two atomic orbitals to combine and form a σ molecular orbital, which of the following conditions must be satisfied according to Molecular Orbital Theory? (a) The two orbitals must have the same energy and compatible symmetry about the internuclear axis (b) The orbitals must have opposite phases so that destructive overlap is maximized (c) The overlap integral between the two orbitals must be non-zero (d) The orbitals must belong to different symmetry species so that mixing is allowedConditions for LCAOLCAO–MO: overlap integral and the symmetry/energy conditions for mixing🟡 Similara, csame (comparable) energy with compatible symmetry, and a non-zero overlap integral
Concept Two atomic orbitals mix appreciably only if three conditions hold together: comparable energy, matching symmetry about the internuclear axis, and appreciable spatial overlap. The first controls how much they split (perturbation theory says the mixing goes as β²/ΔE, so a large energy gap kills it), while the second and third are really one idea seen from two sides — if the symmetries clash, the positive and negative parts of the overlap integral cancel exactly and S = 0, so nothing happens no matter how close the orbitals are in space.
Key relation Overlap integral S = ∫ ψA ψB dτ ≠ 0 is the make-or-break test. S > 0 for in-phase axial overlap (σ), S < 0 for out-of-phase, and S = 0 whenever the two orbitals have different symmetry about the axis — e.g. ∫ ψ(s) ψ(px) dτ = 0 because the + half of px cancels the − half. Splitting scale: ΔEmix ∝ β² / (EA − EB).
Solution
1. Test (a): comparable (ideally identical) energies plus compatible symmetry about the internuclear axis — these are precisely the textbook conditions ⇒ correct.1s of H (−13.6 eV) will not mix usefully with 1s of F (about −700 eV); the gap is far too large.
2. Test (c): a non-zero overlap integral S is the mathematical statement of "the orbitals actually see each other" ⇒ correct. If S = 0 the secular determinant gives no splitting at all.
3. Reject (b): maximising destructive overlap describes the σ* orbital, not the condition for forming a σ bonding MO. Both σ and σ* appear together anyway; the requirement is large overlap, in phase.
4. Reject (d): this is backwards. Orbitals must belong to the same symmetry species to mix — different symmetry species force S = 0 and forbid mixing entirely.

1Explain the formation of (1) Bonding molecular orbital (BMO) and (2) Antibonding molecular orbital (AMO).Formation of BMO & AMOFormation of bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals — full explanation🟢 Exact—
Concept Molecular Orbital Theory (Hund and Mulliken, 1932) treats an electron in a molecule as belonging to the whole molecule, not to one bond. The molecular orbitals are built by the LCAO recipe: take the atomic orbital wave functions ψA and ψB of the two approaching atoms and combine them linearly, ψMO = ψA ± ψB. Because a wave function has a sign as well as a magnitude, there are exactly two ways to do this — add or subtract — and they correspond to the two classic wave phenomena, constructive and destructive interference. The whole of this answer is the physics of those two interferences.
Key relations Bonding: ψ = ψA + ψB ⇒ ψ² = ψA² + ψB² + 2ψAψB. Antibonding: ψ* = ψA − ψB ⇒ ψ*² = ψA² + ψB² − 2ψAψB. The whole difference between a bond and an anti-bond is the sign of the single cross term 2ψAψB — it is added in one case and removed in the other, so the pair is stabilised by exactly the amount the other is destabilised (to first order).
Solution
1. Set-up. Two atoms A and B approach; their valence electron waves ψA and ψB begin to overlap. By LCAO, ψMO = ψA ± ψB, giving two molecular orbitals from two atomic orbitals.
2. (1) Bonding molecular orbital — constructive interference. The two waves arrive in phase (same sign of ψ), so the amplitudes add up.ψ² = ψA² + ψB² + 2ψAψB exceeds the simple sum of the separate atomic densities by 2ψAψB, so the probability of finding the electron in the BMO is greater than in either atomic orbital.
3. Consequences for the BMO: charge builds up between the two nuclei; both nuclei are attracted to this charge cloud, which pulls them together; the energy is lower than that of the isolated atomic orbitals — the orbital is stabilised. It has no node between the nuclei and is written σ or π.
4. (2) Antibonding molecular orbital — destructive interference. The two waves arrive out of phase (opposite signs), so the amplitudes subtract.ψ*² = ψA² + ψB² − 2ψAψB falls short of the separate atomic densities by 2ψAψB, so the electron is less likely to be found between the nuclei than in either atomic orbital.
5. Consequences for the AMO: a nodal plane appears between the nuclei where ψ* = 0; the depleted region leaves the two positive nuclei unscreened, so they repel; the energy is higher than that of the isolated atomic orbitals — the orbital is destabilised. It is written σ* or π* (star = antibonding).
6. Energy picture. On an energy-level diagram the two parent AOs sit at the middle, the BMO drops below them and the AMO rises above them by (to a first approximation) the same amount; the molecule is stable only if more electrons occupy bonding than antibonding orbitals.

                        /  \
   AO 1s ------ o      /    \      o ------ AO 1s
                        \    /
                       σ 1s   (BMO)  ---- lower energy, no node, nuclei attract

 BMO :  ψ  = ψA + ψB     ψ²  = ψA²+ψB² + 2ψAψB   density INCREASED  -> stabilised
 AMO :  ψ* = ψA - ψB     ψ*² = ψA²+ψB² - 2ψAψB   density DECREASED  -> destabilised

⚡ JEE Trick Board examiners want the algebra plus the four consequences. Write ψ = ψA ± ψB, square it, and point at the ±2ψAψB term — then list density, node, nuclear attraction/repulsion, energy for each orbital. Trap: writing only "bonding MO has lower energy" without the interference argument loses most of the marks; and note the subtlety that in exact treatments the AMO is destabilised slightly more than the BMO is stabilised (the book's own Quick Recap keys this statement as True).
Key point: LCAO gives ψ = ψA + ψB (constructive interference ⇒ extra density +2ψAψB between the nuclei, no node, nuclei attracted, energy lowered ⇒ bonding MO) and ψ* = ψA − ψB (destructive interference ⇒ density reduced by 2ψAψB, nodal plane between nuclei, nuclei repelled, energy raised ⇒ antibonding MO).

1Molecular orbitals are formed by: (a) Sharing of electrons (b) Transfer of electrons (c) Linear combination of atomic orbitals (d) Magnetic interactionOrigin of MOsLinear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) — the basis of MO theory🟡 SimilarcLinear combination of atomic orbitals
Concept MO theory replaces the "shared pair" picture of Lewis and valence bond theory with a wave-mechanical one. Since electrons are waves, the correct way to build a molecular wave function is to superpose the atomic wave functions of the atoms involved — that superposition is what "linear combination" means. Sharing and transfer of electrons describe covalent and ionic bonding at the Lewis level; they are outcomes, not mechanisms of orbital formation.
Key relation ψMO = cAψA ± cBψB, usually written simply as ψMO = ψA ± ψB for a homonuclear pair. "Linear" means the atomic functions appear to the first power only, multiplied by constants and added — never multiplied together or squared.
Solution
1. Recall the second salient feature of MOT from the theory: "Molecular orbitals are formed by linear combination of atomic orbitals of proper symmetry and comparable energy."
2. The method is named after the process itself — LCAO = Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals ⇒ option (c).
3. Reject (a): sharing of electrons is Lewis/VBT language for a covalent bond; it says nothing about how orbitals are constructed.
4. Reject (b): transfer of electrons produces ions and describes ionic bonding, an entirely different model.
5. Reject (d): magnetic interaction is a consequence MO theory can explain (the paramagnetism of O₂), never the cause of MO formation.

2In bonding molecular orbitals, electron density is: (a) Less between nuclei (b) Same as atomic orbitals (c) Greater between nuclei (d) Absent between nucleiBMO electron densityDifference between bonding MO and antibonding MO🟡 SimilarcGreater between nuclei
Concept A bonding molecular orbital exists precisely because electron density accumulates in the internuclear region. That accumulated negative charge sits between two positive nuclei and attracts both of them simultaneously — this electrostatic "glue" is the bond. Quantitatively the extra density is the cross term 2ψAψB produced when the two atomic waves add in phase. Without that extra density there is no lowering of energy and therefore no bond.
Key relation ψ² = (ψA + ψB)² = ψA² + ψB² + 2ψAψB ψA² + ψB² is what you would get if the atoms simply sat side by side without interacting; the term +2ψAψB is the genuine bonding surplus, and it is largest exactly midway between the nuclei where both ψA and ψB are appreciable.
Solution
1. Form the bonding combination ψ = ψA + ψB (the two waves in phase).
2. Square it: ψ² = ψA² + ψB² + 2ψAψB. Since both ψA and ψB are positive there, the cross term is positive ⇒ density is increased.
3. Locate the increase: midway between the nuclei both atomic functions still have significant amplitude, so 2ψAψB peaks there ⇒ density is greatest exactly between the nuclei ⇒ option (c).
4. Eliminate: (a) "less between nuclei" is the antibonding MO; (b) "same as atomic orbitals" would mean no interaction and hence no bond; (d) "absent between nuclei" describes the nodal plane of an antibonding orbital, an even stronger version of (a).

⚡ JEE Trick Anchor everything to the sign of the cross term: +2ψAψB ⇒ bonding, −2ψAψB ⇒ antibonding. Trap: option (b) tempts students who reason "electrons were already there"; MO theory's entire point is that the redistribution, not the count, creates the bond.

Concept The antibonding orbital is the exact mirror image of the bonding one. Subtracting the two atomic waves removes the same cross term that addition supplied, so the internuclear region is drained of electron density rather than enriched. In the homonuclear case the drainage is total on one plane — the density falls all the way to zero at the midpoint, producing the nodal plane. An electron placed there does no screening, so the bare nuclear repulsion dominates and the molecule is destabilised.

1. Write the antibonding combination ψ* = ψA − ψB (waves out of phase).
2. Square: ψ*² = ψA² + ψB² − 2ψAψB. The negative cross term means the density is reduced wherever both orbitals overlap — i.e. between the nuclei ⇒ option (c).

4Molecular orbitals follow which rules? (a) Aufbau principle (b) Hund's rule (c) Pauli exclusion principle (d) All of theseFilling rules for MOsAufbau principle, Hund's rule and Pauli's exclusion principle🟡 SimilardAll of these
Concept The sixth salient feature of MOT states it plainly: molecular orbitals are filled in exactly the same way as atomic orbitals. That is not a coincidence — the three filling rules are consequences of general quantum mechanics (energy minimisation, antisymmetry of the electron wave function, and exchange stabilisation), and none of them refers to whether the orbital is centred on one nucleus or several. So all three carry over unchanged from atoms to molecules.
Key relations Aufbau: fill the lowest-energy MO first — σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < (π2px = π2py) < σ2pz < … for B₂, C₂, N₂. Pauli: max 2 e⁻ per MO, with opposite spins. Hund: degenerate MOs are singly filled first, with parallel spins. Hund's rule applied to the degenerate π*2p pair is what gives O₂ its two unpaired electrons and hence its paramagnetism — the single greatest triumph of MO theory.
Solution
1. Aufbau — electrons enter the lowest available MO first. Demonstrated by H₂: both electrons go into σ1s, not σ*1s.
2. Pauli — no MO holds more than two electrons, and those two must be spin-paired. Demonstrated by He₂: σ1s takes 2, so the next 2 are forced into σ*1s.
3. Hund — for the degenerate π2px and π2py (or π*2px, π*2py), electrons occupy them singly with parallel spins before pairing.O₂ has 2 electrons for the degenerate π* pair; Hund puts one in each with parallel spins ⇒ two unpaired electrons ⇒ paramagnetic, as observed.
4. All three are used, and none of (a), (b), (c) alone is sufficient ⇒ the answer must be (d) All of these.

Answer: (d) All of these — Aufbau, Hund and Pauli all apply to molecular orbitals.

Crucially, g/u is not the same as bonding/antibonding — for σ orbitals bonding happens to be g, but for π orbitals the labelling is reversed, with the bonding π being u and the antibonding π* being g. That reversal is exactly what this question probes.
Key relation σ bonding = g, σ* = u but π bonding = u, π* = g. Key relation nodal planes = (σ:0, π:1, δ:2) + (1 if antibonding). Total 2 ⇒ π* (1 + 1) or δ bonding (2 + 0). The Quick Recap of this lecture confirms it: "π*(2px) has two nodal planes whereas π(2px) has only one nodal plane" — keyed as True.
Solution
1. (a) σ2pz: σ ⇒ 0, no star ⇒ 0 ⇒ 0 nodal planes. Excluded.
2. (b) π*2px: π ⇒ 1 (the plane containing the axis, i.e. the yz-plane), star ⇒ 1 (the plane perpendicular to the axis, midway between the nuclei) ⇒ 2. ✔
3. (c) π*2py: identical reasoning, with the π nodal plane now the xz-plane ⇒ 2. ✔Degenerate partners must have the same node count — a good self-check: if you had assigned different counts to (b) and (c) you would know an error had crept in.
4. (d) σ*2pz: σ ⇒ 0, star ⇒ 1 ⇒ 1 nodal plane. Excluded.
5. Answer: b, c.
 MO           π/σ term   star term   TOTAL   which planes
 σ  2pz          0          0          0     --
 π* 2px          1         +1          2     yz-plane (contains axis) + plane ⟂ axis   ✓
 π* 2py          1         +1          2     xz-plane (contains axis) + plane ⟂ axis   ✓
 σ* 2pz          0         +1          1     plane ⟂ axis only
                                             <-- answer (b, c)
 full ladder :  σ = 0 · π = 1 · σ* = 1 · π* = 2 · δ = 2 · δ* = 3

Trap: degenerate pairs such as π*2px and π*2py always appear together in these multiple-correct items; if you select one you must select the other, so an answer containing exactly one of them is automatically wrong.
Answer: (b) and (c) — π*2px and π*2py.
