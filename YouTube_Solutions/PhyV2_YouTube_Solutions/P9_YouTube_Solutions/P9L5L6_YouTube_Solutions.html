1A body of mass m1 is moving with a velocity V. It collides with another stationary body of mass m2. They get embedded. At the point of collision, the velocity of the system (a) Increases (b) Decreases but does not become zero (c) Remains same (d) Become zero — (Level-1)Perfectly inelastic collision, momentumA body of mass m1 is moving with a velocity V. It collides with another stationary body of mass ...🟢 ExactbDecreases but does not become zero
Concept "Embedded" is the keyword — the two bodies move off together as one lump, which is the definition of a perfectly inelastic collision. Only linear momentum survives such a collision; kinetic energy does not. So the only honest way to find the common velocity is to conserve momentum, not to guess from intuition about "losing energy therefore losing all speed".
Formula m₁V = (m₁+m₂)v ⇒ v = m₁V/(m₁+m₂) — masses in kg, V, v in m/s. kg·(m/s) on both sides ⇒ dimensionally consistent; v is just V scaled down by the pure fraction m₁/(m₁+m₂).
Solution
1. Before collision: body 1 carries all the momentum, p = m₁V; body 2 is at rest, contributing zero.
2. Since no external horizontal force acts during the (instantaneous) collision, total momentum is conserved: m₁V + 0 = (m₁+m₂)v.
3. Solve: v = m₁V/(m₁+m₂).Because m₂ > 0, the denominator is strictly larger than m₁, so v is strictly less than V — the system must slow down.
4. Check that v cannot be zero: the fraction m₁/(m₁+m₂) is a ratio of two positive numbers, so it is always strictly between 0 and 1 — never exactly 0 (that would need m₁ = 0, i.e. no first body at all).
5. So the system's velocity is somewhere strictly between 0 and V — decreased, but not zero. Option (d) would only be true for a perfectly inelastic head-on equal-and-opposite momentum collision, not this one-body-moving case.
  BEFORE:      m1 ──▸ V         m2 (at rest)
               ●───▸              ○

  AFTER (embedded, move as ONE body):
               (m1+m2) ──▸ v = m1V/(m1+m2)
                  ●●───▸           0 < v < V
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever one body is initially at rest and they stick together, the common velocity is always V multiplied by a proper fraction m₁/(m₁+m₂) — so it is guaranteed non-zero unless V itself is zero. Trap: students who just did an inelastic-collision energy-loss problem often default to "energy is lost ⇒ everything drops to zero" — energy loss and velocity loss are not the same statement.
Answer: (b) Decreases but does not become zero — v = m₁V/(m₁+m₂).
2A block of mass 0.50 kg is moving with a speed of 2.00 ms⁻¹ on a smooth surface. It strikes another mass of 1.00 kg and then they move together as a single body. The energy loss during the collision is (a) 1.00 J (b) 0.67 J (c) 0.34 J (d) 0.16 J — (Level-2)Inelastic collision, KE lossHow to Calculate Energy Lost in a Collision (Step-by-Step)🟡 Similarb0.67 J
Concept This is the twin of the previous question, now with real numbers: find the common velocity from momentum conservation first, then take the straightforward difference KEinitial − KEfinal. "Smooth surface" is there only to say no energy leaks to friction with the ground — all the loss happens strictly inside the collision.
Formula m₁u₁ = (m₁+m₂)v; ΔKE = ½m₁u₁² − ½(m₁+m₂)v² — masses in kg, speeds in m/s, energy in J. kg·(m/s)² = J on every term, so subtracting two KE's legitimately gives an energy in joules.
Solution
1. Data: m₁ = 0.50 kg, u₁ = 2.00 m/s, m₂ = 1.00 kg (at rest).
2. Momentum conservation: (0.50)(2.00) = (0.50+1.00)v ⇒ 1.00 = 1.50v ⇒ v = 2/3 m/s ≈ 0.667 m/s.
3. Initial KE: ½(0.50)(2.00)² = ½(0.50)(4) = 1.00 J.
4. Final KE: ½(1.50)(2/3)² = ½(1.50)(4/9) = (0.75)(4/9) = 3/9 = 0.333 J.Combined mass is bigger but the speed drops by a factor of 3, and speed enters squared — so KE falls a lot faster than momentum did.
5. Energy loss: ΔKE = 1.00 − 0.333 = 0.667 J ≈ 0.67 J.
6. Sanity check with the shortcut formula for one-body-moving inelastic collisions: ΔKE = ½·[m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂)]·u₁² = ½·[(0.5×1)/1.5]·4 = ½·(1/3)·4 = 2/3 J ✓ matches.
  BEFORE: 0.5kg ──▸2.00m/s    1.0kg (rest)      KE_i = 1.00 J

  AFTER (stuck together, 1.5 kg):
          1.5kg ──▸ v = 2/3 m/s               KE_f = 0.33 J

          ΔKE = 1.00 − 0.33 = 0.67 J  (lost as heat/sound/deformation)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the reduced-mass shortcut ΔKE = ½ μ u₁² with μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) for "one body moving, hits a stationary body, they stick" — it skips computing v entirely and goes straight to the answer. Trap: option (a) 1.00 J is the initial KE with the final KE forgotten (as if all of it were lost); option (d) 0.16 J comes from using u₁² only once instead of squaring v correctly.
Answer: (b) 0.67 J (common velocity 2/3 m/s).
3Prove that in an elastic oblique collision between 2 bodies of the same mass, with one mass initially at rest, the final velocities are perpendicular to each other. — (Level-3)Elastic oblique collision, equal mass, proofOblique Elastic Collision between Two Identical Bodies will move Perpendicularly🟢 Exactθ₁ + θ₂ = 90°
Concept This is the master result behind every "billiard-ball" question in this lecture — it is why questions 4, and Level-4 Q10, and Level-6 all lean on the same 90° fact. The proof uses both conservation laws an elastic collision gives you: momentum (a vector equation, split into x and y) and kinetic energy (a scalar equation) — and combines them algebraically rather than solving for the angles directly.
Formula Momentum (x): mu = mv₁cosθ₁ + mv₂cosθ₂. Momentum (y): 0 = mv₁sinθ₁ − mv₂sinθ₂. Energy: ½mu² = ½mv₁² + ½mv₂². All masses cancel since both bodies have mass m. Every term is (mass)(speed)² or (mass)(speed) — dimensionally momentum vs energy, but since m cancels identically on both sides of each equation, only the speeds and angles matter.
Solution
1. Cancel m throughout: u = v₁cosθ₁ + v₂cosθ₂  …(i); 0 = v₁sinθ₁ − v₂sinθ₂  …(ii); u² = v₁² + v₂²  …(iii, energy).
2. Square (i): u² = v₁²cos²θ₁ + v₂²cos²θ₂ + 2v₁v₂cosθ₁cosθ₂.
3. Square (ii): 0 = v₁²sin²θ₁ + v₂²sin²θ₂ − 2v₁v₂sinθ₁sinθ₂.
4. Add steps 2 and 3: u² = v₁²(cos²θ₁+sin²θ₁) + v₂²(cos²θ₂+sin²θ₂) + 2v₁v₂(cosθ₁cosθ₂ − sinθ₁sinθ₂).Each bracket collapses to 1 by the Pythagorean identity, and the cross-term is exactly cos(θ₁+θ₂) by the cosine addition formula.
5. So u² = v₁² + v₂² + 2v₁v₂cos(θ₁+θ₂). Compare with (iii): u² = v₁² + v₂² already, forcing 2v₁v₂cos(θ₁+θ₂) = 0.
6. Since the collision is genuinely oblique (both bodies move off, v₁ ≠ 0 and v₂ ≠ 0), the only way the product is zero is cos(θ₁+θ₂) = 0, i.e. θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°. QED.

                 ╲ ╱
   m ──▸u ────────●──────────  (target m, at rest)
                 ╱ ╲ θ2
              v2╱   ╲

   momentum(x,y) + KE conservation  ⇒  2 v1 v2 cos(θ1+θ2) = 0
                                     ⇒  θ1 + θ2 = 90°   (v1,v2 ≠ 0)
⚡ JEE Trick The 90° rule needs THREE conditions simultaneously — elastic, equal masses, and target initially at rest — change any one and the right angle disappears. Trap: applying this rule to inelastic or unequal-mass collisions is a very common exam error; always check all three boxes before invoking it. Once proven, any "find θ₁ given θ₂" question in this chapter becomes a one-line subtraction from 90°.
Answer: θ₁ + θ₂ = 90° — the two bodies always separate at right angles.

Concept This is the direct application of the previous question's proof — no new derivation needed. Elastic + equal masses + target initially at rest is the exact NCERT "billiard ball" example, and it is a gift question once you recognise the 90° rule; solving it from scratch with momentum/energy equations would be needlessly slow.
Formula θ₁ + θ₂ = 90° for an elastic collision between equal masses with one initially at rest (proved in Suggested Problem 3). Both θ's are pure angles (degrees), so the relation is dimensionally trivial — it's a geometric consequence of momentum + energy conservation, not a new physical law.
Solution

2. State the theorem: θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°, where θ₁ is the cue ball's deflection and θ₂ is the target ball's launch angle (both measured from the cue ball's original direction).
3. The player wants the target ball to head into the pocket at θ₂ = 37° from the original line.
4. Substitute: θ₁ + 37° = 90°.

6. 5Uniform discs of the same material undergo collision on a smooth horizontal table as shown: a disc of radius 1 cm moving at 5 cm/s (offset 3 cm from the centre-line) strikes a disc of radius 4 cm at rest, with the geometry giving a 3-4-5 line-of-centres triangle. Determine the velocities after collision if e = 0.5. — (Level-5)Oblique disc collision, restitution, normal/tangential split5.3 Oblique Impact of Smooth Spheres (FM1 — Elastic collisions in 2 dimensions)🟡 Similarv₁≈3.42 cm/s (≈82° off original line) · v₂≈0.35 cm/s (37° off original line)
Concept This is an off-centre (glancing) collision: the line joining the two centres at the instant of contact is not along the incoming velocity. Split every velocity into a component along the line of centres (the "normal", where restitution and momentum rules apply exactly as in 1-D) and a component perpendicular to it (the "tangential", which is untouched because the discs are smooth — no friction, no tangential impulse).
Formula Geometry: radii 1 cm + 4 cm = 5 cm apart at contact, offset 3 cm ⇒ a 3-4-5 triangle, so cosθ=4/5, sinθ=3/5 between the line of centres and the initial velocity. Mass ∝ area ∝ r² (same material, so thickness cancels) ⇒ m₂ = 16m₁. Normal: m₁u_n = m₁v₁ₙ + m₂v₂ₙ, e = (v₂ₙ−v₁ₙ)/u_n. Tangential: v₁ₜ = u_t, v₂ₜ = 0. cm/s stays cm/s throughout since e and the mass ratio are both dimensionless.
Solution
1. Resolve u = 5 cm/s: u_n = 5×(4/5) = 4 cm/s, u_t = 5×(3/5) = 3 cm/s.
2. Momentum along the normal (m₂=16m₁, m₁ cancels): 4 = v₁ₙ + 16v₂ₙ  …(i)
3. Restitution along the normal: v₂ₙ − v₁ₙ = e·u_n = 0.5×4 = 2  …(ii)
4. Solve (i)+(ii): from (ii), v₁ₙ = v₂ₙ−2; substitute in (i): 4 = (v₂ₙ−2)+16v₂ₙ = 17v₂ₙ−2 ⇒ v₂ₙ = 6/17 ≈ 0.35 cm/s, so v₁ₙ = 6/17−2 = −28/17 ≈ −1.65 cm/s (small disc rebounds backward along the normal).
5. Tangential components carry over unchanged: v₁ₜ = 3 cm/s (small disc), v₂ₜ = 0 (big disc never had one).No tangential force exists between smooth discs, so whatever sideways motion the small disc had, it keeps — and the big disc, hit dead-on along the normal only, never picks up any.
6. Small disc's resultant speed: v₁ = √(v₁ₙ²+v₁ₜ²) = √((28/17)²+3²) = √(3385)/17 ≈ 3.42 cm/s, directed about 82° from the original line (mostly deflected sideways since it rebounds along the normal but keeps its full tangential push).
7. Big disc's resultant speed: v₂ = v₂ₙ = 0.35 cm/s, directed exactly along the line of centres, i.e. 37° from the original line (θ = 37° being the very angle used in Suggested Problem 4).
   before:  1cm ●──▸5cm/s   (offset 3cm)      4cm ⊙ (rest)
            line of centres at θ=37° (cos=4/5, sin=3/5)

   normal (along centres):  u_n=4 → v1n=−28/17, v2n=6/17   (e=0.5)

⚡ JEE Trick The instant you see "strikes off-centre / glancing / offset", stop thinking 1-D — decompose immediately into normal (restitution + momentum) and tangential (untouched) axes; a 3-4-5 (or 5-12-13) triangle in the geometry is the exam-setter's way of keeping cosθ, sinθ clean. Trap: applying e to the full speed instead of only u_n is the single most common error in this problem family.
Answer: small disc ≈ 3.42 cm/s (≈82° from original path); big disc ≈ 0.35 cm/s (37° from original path, along the line of centres).

Formula p_x = mv (east), p_y = mv (north). Resultant momentum P = √(p_x²+p_y²), final speed V = P/(2m). kg·(m/s) combines in quadrature exactly like any 2-D vector magnitude; dividing by the new total mass 2m converts momentum back to a velocity in m/s.

3. Direction of P: tanφ = p_y/p_x = mv/mv = 1 ⇒ φ = 45° from east — exactly "north-east", confirming the direction stated in the question is consistent with momentum conservation (a useful self-check).

5. Numerically, v/√2 ≈ 0.707v — less than either original speed v, which makes sense: the two initial velocities partly cancel in "spreading effort" across two perpendicular directions, only √2/2 of the individual speed survives in the combined mass.

4A ball of mass M falls from a height h on a floor for which the coefficient of restitution is e. Concept Each bounce off the fixed, immovable floor is its own mini restitution problem: e is defined between the ball's speed just after and just before that particular impact. Since speed-before-impact and rebound-height are related by simple free-fall kinematics, each bounce multiplies the height by e², and successive bounces multiply — they don't add.
Formula Speed just before impact from height H: v = √(2gH). Restitution off a fixed floor: e = v'/v (rebound speed / impact speed). Rebound height: H' = v'²/2g = e²H. √(m/s²·m) = m/s ✓ for v; squaring e (dimensionless) and multiplying by H (m) keeps H' in metres.
Solution
1. Drop height h. Speed just before the 1st impact: v₀ = √(2gh).
2. Rebound speed after 1st impact: v₁ = e v₀. Height reached: h₁ = v₁²/2g = e²(2gh)/2g = e²h.This is the answer to option (a) — the height after ONE rebound, a decoy for anyone who stops early.
3. The ball now falls back down from h₁ and hits the floor a 2nd time. Speed just before 2nd impact: v₁' = √(2g h₁) (same free-fall relation, now with h₁ as the drop height).
4. Rebound speed after 2nd impact: v₂ = e v₁' = e√(2g h₁). Height reached: h₂ = v₂²/2g = e²h₁.
5. Substitute h₁ = e²h from step 2: h₂ = e²(e²h) = e⁴h.Each bounce contributes its own factor of e², so n bounces give a height of e^(2n) h — a clean geometric pattern worth memorising.
6. Since 0 < e < 1, e⁴ < e² < 1, confirming each successive bounce reaches a lower height than the last — physically sensible energy loss on every impact.

Formula Always true (any collision, no external force): Σp_before = Σp_after. True only if elastic: ΣKE_before = ΣKE_after. Solution

2. Sum of internal forces = 0 at every instant ⇒ rate of change of total momentum = 0 ⇒ total momentum is conserved, regardless of whether energy is lost to heat, sound or deformation.
3. By definition, a collision is called "elastic" precisely when kinetic energy also happens to be conserved, and "inelastic" precisely when it is not — some KE converts irreversibly into other forms (heat, sound, permanent deformation).

5. What remains true regardless: momentum. So among the options, only (b) is correct — not (a) alone, not (c) "both", and not (d) "neither" (momentum still holds).

6A perfectly elastic oblique collision takes place between a moving particle and a stationary particle of equal masses. Show that after the collision, the two particles move at right angles to each other.Elastic collision, equal mass, proof (vector method)Oblique Elastic Collision between Two Identical Bodies will move Perpendicularly🟡 Similarv₁·v₂ = 0 ⇒ the two velocities are perpendicular
Concept This is the exact same theorem as Suggested Problem 3 — the book asks for the proof a second time in this lecture, which is a good opportunity to present it via the cleaner VECTOR method instead of resolving into x/y components. The vector dot-product approach reaches the same 90° conclusion in fewer lines and is worth having as a second tool.
Formula Momentum (vector form): u⃗ = v⃗₁ + v⃗₂ (mass m cancels). Energy: u² = v₁² + v₂² (KE conservation, mass cancels). Both are scalar/vector versions of the same two conservation laws used before — just combined differently here.
Solution
1. Let the moving particle (mass m) have initial velocity u⃗, and the stationary target (mass m) have zero initial velocity. After the elastic collision, their velocities are v⃗₁ and v⃗₂.
2. Momentum conservation (vector, m cancels since both masses equal): m u⃗ = m v⃗₁ + m v⃗₂ ⇒ u⃗ = v⃗₁ + v⃗₂.
3. Take the dot product of both sides with themselves: u⃗·u⃗ = (v⃗₁+v⃗₂)·(v⃗₁+v⃗₂) = v⃗₁·v⃗₁ + v⃗₂·v⃗₂ + 2(v⃗₁·v⃗₂) = v₁² + v₂² + 2(v⃗₁·v⃗₂).
4. So: u² = v₁² + v₂² + 2(v⃗₁·v⃗₂) …(i)
5. Energy conservation (elastic, equal masses, m cancels): ½mu² = ½mv₁² + ½mv₂² ⇒ u² = v₁² + v₂² …(ii)
6. Compare (i) and (ii): substituting (ii) into (i) forces 2(v⃗₁·v⃗₂) = 0 ⇒ v⃗₁·v⃗₂ = 0. Since v⃗₁ and v⃗₂ are both non-zero (a genuine oblique collision, not a degenerate head-on case), a zero dot product means they are perpendicular. QED.

   u·u = (v1+v2)·(v1+v2) = v1²+v2²+2(v1·v2) = u²
   ⇒ 2(v1·v2) = 0  ⇒  v1·v2 = 0  ⇒  v1 ⊥ v2
⚡ JEE Trick Keep BOTH proof techniques ready: the component (x/y) method for when you also need the individual angles, and this vector dot-product method for a fast, elegant "show that" answer when only the perpendicularity itself is asked. The vector method generalises immediately to 3-D, while the component method would need a third equation for a z-axis.
Answer: v⃗₁·v⃗₂ = 0, so the two particles' velocities are perpendicular after the collision.
7The system is initially at rest (a 1 kg block hangs over a pulley, connected by a string to a 4 kg block resting on the floor). The 1 kg block is lifted through 1.25 m and released. Find the speed (m/s) at which the 4 kg block leaves the floor.Sudden jerk in a string, impulsive tensionImpulsive Tensions in strings | sudden jerk in strings in a pulley block system🟡 Similar1 m/s
Concept "Lifted through 1.25 m" means the 1 kg block is raised TOWARD the pulley, creating 1.25 m of SLACK on the 4 kg block's side of the string. On release, the 1 kg block free-falls (string does nothing) until the slack is used up — then the string suddenly snaps taut, delivering an impulsive tension that jerks the 4 kg block into motion. This jerk is momentum-conserving (like a collision) but NOT energy-conserving — treat it exactly like a perfectly inelastic collision between the two blocks, mediated by the string.
Formula Free-fall speed at end of slack: v₀ = √(2gh). Sudden jerk (momentum conserved along the string, common final speed v since the inextensible string then locks the two speeds together): m₁v₀ = (m₁+m₂)v.
Solution
1. Free-fall phase: the 1 kg block falls freely through h=1.25 m (string slack, only gravity acts): v₀ = √(2×10×1.25) = √25 = 5 m/s (using g=10 m/s²).
2. Jerk phase: the instant the string becomes taut, an impulsive tension acts equally (and oppositely, via the pulley) on both blocks for a vanishingly short time — gravity's effect during this instant is negligible compared to the impulsive tension.
3. Since the string is inextensible and now taut, both blocks are forced to share the SAME speed magnitude v immediately after the jerk (one going down, the other up, linked by the pulley).
4. Treat this exactly like a perfectly inelastic collision: the falling 1 kg block (speed v₀) "collides" via the string with the 4 kg block (at rest), and together they move at common speed v: m₁v₀ = (m₁+m₂)v ⇒ (1)(5) = (5)v ⇒ v = 1 m/s.
5. This is the speed at which the 4 kg block begins to move — i.e., the moment it leaves the floor, since immediately after the jerk both blocks share this common upward-for-4kg / downward-for-1kg speed.
6. ⚠ Energy is genuinely lost in this jerk (just like in a collision): KE before jerk = ½(1)(5²)=12.5 J; KE just after = ½(5)(1²)=2.5 J — 10 J is dissipated as the string suddenly takes up the slack. A solution that instead applies energy conservation across the WHOLE 1.25 m fall (treating the string as taut the entire time) is inconsistent with the physical setup — a taut string could never let a 1 kg weight overcome a 4 kg weight to fall at all, so the system could not have been in motion during that phase.

   PHASE 2 (sudden jerk, like inelastic collision):
            1kg(v0=5) ── string taut ──▸ common v
            m1 v0 = (m1+m2) v  ⇒  1×5 = 5×v  ⇒  v=1 m/s

   4kg block leaves the floor at v = 1 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a string/rope suddenly becomes taut after slack, treat that instant exactly like a perfectly inelastic collision — momentum conserved, energy lost, common final speed from m₁v₀=(m₁+m₂)v. Never apply plain energy conservation across a slack-then-taut event; that error is common but always overestimates the final speed.
