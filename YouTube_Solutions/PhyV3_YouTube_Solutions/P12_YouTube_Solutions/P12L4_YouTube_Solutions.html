1The moment of inertia (MI) of a body primarily determines (a) how fast it can translate linearly (b) how easily it can start or stop rotating (c) the direction of its weight (d) its electrical resistance — (Level-1)Meaning of moment of inertiaRotational Motion 01: Introduction (Class 11, Ch 7)🟡 Similarbhow easily it can start or stop rotating
Concept Newton's second law for rotation is τ = Iα, and it is read exactly as F = ma. The quantity sitting between the push and the resulting change of motion is the body's inertia: mass for straight-line motion, moment of inertia for rotation about a fixed axis. So I answers one question only — how stubborn is this body when you try to spin it up or slow it down about this axis.
Formula τ = Iα → α = τ/I. The same torque on a bigger I gives a smaller α — literally "harder to start or stop rotating".
Solution
1. Definition: I = Σmiri², units kg m² — a mass-like unit.The r² is why one body has a different I about every different axis.
2. Test (a): translation obeys F = ma and its only inertia is m. Sliding mass outward changes I but not m, so I cannot govern translation.
3. Test (b): from α = τ/I a flywheel with large I barely responds to a torque and, once spinning, resists stopping — correct.
4. Test (c): the weight direction is set by gravity, vertically down, whatever the mass distribution; I has no say in it.
5. Test (d): resistance depends on resistivity, length and area — purely electrical, no mechanical inertia in it.Distractors (c) and (d) are there only to catch blind guessing.
6. Numeric check: I = MR²/2 = (2)(0.4)²/2 = 0.16 kg m², so α = τ/I = 0.32/0.16 = 2 rad/s²; double I and α halves.That halving is option (b) written in numbers.

 ---------------       -------------------
 mass   m              moment of inertia I
 force  F              torque  tau
 F = m a               tau = I alpha
 m resists change      I resists change
 of linear speed       of spin rate
⚡ JEE Trick Option (a) tempts anyone who half-remembers "inertia resists motion" and drops the word rotational. Write the analogy table above before any conceptual rotation MCQ: every symbol on the left has exactly one partner on the right, and I always pairs with m. Remember too that I is axis-dependent, so a body has many moments of inertia but only one mass.
Answer: (b) how easily it can start or stop rotating.
2A grindstone has a constant angular acceleration of 4 rad s−2. Starting from rest, calculate the angular speed of the grindstone 2.5 second later. — (Level-2)First equation of rotational motionRotational Kinematics Problems — Equations & Formulas🟡 Similar10 rad s−1
Concept A grindstone spun up by a motor turns about a fixed axle, so every point on it sweeps the same angle in the same time and one number ω describes the whole body. The angular acceleration is stated as constant, which is exactly the condition under which the three rotational kinematic equations hold. They are the linear SUVAT equations with s→θ, v→ω, a→α, so no new physics is needed.
Formula ω = ω0 + αt. This is the integral of α = dω/dt with α pulled out of the integral, which is legal only because α is constant.
Solution
1. List the data: ω0 = 0 (starts from rest), α = 4 rad/s², t = 2.5 s, and ω is wanted.
2. Pick the equation containing exactly those four symbols — the first one, ω = ω0 + αt.Choosing by "which variable is missing" saves time: θ is absent here, so use the θ-free equation.
3. Substitute and evaluate: ω = ω0 + αt = 0 + (4)(2.5) = 10 rad/s.
4. Units check: rad s−2 × s = rad s−1, so the answer is an angular speed, not a linear one.Radian is dimensionless, so this cannot be converted to m/s without a radius.
5. The angle swept, if wanted, is θ = ½αt² = ½(4)(2.5)² = 12.5 rad ≈ 1.99 revolutions.
6. Cross-check with the third equation: ω² = ω0² + 2αθ = 0 + 2(4)(12.5) = 100, so ω = 10 rad/s — the same value.Two independent equations agreeing is the fastest way to trust a kinematics answer.

                    alpha = 4 rad/s^2
                    t = 2.5 s

 theta = 0.5 alpha t^2 = 0.5(4)(6.25) = 12.5 rad

⚡ JEE Trick The classic trap is answering 10 rev/s or converting to rpm because the word "grindstone" suggests machinery. Nothing in the data mentions revolutions, so the answer stays in rad/s. The second trap is the phrase "2.5 second later" — later than the start, so t = 2.5 s, not an interval added to some earlier time.
Answer: 10 rad s−1.
3Calculate the kinetic energy of rotation of a circular disc of mass 1 kg and radius 0.2 m rotating about an axis passing through its centre and perpendicular to its plane. The disc is making 30/π rotations per minute. — (Level-3)Rotational kinetic energy of a discRotational Motion in 1 Shot — All Concepts & PYQs (JEE)🟡 Similar0.01 J
Concept Every particle of the spinning disc moves on its own circle with speed vi = ωri, so summing ½mivi² over the body gives ½ω²Σmiri² = ½Iω². The odd-looking rate 30/π rpm is chosen deliberately so that the factor 2π in ω = 2πn cancels the π and leaves a clean number. Work in SI throughout: kg, m, rad/s.
Formula Idisc = ½MR² and K = ½Iω². The ½MR² is for the axis through the centre perpendicular to the plane, which is exactly the axis named.
Solution
1. Convert the rate to rev/s: n = (30/π) per minute = (30/π)/60 = 0.5/π rev/s.Dividing by 60 first keeps the π visible so you can watch it cancel.
2. Angular speed: ω = 2πn = 2π(0.5/π) = 1 rad/s — the π cancels exactly.
3. Moment of inertia: I = ½MR² = ½(1)(0.2)² = ½(0.04) = 0.02 kg m².
4. Kinetic energy: K = ½Iω² = ½(0.02)(1)² = 0.01 J.
5. Sanity check on size: the rim speed is v = ωR = 0.2 m/s, so a full 1 kg moving at 0.2 m/s would carry only 0.02 J; the disc has half of that because the inner mass moves slower.K is always less than ½Mvrim² for a body spinning about its centre.
6. Note what is not asked: the centre is fixed, so there is no translational term ½Mvcm² to add.Only a rolling disc would need the extra ½Mvcm², doubling the answer.

 w     = 2 pi n = 1 rad/s
 I     = 0.5 M R^2 = 0.5 (1)(0.04) = 0.02 kg m^2
 K     = 0.5 I w^2 = 0.5 (0.02)(1) = 0.01 J

Second, forgetting the ½ in I = ½MR² and using the ring value MR² doubles the answer to 0.02 J. Whenever a question hands you a π in the data, expect it to cancel — if it survives to your answer, re-check the conversion.

4A uniform rod of length ℓ, mass m is hung from two strings of equal length from a ceiling as shown in figure. Determine the tensions in the strings? (String A hangs from the left end of the rod; string B hangs at a distance 3ℓ/4 from A, leaving ℓ/4 of rod beyond B.) — (Level-4)Rotational equilibrium of a hung rodTorque — A Meter Stick in Static Equilibrium🟡 SimilarTA = mg/3, TB = 2mg/3
Concept The rod hangs at rest, so it is in complete equilibrium: the vector sum of forces is zero and the sum of torques about every point is zero. Three vertical forces act — the two string tensions up and the weight mg down at the centre of mass. Because the rod is uniform its weight acts at the geometric mid-point, a distance ℓ/2 from the left end, which lies between the two strings.
Formula ΣF = 0 and Στ = 0. Taking torques about a point where an unknown acts kills that unknown, so one equation carries one unknown.
Solution
1. Set the origin at the left end. Then A acts at x = 0, B at x = 3ℓ/4, and the weight mg at x = ℓ/2.The ℓ/4 stub beyond B carries no separate force; it is already inside "uniform rod".
2. Vertical balance: TA + TB = mg.
3. Torque about A (this removes TA): TB(3ℓ/4) = mg(ℓ/2).
4. Solve: TB = mg(ℓ/2)(4/3ℓ) = 2mg/3 ≈ 0.667 mg.
5. Back-substitute: TA = mg − 2mg/3 = mg/3 ≈ 0.333 mg.B is nearer the centre of mass, so B must carry the larger share — the numbers agree.
6. Independent check, torques about B: TA(3ℓ/4) = mg(3ℓ/4 − ℓ/2) = mg(ℓ/4), so TA = mg/3.Any pivot must give the same answer; if it does not, a lever arm is wrong.
   A            cm        B
   |------------|---------|-----|
   0          l/2       3l/4    l
   T_A          mg       T_B
 sum F : T_A + T_B = mg
 about A: T_B (3l/4) = mg (l/2) -> T_B = 2mg/3
 then    T_A = mg - 2mg/3 = mg/3
⚡ JEE Trick The tempting wrong answer is mg/2 each, from assuming the strings are symmetric about the centre — but B is off-centre, so the split is 1:2. The second trap is measuring B's lever arm from the centre of mass instead of from the pivot you actually chose. Fix the origin first, write every x-coordinate down, and the lever arms cannot go wrong.
Answer: TA = mg/3 and TB = 2mg/3.

Concept The step-ladder is a symmetric two-bar frame. The floor is frictionless, so it can only push straight up; the legs would splay apart were it not for the crossbar, which is therefore in tension. Treat the whole ladder first to get the normals, then isolate one leg and take torques about the apex — that pivot removes the unknown hinge force the two legs exert on each other.

1Force in linear motion has its analogue in rotational motion: (a) moment of inertia (b) torque (c) angular momentum (d) weight — (Level-1)Linear–rotational analogy: force ↔ torqueRotation — Torque and Rotational Dynamics (JEE Physics, Lecture 3)🟡 Similarbtorque
Concept Rotational dynamics is built by copying translational dynamics symbol for symbol. Displacement becomes angular displacement, velocity becomes ω, mass becomes I, and the agent that changes the state of motion becomes torque. The pairing is fixed by the place each quantity occupies in its law of motion, not by how the words sound — so "the analogue of force" means "whatever sits where F sits in F = ma".
Formula F = ma and τ = Iα, with τ = r F sinθ. Line the two laws up and F and τ occupy the identical slot, which settles the analogy in one look.
Solution
1. Write the two laws one under the other: F = ma, τ = Iα. Matching slot by slot gives F ↔ τ, m ↔ I, a ↔ α.
2. Test (a): I = Σmiri² carries units kg m², a mass-like unit — so I is the analogue of mass, not of force.A quick unit check kills this option: N versus kg m² are not comparable.
3. Test (c): L = Iω matches p = mv, so angular momentum is the analogue of linear momentum, not of force.
4. Test (d): weight is one particular force (W = mg); it is a member of the family F belongs to, not the rotational partner of it.
5. Test (b): τ = r F sinθ has units N m and, put into τ = Iα, it produces angular acceleration exactly as F produces a — correct.Torque is not a new kind of push; it is force weighted by its lever arm.
6. Numeric check: I = MR²/2 = (2)(0.4)²/2 = 0.16 kg m² and τ = FR = (2)(0.4) = 0.8 N m give α = τ/I = 0.8/0.16 = 5 rad/s², the rotational twin of a = F/m.
 linear            rotational
 --------------    -----------------
 s   displacement  theta
 v   velocity      omega
 a   acceleration  alpha
 m   mass          I  = sum m r^2
 F   force         tau = r F sin(th)
 F = m a           tau = I alpha
⚡ JEE Trick Option (a) traps anyone who pairs by the word "inertia" instead of by position in the equation — moment of inertia partners mass. Option (c) traps the student who remembers that both torque and angular momentum involve r × (something) and picks the wrong one; τ = r×F pairs with F while L = r×p pairs with p. Build the table above once and every analogy MCQ in this chapter answers itself.
Answer: (b) torque.
2When a steady torque or couple acts on a body, the body: (a) continues in a state of rest or of uniform motion by Newton's 1st law (b) gets linear acceleration by Newton's 2nd law (c) gets an angular acceleration (d) continues to rotate at a steady rate — (Level-1)Steady torque produces constant angular accelerationRotational Motion (Part-1) — MOI, Torque & Static Equilibrium🟡 Similarcgets an angular acceleration
Concept A couple is a pair of equal, opposite, non-collinear forces: its vector sum is zero but its moment is not. Zero net force means the centre of mass does not accelerate; a non-zero net torque means the spin rate must change. So a steady couple leaves translation untouched and drives rotation at a constant rate of change of ω — the rotational counterpart of a constant force on a free particle.
Formula ΣF = 0 for a couple, while τ = Iα ≠ 0. A constant τ with a fixed I forces α to be a non-zero constant, not zero.
Solution
1. Couple: forces F and −F separated by perpendicular distance d, so ΣF = 0 but τ = Fd about every point.That "every point" is the signature of a couple — its moment does not depend on the pivot chosen.
2. Test (a): Newton's first law needs zero net torque as well as zero net force; here τ ≠ 0, so the rotational state cannot stay unchanged.
3. Test (b): linear acceleration needs ΣF ≠ 0, and for a couple ΣF = 0 exactly — the centre of mass stays put or drifts uniformly.
4. Test (d): "steady rate" would need α = 0, i.e. τ = 0. A steady torque gives steady α, which makes ω grow linearly, not stay fixed.Confusing "steady torque" with "steady speed" is the classic first-law slip carried over from F = ma.
5. Test (c): from α = τ/I a constant τ gives a constant, non-zero α — correct.
6. Numeric check: τ = Fd = (5)(0.4) = 2 N m on I = 0.5 kg m² gives α = 2/0.5 = 4 rad/s², so ω = αt climbs 4 rad/s every second while the body's centre never accelerates.
      F  ->
  ----+------------+----   d = 0.4 m
                <- F
 sum F = 0    -> no linear accel
 tau  = F d = 5 x 0.4 = 2 N m
 alpha = tau / I = 2 / 0.5 = 4 rad/s^2
 omega = 0, 4, 8, 12 ... rad/s
⚡ JEE Trick Option (d) is the trap: students transfer "constant force → constant acceleration" correctly but then read "constant α" as "constant ω". Say it aloud — a steady torque makes the spin rate change steadily. Option (b) is the second trap, from forgetting that a couple's two forces cancel, so no linear acceleration can appear no matter how big F is.
Answer: (c) gets an angular acceleration.
3A rigid body is rotating about an axis, to stop the rotation we have to apply: (a) pressure (b) force (c) torque (d) momentum — (Level-1)Only torque changes spin; a force through the axis does nothingRotational Motion 03 — Rotational Equilibrium and Torque Problems🟡 Similarctorque
Concept Stopping a spin means driving ω to zero, and only an angular deceleration can do that. From τ = Iα, the one quantity that can produce α is torque. A force is necessary but not sufficient: a force whose line of action passes through the rotation axis has zero lever arm and therefore zero torque, so it can press hard on the body and still not slow it by one radian per second.
Formula τ = r F sinθ = Iα and ω = ω0 + αt. Setting ω = 0 needs α ≠ 0, which needs τ ≠ 0 — not merely F ≠ 0.
Solution
1. Requirement: ω must fall from ω0 to 0, so α ≠ 0, so from τ = Iα we need a non-zero net torque.
2. Test (b): a radial force aimed straight at the axis has r sinθ = 0, so τ = 0 and ω never changes.Push a spinning wheel exactly toward its hub as hard as you like — it keeps spinning; that is why (b) is wrong.
3. Test (a): pressure is force per unit area, a scalar; it has no lever arm and no sense of rotation, so it cannot appear in τ = Iα.
4. Test (d): momentum is a state of the body, not an action applied to it; you can transfer momentum, but what stops the spin is the moment of that transfer.
5. Test (c): applying a tangential force at radius r gives τ = Fr opposing ω, hence α = −τ/I and the body halts — correct.A brake shoe works exactly this way: tangential friction at the rim, maximum lever arm.
6. Numeric check: I = 0.16 kg m² spinning at ω0 = 10 rad/s, braked by τ = 0.8 N m, gives α = −0.8/0.16 = −5 rad/s² and t = ω0/|α| = 2 s to stop.
  spinning disc, axis at O
        F (radial)          F (tangential)
   ----> * O                   O *----
   arm = 0, tau = 0        arm = r, tau = F r

⚡ JEE Trick Option (b) is the near-miss designed to catch fast readers: every torque is produced by a force, so "force" feels right, but the question asks what must be applied, and a force alone is not enough — it must be off-axis. The exam version of this idea is the door: push on the hinge line and nothing happens, push at the handle and it swings. Always ask "what is the lever arm?" before you accept a force as the answer.
Answer: (c) torque.

Concept A rigid body obeys two independent laws: ΣF = Macm governs where the centre of mass goes, and Στ = Iα governs how fast it spins. Each cause owns exactly one effect. Torque appears only in the second equation, so its sole job is to change the rotational state; it has no entry in the translational equation at all.
Formula ΣF = Macm and Στcm = Iα. Two separate equations means two separate causes — τ never enters the first one.
Solution
1. Split the dynamics: translation is decided by ΣF alone, rotation about the centre of mass by Στ alone.
2. Test (a): a pure couple has ΣF = 0 with τ = Fd ≠ 0; the centre of mass stays at rest, so torque plainly does not cause translation.This single counter-example eliminates (a) and (d) together.

4. Test (c): oscillation needs a restoring torque, τ = −kθ. That is a special sign-dependent case, not what torque does in general.A constant torque gives runaway spin, not oscillation — so (c) describes one situation, not the cause.
5. Test (b): from α = τ/I any non-zero torque immediately changes ω, which is rotatory motion — correct.
6. Numeric check: a tangential 2 N on a rim of radius 0.4 m gives τ = 0.8 N m; with I = 0.16 kg m², α = 5 rad/s², while a fixed axle supplies the reaction that keeps acm = 0.The axle absorbing the net force is what makes the motion purely rotatory.

1A fly wheel of moment of inertia 6 × 10−2 kg m² is rotating with an angular velocity of 20 rad s−1. Find the steady torque (in Nm) required to bring it to rest in 4 seconds. — (Level-2)τ = Iα with a braking torqueRotational Motion — Most Important Questions in 1 Shot (JEE Main)🟡 Similar−0.3 N m (magnitude 0.3 N m, retarding)
Concept A steady torque produces a constant angular acceleration, so the flywheel's spin decays linearly in time exactly as a car's speed does under constant braking. Two steps settle the problem: get α from the rotational first equation of motion, then convert it to torque with τ = Iα. Because the torque opposes the spin it comes out negative; only its magnitude is asked for as a size.
Formula ω = ω0 + αt and τ = Iα. "Steady torque" guarantees α is constant, which is the only condition under which the linear kinematic form is legal.
Solution
1. Data: I = 6 × 10−2 kg m² = 0.06 kg m², ω0 = 20 rad/s, ω = 0, t = 4 s.
2. Angular acceleration: α = (ω − ω0)/t = (0 − 20)/4 = −5 rad/s².The minus sign is not decoration — it says the torque acts against the rotation.
3. Torque: τ = Iα = (0.06)(−5) = −0.3 N m, i.e. 0.3 N m applied opposite to ω.
4. Cross-check by angular impulse: τt = ΔL = IΔω = (0.06)(−20) = −1.2 kg m²/s, so τ = −1.2/4 = −0.3 N m — same value.The impulse route skips α entirely and is faster when the time is given.
5. Angle turned while stopping: θ = ½(ω0 + ω)t = ½(20)(4) = 40 rad, about 6.4 revolutions.
6. Energy audit: KE = ½Iω0² = ½(0.06)(400) = 12 J, and |τ|θ = (0.3)(40) = 12 J — the brake absorbs exactly the stored rotational energy.Work–energy agreeing to the joule is the strongest check available here.
