1Which statement correctly describes gases?Gas characteristicsA-GASCHAR🟡 SimilarcThey assume the shape and volume of the container.
Concept A gas has neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume. Its molecules are far apart with negligible intermolecular attraction and move randomly at high speed, so they spread out to occupy every corner of whatever vessel holds them. This is exactly what distinguishes a gas from a liquid (fixed volume, no fixed shape) and a solid (both fixed) — the gas takes on both the shape and the volume of its container.
Key idea gas: no fixed shape + no fixed volume ⇒ fills the container completely. Because the molecules are not held together, expansion continues until the walls stop it — so container shape = gas shape and container volume = gas volume.
Solution
1. Compare the three states on "fixed shape?" and "fixed volume?":
 state    fixed shape?   fixed volume?   fills container?
 solid    yes            yes             no
 liquid   no             yes             no (only to its own volume)
 gas      no             no              YES — shape AND volume
2. Only the gas answers "no" to both, so it adopts the container's shape and expands to fill its whole volume.Weak intermolecular forces + random motion let the molecules disperse throughout the vessel.
3. That statement is option (c); the other options describe liquids/solids or are false for gases.
⚡ JEE Trick The one-line test for "is it a gas?": a gas is the only state with no definite shape AND no definite volume. Trap: statements saying a gas has a "definite volume" describe a liquid — reject them on sight.
Answer: (c) They assume the shape and volume of the container.
2At NTP a gas occupies 5.6 L — calculate moles (Vm=22.4 L)Mole ↔ volumewatch?v=QMnujSZpKZE🟢 Exactc0.25 mol
Concept Avogadro's principle: equal volumes of all ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. At NTP (0 °C, 1 atm) one mole of any gas — H₂, CO₂, SO₂, it does not matter — occupies the molar volume Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹. So a "volume at NTP → moles" question is pure division: count how many 22.4-L packets fit in the given volume. The gas's identity is a distractor, never a data item.
Formula n = V / Vm Vm is by definition the volume of one mole at the stated conditions, so dividing the total volume by it counts moles directly. Data: V = 5.6 L, Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (given).
Solution
1. Conditions check: the gas is at NTP and the question hands us Vm = 22.4 L — the formula applies with no correction.If T or P were non-standard we would need PV = nRT instead.
2. Substitute: n = V/Vm = 5.6/22.4 = 0.25 mol.5.6/22.4 = 56/224 = 1/4 — cancel before reaching for a calculator.
3. Cross-check by scaling down: 22.4 L ⇒ 1 mol, so 22.4/4 = 5.6 L ⇒ 1/4 mol. The given volume is exactly a quarter of the molar volume, so exactly a quarter mole.
4. Optional depth: number of molecules N = n × NA = 0.25 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.506×10²³ molecules, and if the gas were, say, CO₂ its mass would be 0.25 × 44 = 11 g — every mole quantity scales by the same 0.25.
 V (L)    V_m (L/mol)    n = V/V_m
 5.6      22.4           0.25 mol    <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the 22.4 ladder — 22.4 L→1 mol, 11.2→0.5, 5.6→0.25, 2.24→0.1 — these exact volumes appear in paper after paper, so the answer should be instant. Trap: "new STP" (1 bar, 0 °C) has Vm = 22.7 L; use 22.4 only for classic NTP/STP at 1 atm, or when the paper states it (as here).
Answer: (c) 0.25 mol.

4Containers A (rigid) & B (piston), equal moles, heated equally — which statement?Gas behaviourLevel 2 Problems on SPM | Piston Fitted Container | States of Matter | Class 11 Chemistry | JEE🟢 ExactbVolume of B will increase while pressure of A will increase.
Concept Heating a gas raises its temperature, and by the ideal-gas law PV = nRT something on the left must rise to match. Which one rises depends on what the container allows to change. A rigid vessel (A) locks the volume, so the extra "push" shows up as higher pressure. A piston vessel (B) is free to move against a constant external pressure, so the gas expands and its volume rises while its pressure stays fixed at the outside value.
Formula PV = nRT (n equal in both, same ΔT). Rigid ⇒ V constant ⇒ P ∝ T (Gay-Lussac); movable piston ⇒ P constant ⇒ V ∝ T (Charles). The constraint decides which variable absorbs the temperature rise.
Solution
1. Container A (rigid): V is fixed, so with T↑, P must rise — pressure of A increases (Gay-Lussac, P ∝ T at constant V).
2. Container B (piston, constant external P): P is pinned by the atmosphere/piston, so with T↑, V must rise — volume of B increases (Charles, V ∝ T at constant P).
3. Combine: "Volume of B increases while pressure of A increases" — exactly option (b).
 container   constraint        T↑ forces        result
 A (rigid)   V = constant      P must rise      pressure ↑
 B (piston)  P = constant      V must rise      volume ↑
⚡ JEE Trick In PV=nRT, first ask "what is held constant?" — that variable's partner absorbs the change. Rigid → pressure moves; free piston → volume moves. Trap: options that let A's volume change (it can't — walls are rigid) or B's pressure change (it can't — piston holds P) are automatically wrong.
Answer: (b) Volume of B will increase while pressure of A will increase.

1What are the different states of matter?States of matterA-STATES🟡 Similar—
Concept Matter exists in different states depending on the balance between the intermolecular forces holding particles together and the thermal (kinetic) energy pulling them apart. At the syllabus level there are three physical states — solid, liquid and gas — with plasma (an ionised gas) sometimes named as a fourth. Which state a substance shows depends on temperature and pressure; the same substance (e.g. water) can move between all three.
Key relation state is set by (intermolecular force) vs (thermal energy). Strong forces > thermal energy ⇒ solid; comparable ⇒ liquid; thermal energy ≫ forces ⇒ gas.
Solution
1. Solid — strong forces, particles locked in a lattice: definite shape, definite volume, nearly incompressible.
2. Liquid — moderate forces, particles touch but slide: no definite shape (takes container shape), definite volume, slightly compressible.
3. Gas — negligible forces, particles far apart and fast: no definite shape, no definite volume (fills the container), highly compressible.
 state    forces      shape       volume      compressibility
 solid    strong      definite    definite    negligible
 liquid   moderate    no          definite    slight
 gas      negligible  no          no          high
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the states as a ladder of decreasing intermolecular force: solid → liquid → gas. Heating climbs down the ladder (melting, boiling); cooling/compression climbs up (condensation, freezing). Plasma is a bonus fourth state only if the question hints "at very high temperature."
Key point: the three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas (plasma as a fourth), determined by the competition between intermolecular forces and thermal energy.
2What are intermolecular forces? How do they differ across states?Intermolecular forcesA-STATES🟡 Similar—
Concept Intermolecular forces are the attractive (and weakly repulsive) forces acting between neighbouring molecules — quite distinct from the strong intramolecular bonds within a molecule. They include weak van der Waals (London dispersion, dipole–dipole) forces and stronger hydrogen bonds. Their strength relative to the molecules' thermal energy is precisely what fixes the physical state and explains why solids are rigid while gases fly apart.
Key relation force strength: solid > liquid ≫ gas; and as force ↓, molecular separation ↑ and freedom of motion ↑. Closer, more tightly bound molecules mean a more ordered, less mobile state.
Solution
1. Define: intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules; they hold condensed matter together.
2. Solids — forces strongest; molecules held in fixed positions, only vibrate ⇒ rigid, ordered.
3. Liquids — forces weaker; molecules stay close but can slide past one another ⇒ flow, fixed volume.
4. Gases — forces negligible; molecules far apart, move freely at high speed ⇒ fill any container, easily compressed.
 state    intermolecular force   molecular arrangement   motion
 solid    strongest              fixed lattice           vibrate in place
 liquid   moderate               close, disordered       slide/flow
 gas      negligible             far apart               free, rapid
⚡ JEE Trick Do not confuse intermolecular (between molecules — decides state, boiling point) with intramolecular/chemical bonds (within a molecule — decide the substance's identity). Higher intermolecular forces ⇒ higher melting/boiling points. Trap: breaking intermolecular forces (boiling water) does not break the O–H bonds inside H₂O.
Key point: intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules; they are strongest in solids, weaker in liquids and negligible in gases — which is why the states differ in rigidity, order and compressibility.
3List the characteristic physical properties of gasesGas characteristicsA-GASCHAR🟡 Similar—
Concept Every characteristic property of a gas traces back to one structural fact: its molecules are far apart with negligible intermolecular attraction and are in constant, rapid, random motion. From that single picture you can derive all the observable behaviours — expansion, compressibility, low density, mixing and pressure — so it is worth stating the cause once and reading the properties off it.
Key relation large separation + weak forces + random rapid motion ⇒ gas behaviour. Empty space between molecules explains compressibility and low density; free motion explains expansion, diffusion and wall pressure.
Solution — the characteristic physical properties are:
1. No fixed shape or volume — a gas expands to fill the whole container.
2. Highly compressible — large gaps between molecules can be squeezed, unlike solids/liquids.
3. Low density — few molecules per unit volume, so mass per volume is small (≈1000× less than liquids).
4. Exert pressure equally in all directions — molecules strike every wall; pressure is isotropic.
5. Mix/diffuse freely — gases intermingle in all proportions to form homogeneous mixtures.
6. Expand on heating / respond to pressure — obey the gas laws (V ∝ T, V ∝ 1/P).
 property            underlying reason
 fills container     free random motion, weak forces
 compressible        large intermolecular space
 low density         few molecules per unit volume
 uniform pressure    molecules hit all walls equally
 diffuse & mix       high molecular speed, no attraction
⚡ JEE Trick If asked to "list" gas properties, anchor each to the far-apart-and-freely-moving model — examiners award the mark for linking property to cause, not just naming it. Trap: writing "definite volume" for a gas — that is a liquid property.
Key point: gases have no fixed shape or volume, are highly compressible, have low density, exert pressure equally in all directions, and diffuse/expand freely — all consequences of far-apart, freely moving molecules with negligible attraction.
4Explain the measurable properties of gasesMeasurable propertiesA-MEASURE🟡 Similar—
Concept To describe the state of a gas quantitatively we need a small set of measurable properties. Four suffice — mass (or moles), volume, pressure and temperature — because any one is fixed once the other three are known, through the ideal-gas equation. Knowing how each is defined and measured is the foundation for every numerical in this chapter (mole/NTP volume, barometer, manometer, unit conversion).
Key relation PV = nRT links all four. n = m/M ties mass to moles; so specifying P, V, T and amount completely determines the gas — this is why exactly these four are the "measurable properties."
Solution
1. Mass / amount — found by weighing the container full and empty; the difference is the gas mass m, converted to moles by n = m/M.
2. Volume — a gas fills its vessel, so its volume equals the container volume (SI unit m³; common L, mL).
3. Pressure — force per unit area on the walls, measured by a barometer (atmosphere) or manometer (a confined gas); units atm, bar, Pa, mmHg/torr.
4. Temperature — a measure of average kinetic energy; measured with a thermometer and used on the absolute (Kelvin) scale in gas laws (K = °C + 273.15).
 property     symbol   typical units        measured by
 mass/amount  m, n     g, mol               balance (weigh full − empty)
 volume       V        m³, L, mL            = container volume
 pressure     P        atm, bar, Pa, mmHg   barometer / manometer
 temperature  T        K (= °C + 273.15)    thermometer
⚡ JEE Trick Always convert to the "gas-law-ready" units before substituting into PV=nRT: T in K, P and V matching R. Trap: leaving temperature in °C is the single most common error — the gas laws are only linear in the absolute (Kelvin) temperature.
Key point: the measurable properties of a gas are mass/amount, volume, pressure and temperature; they are linked by PV = nRT, so fixing three fixes the fourth.

2Gases can be liquefied by …LiquefactionA gas can be liquefied by ___________. | CLASS 11 | STATES OF MATTER (GASES AND LIQUIDS) | CHEMISTRY🟢 ExactcBoth A and B (decreasing temperature & increasing pressure)
Concept Liquefaction means forcing gas molecules close enough for their intermolecular attractions to take hold. Two levers do this: lowering the temperature (removes kinetic energy so molecules move slowly enough to be trapped by attraction) and raising the pressure (pushes molecules physically closer together). In practice both are used together — but cooling is essential, because pressure alone cannot liquefy a gas that is above its critical temperature.
Key relation liquefy ⇐ T↓ and/or P↑ (below critical temperature). Below Tc, enough pressure liquefies the gas; above Tc, no pressure can — so cooling below Tc is the enabling step.
Solution
1. Effect of cooling: lowers average kinetic energy; slow molecules are captured by intermolecular forces → condensation.
2. Effect of compression: reduces the space between molecules so attractions become significant → condensation.
3. Both statements A (decrease T) and B (increase P) are correct ways to liquefy a gas, so the answer is Both A and B — option (c).
 method          what it does                     helps liquefy?

 ⇒ Both A and B                                     ✓ option (c)
⚡ JEE Trick "Cool AND compress" is the standard recipe, but the decisive factor is temperature — a gas above its critical temperature cannot be liquefied by any pressure. Trap: choosing "only increasing pressure" ignores this critical-temperature limit.
Answer: (c) Both A and B (decreasing temperature & increasing pressure).

4Formula for pressure from a mercury column?P=h·d·gFormula for pressure in liquids is (a) P = hdg where h is height, d is density, g is acceleration due to gravity🟢 ExactcP = h × d × g
Concept The pressure at the bottom of a stationary fluid column comes from the weight of the fluid above pressing on the area below. Deriving it from first principles shows why the answer is a product of height, density and gravity — and why the tube's cross-section cancels out, so a thin and a wide barometer read the same pressure for the same mercury height.
Formula P = h · d · g (h = column height, d = fluid density, g = gravitational acceleration). Pressure = force/area = weight/area; the derivation below shows the area cancels, leaving h·d·g.
Solution
1. Take a column of fluid, height h and cross-sectional area A.Set up the geometry so we can find the weight sitting on area A.
2. Volume of fluid = A × h; mass = density × volume = d·A·h.
3. Weight (force) = mass × g = d·A·h·g.
4. Pressure = force ÷ area = (d·A·h·g) ÷ A = h·d·g — the area A cancels.
 P = force/area = (d·A·h·g) / A = h·d·g
   (cross-section A cancels ⇒ tube width is irrelevant)
5. So the correct formula is P = h × d × g — option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Because A cancels, barometer readings depend only on the height of mercury, never the tube's thickness. Keep units consistent: SI (m, kg m⁻³, 9.8 m s⁻²) → Pa; CGS (cm, g cm⁻³, 980 cm s⁻²) → dyne cm⁻². Trap: options with h/dg or hd/g are dimensionally wrong — only the triple product gives pressure.
Answer: (c) P = h × d × g.

Concept The mole is a counting unit: dividing a sample's mass by its molar mass tells you how many "chemist's dozens" of particles it contains. For a compound the molar mass is just the sum of the atomic masses in one formula unit. CO₂ is the classic self-checking example because its molar mass works out to 44 g mol⁻¹ — the very number in the question — so the arithmetic collapses to 44 ÷ 44.
