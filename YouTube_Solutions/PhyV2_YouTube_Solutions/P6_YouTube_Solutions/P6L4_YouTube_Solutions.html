1Two masses m₁ and m₂ connected by an inextensible cord, m₁ sliding on a frictionless surface and m₂ hanging from a light frictionless pulley. Mass of the falling mass m₂ if m₁ and tension T are known? (a) T/g (b) Tm₁/(gm₁−T) (c) (m₁g−T)/g (d) None — (Level-1)Table + hanging mass, tensionMass on table & hanging mass over pulley🟡 SimilarbTm₁/(gm₁ − T)
Concept This is the classic table-and-hanging-mass pair. The cord is inextensible, so the sliding block m₁ and the falling block m₂ share one magnitude of acceleration a (m/s²); the pulley is light and frictionless, so the same tension T (N) acts on both ends. The trick here is that T is given, not unknown — so the fastest route is to get a from the block whose only horizontal force is T, then feed that a into m₂'s equation and solve for m₂.
Formula Newton's second law on each body: for m₁ (horizontal, frictionless) T = m₁ a — T in N, m₁ in kg, a in m/s². For m₂ (vertical, taking downward positive) m₂ g − T = m₂ a — g in m/s². Both read force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s²), so every term is in newtons. Constraint: the cord length is fixed, so a₁ = a₂ = a.
Solution
1. Free-body of m₁ on the smooth table: the only unbalanced horizontal force is the cord tension T (N), pulling toward the pulley.Weight m₁g and normal N are vertical and cancel; the surface is frictionless, so no friction term.
2. Apply T = m₁ a ⇒ a = T / m₁ (units: N / kg = m/s² ✓).This is the whole point of the problem — knowing T and m₁ already fixes a, before m₂ is even used.
3. Free-body of the hanging m₂: weight m₂g (N) down, tension T (N) up, and it accelerates down at the same a.
4. Apply m₂ g − T = m₂ a and substitute a = T/m₁:
  m₂ g − T = m₂ (T / m₁)
5. Collect the m₂ terms on one side:
  m₂ g − m₂ T / m₁ = T  ⇒  m₂ ( g − T / m₁ ) = T
6. Solve and clear the inner fraction by multiplying top and bottom by m₁:
  m₂ = T / (g − T/m₁) = T m₁ / (g m₁ − T)Units check: N·kg / (m·s⁻²·kg − N) = N·kg / N = kg ✓ — a mass, as required.

2A smooth wedge has angle θ = 30°. Find acceleration of a block sliding down it. (a) 5.0 (b) 8.7 (c) 3.0 (d) 9.8 m/s² — (Level-2)Block on smooth incline, a = g sinθAcceleration of block down a frictionless incline🟡 Similara5.0 m/s²
Concept A block on a smooth (frictionless) incline feels only two forces: its weight mg straight down and the normal reaction N perpendicular to the surface. Resolving along and perpendicular to the incline, N kills the perpendicular component of the weight, leaving only the along-incline component mg sinθ to drive the motion. Because the mass cancels, the acceleration depends on θ and g alone — no mass is given in the question, and none is needed.
Formula a = g sinθ — a in m/s², g = 9.8 m/s², θ the incline angle (dimensionless). Derived from m a = mg sinθ, in which the mass m (kg) cancels from both sides, leaving m/s² × a pure number = m/s² ✓. Perpendicular: N = mg cosθ (N), which does no work and produces no acceleration because the block stays on the surface.
Solution
1. Set axes along the incline (direction of motion) and perpendicular to it.Choosing axes along the incline means the acceleration has only one component, so no vector components of a need to be split.
2. Resolve the weight mg (N): component along the slope = mg sinθ, component into the slope = mg cosθ.
3. Perpendicular equation: N − mg cosθ = 0 (no acceleration perpendicular to the surface) ⇒ N = mg cosθ (N).
4. Along-slope equation: mg sinθ = m a. The surface is smooth, so there is no friction term.
5. Cancel m (kg) from both sides: a = g sinθ (m/s²).
6. Substitute θ = 30°, sin30° = 0.5, g = 9.8 m/s²:
  a = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s² ≈ 5.0 m/s²4.9 rounds to the printed option 5.0 m/s²; with the exam shortcut g = 10 m/s² you get exactly 5.0 m/s².

3Find speed of block A (A horizontal, over pulley to hanging block B), given v_B = 12 m/s. — (Level-3)Pulley velocity constraintBlock A vs block B speed relation (constraint)🟡 Similar8 m s⁻¹
Concept This is a pure string-constraint (kinematics) problem — no masses, no forces needed. The book's figure shows one continuous inextensible string, and block A is tied into it in three places: once directly from A's top, and twice through the movable pulley bolted to A. Block B hangs from a movable pulley supported by two strands. Whenever a body is held by n strands of the same string, that body's speed is multiplied by n in the length bookkeeping — so the answer follows from counting strands, not from Newton's laws.
Formula Inextensible string ⇒ Σ (segment lengths) = constant (m), so Σ (rate of change of each segment) = 0 (m/s). Equivalently, (strands on A) × v_A = (strands on B) × v_B, with every v in m/s. A speed multiplied by a pure count stays in m/s, so both sides are m/s ✓. Read from the figure: strands on A = 3, strands on B's pulley = 2.
Solution
1. Trace the single string in the figure. Its five parts are: (i) top of A → the upper fixed pulley; (ii) upper fixed pulley → down to B's movable pulley; (iii) B's movable pulley → up to the second fixed pulley; (iv) that pulley → left, around the pulley bolted to A; (v) around A's pulley → right, to the fixed wall.Segments (i), (iv) and (v) all run horizontally between A and fixed points, so each one changes by exactly the displacement of A.
2. Count how many horizontal segments A's motion changes: segment (i) + the two segments (iv) and (v) at A's own pulley = 3 strands on A.
3. Count the vertical strands supporting B's movable pulley: segments (ii) and (iii) = 2 strands on B.
4. Length bookkeeping: if A moves a distance d (m) toward the pulleys, the three horizontal segments shorten by a total of 3d (m). That freed length must go into the two vertical strands, so B descends h (m) with 2h = 3d.The string cannot stretch, so length removed from one place must appear somewhere else.
5. Differentiate with respect to time: 3 v_A = 2 v_B (m/s on both sides ✓).
6. Substitute v_B = 12 m/s: 3 v_A = 2 × 12 = 24 m/s ⇒ v_A = 24 / 3 = 8 m/s.

Equivalent power check: T·3·v_A must equal T·2·v_B for an ideal string, giving the same relation instantly. Trap: a movable pulley bolted to a block contributes two strands to that block, not one — miscounting here is the single commonest error; here A's own pulley gives 2, plus the direct strand = 3.
Answer: v_A = 8 m/s (block A moves at two-thirds of B's speed, since 3 strands act on A against 2 on B).
4Find the acceleration of block A (a_A = ?) assuming all surfaces smooth; a_C = 2 m/s², a_B = 1 m/s². — (Level-4)Pulley acceleration constraintPulley-block constrained motion (JEE/NEET)🟡 Similara_A = ½ m s⁻² (0.5 m/s²)
Concept Another pure constraint problem, but with a twist: instead of a block hanging from the movable pulley, the pulley itself carries a second string with blocks B and C on its two sides. So there are two independent strings. Block A is tied to string 1, which runs over a fixed pulley at the table edge and ends at the centre of the movable pulley P — hence A's acceleration equals P's acceleration. Then P's acceleration is the average of B's and C's, because P carries the second string's two strands equally.
Formula String 1 (A → fixed pulley → centre of P), one strand each end: a_A = a_P (m/s²). String 2 (over P, with B and C hanging): a_P = (a_B + a_C) / 2, taking the same downward-positive sign for a_B, a_C and a_P. This is the standard "movable pulley moves at the mean of its two loads" result: the two segment lengths from P down to B and to C must add to a constant, so 2a_P = a_B + a_C, m/s² throughout ✓.
Solution
1. Fix a sign convention: take downward as positive for every vertical quantity.Mixing signs is the only real danger here; declaring the convention up front removes it.
2. Read the figure's data with that convention: C accelerates down at 2 m/s² ⇒ a_C = +2 m/s². B accelerates up at 1 m/s² ⇒ a_B = −1 m/s².
3. String 2 runs over pulley P: the length from P down to B plus the length from P down to C is constant, so 2 a_P = a_B + a_C.
4. Substitute: 2 a_P = (−1) + (+2) = +1 m/s² ⇒ a_P = +0.5 m/s² (i.e. pulley P accelerates 0.5 m/s² downward).B and C are not symmetric — C wins by 1 m/s² — so P drifts down at half that difference.
5. String 1 has A on one end and P's axle on the other, with just one fixed pulley between them and one strand on each body: whatever length P gains going down, A loses going right ⇒ a_A = a_P = 0.5 m/s².
6. Direction: P moves down, so A is dragged horizontally toward the pulley at the table edge, at 0.5 m/s².

Trap: adding 1 + 2 = 3 and halving to 1.5 m/s² — that ignores that B goes up while C goes down, so they partly cancel.

3Figure shows a uniform rod of length 30 cm, mass 3.0 kg. The strings are pulled by constant forces 20 N and 32 N. Find the force exerted by the 20 cm part of the rod on the 10 cm part (in Newton). All surfaces smooth; strings and pulleys light.Force between parts of a pulled rodUniform rod 30 cm, 3 kg, 20 N & 32 N — force by 20 cm part on 10 cm part🟢 Exact24 N
Concept A rigid rod pulled at its ends by unequal forces accelerates as one unit, but every cross-section must transmit a force to drag the material behind it along. To find the force at the 10 cm mark, mentally cut the rod there and apply Newton's second law to one piece — the internal force then appears as an ordinary external pull on that piece. It differs at every cross-section, growing from 20 N at one end to 32 N at the other.
Formula Whole rod: a = Fnet / M — a in m/s², forces in N, M in kg. Cut piece (10 cm next to the 20 N string): F − 20 = m₁₀ a, m₁₀ = M × 10/30. Uniform rod ⇒ λ = 3.0/0.30 = 10 kg/m; each length carries its proportional mass.
Solution
1. The strings pull the ends in opposite directions (over light pulleys): Fnet = 32 − 20 = 12 N, toward the 32 N string.All surfaces smooth ⇒ no friction; the pulleys only redirect the string forces.
2. Every point of the rigid rod: a = Fnet/M = 12/3.0 = 4 m/s², toward the 32 N side.
3. Mass of the 10 cm piece adjacent to the 20 N string: m₁₀ = 3.0 × 10/30 = 1.0 kg.
4. Free-body of that piece: 20 N backward, internal force F forward across the cut: F − 20 = 1.0 × 4 ⇒ F = 24 N.
5. Cross-check via the 20 cm part (2.0 kg): 32 − F = 2.0 × 4 ⇒ F = 24 N ✓.By Newton's third law the two cuts must agree — catches arithmetic slips instantly.
  20 N ◂──[10 cm | 1.0 kg]──F──[20 cm | 2.0 kg]──▸ 32 N
                a = (32−20)/3.0 = 4 m/s²  (toward 32 N)
  10 cm piece:  F − 20 = 1.0×4   ⇒  F = 24 N   <-- answer
  20 cm piece:  32 − F = 2.0×4   ⇒  F = 24 N   ✓ (third-law check)
⚡ JEE Trick The internal force rises linearly: at a cut x from the 20 N end, F(x) = 20 + λx·a = 20 + 10(0.10)(4) = 24 N — one line, no FBD. Trap: averaging (20+32)/2 = 26 N — that holds only at the midpoint (15 cm), not at the 10 cm cut.
Answer: 24 N (force exerted by the 20 cm part on the 10 cm part).
4If the string is pulled down with a force 120 N as shown (movable-pulley system), then acceleration of the 8 kg block would be. (a) 10 (b) 5 (c) 0 (d) 4 m/s²Force on string → block accelerationMass–pulley system, string pulled by a force🟡 Similarb5 m/s²
Concept The key idea is that an ideal string has the same tension everywhere, and a massless pulley cannot carry net force — whatever the strands pull with must be exactly balanced by whatever is applied to its axle. So the applied 120 N first fixes the tension T via the left movable pulley, and that same T then reappears on the right movable pulley, which is what actually lifts the 8 kg block. The pulley system is a force multiplier: the block feels 2T, not T.
Formula Massless pulley: ΣF = 0 (N) on it, so for the left pulley 2T = F = 120 N ⇒ T in N. For the 8 kg block hanging from the right movable pulley (up positive): 2T − m g = m a — T in N, m in kg, g = 10 m/s², a in m/s². Every term is a force in newtons; dividing by m (kg) at the end gives N/kg = m/s² ✓.
Solution
1. Trace the string: it is fixed to the ceiling on the left, passes under the left movable pulley, up over the fixed pulley hanging from the ceiling, down under the right movable pulley, and back up to the ceiling. One string ⇒ one tension T (N).Ideal pulleys are frictionless and massless, so they do not change the tension — only its direction.
2. The 120 N is applied downward at the axle of the left movable pulley; that pulley is massless, so its two upward strands must balance it exactly:
  2T = 120 N ⇒ T = 60 N.A massless body with a non-zero net force would need infinite acceleration — impossible — so ΣF = 0 on it, whatever it is doing.
3. Now the right movable pulley: it is also held by two strands of the same string, so it is pulled up with
  2T = 2 × 60 = 120 N.
4. The 8 kg block hangs from that pulley. Its weight is
  m g = 8 kg × 10 m/s² = 80 N (down).Using the exam value g = 10 m/s², which the printed options are clearly built on.
5. Newton's second law on the (block + massless pulley), up positive:
  2T − m g = m a ⇒ 120 − 80 = 8 a ⇒ 40 N = 8 a
6. a = 40 / 8 = 5 m/s², directed upward (units: N/kg = m/s² ✓).

6If masses are released from the position shown, speed of mass m₁ just before it collides on the floor (m₁ > m₂). (a) [2m₁gd/(m₁+m₂)]^½ (b) [2(m₁−m₂)gd/(m₁+m₂)]^½ (c) [2(m₁−m₂)gd/m₁]^½ (d) NoneAtwood — speed before impactHanging Atwood machine — masses released from rest🟡 Similarb[2(m₁−m₂)gd/(m₁+m₂)]^½
Concept A standard Atwood machine: two masses over a light frictionless pulley, released from rest, with m₁ > m₂ so m₁ falls through the height d while m₂ rises through the same d. Because the string is inextensible the two speeds are always equal in magnitude, and because the pulley and string are ideal no mechanical energy is lost. That makes energy conservation the fastest route — though the Newtonian route (find a, then use v² = 2ad) gives the identical result, which is a useful cross-check.
Formula Energy method: loss in PE = gain in KE ⇒ (m₁ − m₂) g d = ½ (m₁ + m₂) v² — m in kg, g in m/s², d in m, v in m/s; every term is in joules. m₁ loses m₁gd of PE while m₂ gains m₂gd, so the net release is (m₁−m₂)gd; both masses end up moving at the same speed v, so the total KE is ½(m₁+m₂)v². Newton cross-check: a = (m₁ − m₂) g / (m₁ + m₂) and v² = 2 a d.
