1A body initially at rest explodes into three fragments. Two of the fragments have equal mass and fly off with equal speeds in opposite directions. The third fragment will: (a) remain at rest (b) start moving in the direction of either fragment (c) move perpendicular to the direction of motion of the other two (d) move with zero momentum — (Level-1)Explosion, momentum cancellation3 fragments after explosion ~ Momentum🟡 Similararemain at rest
Concept An explosion involves only internal forces between the fragments, so the total momentum of the system is exactly what it was the instant before the blast — here, zero, since the body starts at rest. Two of the three fragments are given to be a matched pair: equal mass, equal speed, exactly opposite directions. Their momenta are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign, so they cancel each other completely, leaving nothing for the third fragment to balance.
Formula p⃗_total,before = p⃗_total,after (no external force during the explosion). With the body at rest, p⃗_total,before = 0, so p⃗_1 + p⃗_2 + p⃗_3 = 0. Each p = mv has units kg·m/s, and the vector sum must equal the zero vector, not just zero magnitude — direction matters.
Solution
1. Let fragments 1 and 2 have equal mass m and equal speed v, moving in exactly opposite directions: p⃗_1 = mv (say, along +x), p⃗_2 = −mv (along −x).
2. Add these two: p⃗_1 + p⃗_2 = mv + (−mv) = 0.This is the whole point of specifying "equal mass, equal speed, opposite directions" — it is the recipe for two momenta that cancel exactly.
3. Substitute into the conservation equation: 0 + p⃗_3 = 0, so p⃗_3 = 0.
4. The third fragment has some non-zero mass m₃ (it is a real piece of the exploded body). Since p₃ = m₃v₃ = 0 and m₃ ≠ 0, it follows that v₃ = 0 — the third fragment does not move at all.
5. Check the options: (b) and (c) both require the third fragment to move, which is ruled out. (d) "moves with zero momentum" is only possible for a massive body if its velocity is zero — i.e. it is really describing the same physical state as (a), just less directly. The intended, standard answer is (a) remain at rest.Exam-setters phrase the correct option as "remains at rest" precisely because that is the physically meaningful description; a body with zero velocity is at rest, full stop.
   before:              after:
                          p1 = mv  ──▸
   (at rest)     ◂── p2 = −mv
                          p3 = 0  (fragment 3 sits still)

   p1 + p2 + p3 = mv − mv + 0 = 0  =  p_initial   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question gifts you a "matched pair" (equal mass, equal speed, opposite direction) among several fragments of a system that started at rest, that pair's momenta cancel — treat them as invisible and apply conservation to whatever is left. Trap: option (d) is a technically-true rewording of (a); pick the option phrased as the direct physical description the exam wants, not the more roundabout one.
Answer: (a) remain at rest.
2The area of F-t curve is A, where 'F' is the force on one mass due to the other. If one of the colliding bodies of mass M is at rest initially, then its speed just after the collision is _____. — (Level-2)Impulse = area under F-t graphThe area of F-t curve is A, where 'F' is the force on one mass due to the other...🟢 ExactA / M
Concept This question is really testing one geometric fact dressed up as a collision: the area under a force–time graph is the impulse, regardless of how jagged or smooth the F–t curve looks. You never need the exact shape of the curve or the collision duration — only its enclosed area, which is handed to you directly as A.
Formula Impulse–momentum theorem: J = ∫F dt = Δp, and the integral ∫F dt is by definition the area under the F–t curve. So Area = A = Δp = Mv − M(0) = Mv, with M in kg, v in m/s, A in N·s (= kg·m/s). N·s = (kg·m/s²)(s) = kg·m/s, exactly the unit of momentum — so equating A to Mv is dimensionally consistent.
Solution
1. Identify what the shaded area under the given F–t curve represents: by definition of impulse, Area = ∫F dt = J, the impulse delivered to the mass M during the collision.
2. Apply the impulse–momentum theorem to mass M: J = p_final − p_initial.
3. Since M starts at rest, p_initial = M × 0 = 0.
4. So J = p_final − 0 = Mv, where v is the speed of M immediately after the collision.
5. But J is exactly the area A given in the problem: A = Mv.
6. Solve for v: v = A / M.No information about the other colliding body, the contact time, or the peak force is needed — the area alone fixes the answer.

   │      ___
   │     /   \        Area under curve = A  =  impulse J
   │    /     \___
   │   /          \
   └──┴─────────────┴──▸ t
      collision starts   ends

   J = Δp = M v − M(0) = Mv  =  A   ⇒   v = A/M
⚡ JEE Trick Any time a question gives you "area under F–t" instead of a peak force and a time, it wants you to skip straight to impulse = Δp — do not try to reconstruct F(t) or the collision time; they are irrelevant once the area is known. Trap: writing v = A²/(2M) by confusing this with a work–energy (F–x area) problem — F–t area is momentum, F–x area is work/energy, and the two must never be swapped.
Answer: v = A / M.
3A cannon on friction-less ground fires a shell with muzzle velocity 'u' as shown (barrel at 60° to the ground, cannon mass M, shell mass m). Calculate the recoil speed of the cannon. — (Level-3)Horizontal momentum conservation, recoilRecoil Velocity🟡 Similarmu / (2M)
Concept The ground is frictionless, so no external horizontal force acts on the cannon+shell system — horizontal momentum is conserved even though the shell leaves at an angle and the vertical momentum is clearly not conserved (the ground supplies whatever vertical push is needed). Only the horizontal component of the shell's velocity, u cos60°, enters the recoil calculation; the vertical component u sin60° is irrelevant to the cannon's horizontal recoil.
Formula Horizontal momentum conservation: 0 = m(u cos60°) − MV — m, M in kg, u, V in m/s. Both terms are kg·m/s; the shell's forward horizontal momentum must be exactly cancelled by the cannon's backward momentum, since the system started at rest.
Solution
1. Before firing, the cannon+shell system is at rest: total horizontal momentum = 0.
2. Resolve the shell's muzzle velocity into components: horizontal = u cos60°, vertical = u sin60°. Only the horizontal part matters for recoil on frictionless ground.
3. After firing, the shell carries forward horizontal momentum m(u cos60°); let the cannon recoil backward with speed V, carrying momentum MV in the opposite direction.
4. Conserve horizontal momentum: 0 = m(u cos60°) − MV, so MV = mu cos60°.
5. Substitute cos60° = 1/2: MV = mu/2.
6. Solve for V: V = mu / (2M), directed horizontally backward (opposite to the shell's forward horizontal motion).The vertical momentum imbalance is absorbed by the normal reaction from the ground — the ground can push up or pull nothing, but it has no trouble supplying whatever vertical impulse is needed since the cannon never leaves it.

Trap: writing MV = mu (forgetting cos60°) overstates the recoil speed by a factor of 2.
Answer: Recoil speed of cannon V = mu/(2M).
4A man of mass 50 kg jumps off a 100 kg plank lying on a frictionless floor. His velocity w.r.t. the plank is 4 m/s. Calculate the recoil speed of the plank. — (Level-4)Relative velocity, momentum conservationMan and the plank problem🟡 Similar4/3 m/s ≈ 1.33 m/s
Concept The 4 m/s given is a relative velocity (man with respect to plank), not a ground-frame value — this is the single most common trap in "jump off a moving object" problems. You need two equations: momentum conservation (ground frame) and the relative-velocity constraint, solved together.
Formula Momentum conservation: 0 = m_man·v_man − m_plank·v_plank (man and plank recoil in opposite directions). Relative velocity: v_man + v_plank = 4 m/s (their speeds add because they move apart). Units: kg·m/s for momentum terms, m/s for velocities.
Solution
1. Let v_man = ground-frame speed of the man (jump direction), v_plank = ground-frame recoil speed of the plank (opposite direction). System starts at rest, so total momentum stays zero.
2. Momentum conservation: 50·v_man = 100·v_plank, so v_man = 2·v_plank.The lighter body (man) must move twice as fast as the heavier body (plank) for their momenta to balance.
3. Relative velocity condition: since man and plank separate, their ground-frame speeds add up to the given relative speed: v_man + v_plank = 4 m/s.
4. Substitute step 2 into step 3: 2v_plank + v_plank = 4, so 3v_plank = 4.
5. Solve: v_plank = 4/3 m/s ≈ 1.33 m/s.
6. Cross-check: v_man = 2 × 4/3 = 8/3 m/s ≈ 2.67 m/s. Momentum check: 50 × 8/3 = 400/3 ≈ 133.3 kg·m/s, and 100 × 4/3 = 400/3 ≈ 133.3 kg·m/s — equal and opposite ✓. Relative speed check: 8/3 + 4/3 = 12/3 = 4 m/s ✓.Both checks close exactly, confirming the algebra.
   before: man+plank at rest, total momentum = 0

   after:      v_man = 8/3 m/s ▸           ◂ v_plank = 4/3 m/s
              ┌───┐
              │man│                    ═══════════ plank (100 kg)
              └───┘  (50 kg)

   relative speed = 8/3 + 4/3 = 4 m/s  ✓     momenta: 50×8/3 = 100×4/3  ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a problem says "his velocity w.r.t. the X is v", write the relative-velocity equation FIRST (speeds add if separating, subtract if chasing), then bring in momentum conservation — solving them as a simultaneous pair avoids the common error of plugging the relative speed straight into p = mv as if it were a ground-frame value.
Answer: Recoil speed of plank = 4/3 m/s ≈ 1.33 m/s.
5A system of two blocks A (mass 3m) and B (mass m) are connected by an inextensible massless string over a massless, frictionless pulley. Initially the system is at rest. A bullet of mass 'm' moving with velocity 'u' hits block B and gets embedded into it. Find the impulse imparted by the tension force to the block of mass 3m. — (Level-5)Impulsive tension, pulley constraintImpulsive Tensions in strings | A system of two blocks A and B...🟢 Exact3mu / 5
Concept During the very short impact, only impulsive forces matter — the normal contact impulse between bullet and B, and the tension impulse in the string; gravity's impulse over that instant is negligible. The inextensible string over an ideal pulley forces block A and (block B + bullet) to move with the same speed after impact, one up and one down, so a single unknown V describes the whole post-impact motion.
Formula Impulse–momentum theorem applied separately to the bullet, to B, and to A (all masses in kg, velocities in m/s, impulses in N·s = kg·m/s): bullet: ∫N dt = m(u − V); block B: ∫(N − T) dt = mV; block A: ∫T dt = 3mV.
Solution
1. Let V be the common post-impact speed (B + embedded bullet move down with V; A moves up with V, by the string constraint over the pulley).
2. Bullet (mass m, decelerates from u to V due to contact impulse N from B): ∫N dt = m(u − V). — (i)
3. Block B (mass m, accelerates from 0 to V under contact impulse N pushing it and tension impulse T resisting): ∫N dt − ∫T dt = mV, i.e. ∫N dt = mV + ∫T dt. — (ii)
4. Block A (mass 3m, accelerates from 0 to V purely under the tension impulse, since it never touches the bullet): ∫T dt = 3mV. — (iii)
5. Substitute (iii) into (ii): ∫N dt = mV + 3mV = 4mV.
6. Equate this to (i): 4mV = m(u − V), so 4V = u − V, giving 5V = u, hence V = u/5.
7. Finally substitute back into (iii): ∫T dt = 3mV = 3m(u/5) = 3mu/5.This is exactly the impulse asked for — the tension impulse felt by the 3m block, directed upward (toward the pulley, the direction A actually accelerates).

             │     │
        T ▴  │     │  ▴ T
        ┌────┐     ┌────┐   ↓ bullet, mass m, speed u
        │ 3m │     │ m  │ ◂ (embeds into B)
        │ A  │     │ B  │
        └────┘     └────┘
         (up, V)     (down, V)

⚡ JEE Trick In pulley-constrained impact problems, write one impulse–momentum equation per body (never lump masses together across a pulley), use the string constraint to reduce everything to one unknown speed, then solve. Trap: forgetting that the contact impulse N is shared (Newton's third law) between the bullet and B, and appears with opposite signs in their two equations.
Answer: Impulse on the 3m block = 3mu/5 (directed toward the pulley).

1A particle at rest suddenly disintegrates into two particles of equal masses which start moving. The two fragments will: (a) move in the same direction with equal speeds (b) move in any direction with any speed (c) move in opposite directions with equal speeds (d) move in opposite directions with unequal speeds — (Level-1)Momentum conservation, equal-mass splitA particle of mass 4M at rest disintegrates into two particles of mass M and 3M...🟡 Similarcmove in opposite directions with equal speeds
Concept A particle "at rest" has zero momentum, and disintegration is powered by internal forces only — so the two fragments must carry away momenta that sum to the zero vector. With equal masses, this forces both a specific direction relationship (exactly opposite, not just "different") and a specific speed relationship (exactly equal).
Formula p⃗_1 + p⃗_2 = 0 ⇒ m v⃗_1 = −m v⃗_2 ⇒ v⃗_1 = −v⃗_2 (masses cancel since they're equal). Units: kg·m/s for each p, cancelling on both sides.
Solution
1. Write momentum conservation for the disintegration: initial momentum (particle at rest) = 0, so final total momentum must also be 0.
2. p⃗_1 + p⃗_2 = 0 ⇒ m v⃗_1 + m v⃗_2 = 0 (equal masses m each).
3. Divide through by the common mass m: v⃗_1 = −v⃗_2.A negative vector means exactly opposite direction, same magnitude — not "roughly opposite" or "some angle apart".
4. Take magnitudes of both sides: |v⃗_1| = |−v⃗_2| = |v⃗_2|, i.e. the two speeds are numerically equal.
5. Combine steps 3 and 4: the fragments move in exactly opposite directions (step 3) with exactly equal speeds (step 4) — this is option (c).
6. Reject the others: (a) same direction would make p⃗_1 + p⃗_2 = 2mv⃗ ≠ 0 unless v = 0 (no motion, contradicting "start moving"); (b) is too vague — momentum conservation is a hard constraint, not "any" direction/speed; (d) unequal speeds is impossible once the masses are equal, since equal masses require equal |v| for the momenta to cancel.
   before:  ● (at rest, p = 0)

   after:   ◂── v1        v2 ──▸
            m               m
        (equal masses ⇒ v1 = v2 in magnitude, opposite in direction)

   m·v1 + m·v2(opposite sign) = 0   ✓ conserved
⚡ JEE Trick "At rest, disintegrates into N equal-mass pieces" is a signature phrase — for N = 2 it always gives "opposite directions, equal speeds"; for N = 3 the answer instead constrains only the vector sum (as in the Suggested Problems' three-fragment question), so read the mass and count of pieces carefully before assuming the two-piece shortcut applies.
Answer: (c) move in opposite directions with equal speeds.
2A shell explodes and many pieces fly off in different directions. Which of the following is conserved? (a) Kinetic energy (b) Momentum (c) Neither momentum nor KE (d) Momentum and KE — (Level-1)Explosion: momentum conserved, KE is notConservation of Momentum: Explosion Problem II🟡 SimilarbMomentum
Concept The explosion forces (chemical, from the shell's charge) are entirely internal to the shell — they act between the pieces of the shell itself, not on the shell from the outside. Internal forces can never change the system's total momentum, no matter how violent the explosion. But they very much CAN change kinetic energy: the chemical energy of the explosive is converted into extra KE of the fragments, so KE actually increases.
Formula Newton's third law applied pairwise inside the shell ⇒ Σ F⃗_internal = 0 ⇒ dp⃗_total/dt = 0 ⇒ p⃗_total = constant. No equivalent law protects KE — it is a scalar with no corresponding conservation from internal forces alone.
Solution
1. Identify the forces at play: the explosion is driven by forces the pieces of the shell exert on each other (internal), not by any outside agent.
2. Newton's third law guarantees every internal force has an equal-and-opposite partner, so they cancel in pairs when summed over the whole system.
3. Since the net external force is zero throughout the explosion, dp⃗_total/dt = 0, i.e. total momentum is conserved — this rules in option involving momentum.
4. Kinetic energy has no such protection: the explosive's stored chemical energy converts into kinetic energy of the fragments, so total KE strictly increases (this is the whole point of an explosive — it is designed to release energy).
5. Therefore momentum is conserved but KE is not — matching option (b) Momentum alone, and ruling out (a), (c), and (d).
   before:  ●  (shell, momentum P, KE = K0)

   after:      ╲   │   ╱
                ╲  │  ╱   fragments fly off every which way
             ────●────
                ╱  │  ╲
                   fragment momenta sum to P (unchanged)
                   fragment KE sum  >  K0   (explosive energy added)
⚡ JEE Trick "Explosion" and "perfectly inelastic collision" are opposite bookends of the same idea: explosion = momentum conserved, KE increases (energy released); inelastic collision = momentum conserved, KE decreases (energy absorbed/lost as heat/sound/deformation). Elastic collision is the only case where both are conserved. Trap: assuming "conserved" must mean "everything is conserved" — always ask separately about momentum and KE.
Answer: (b) Momentum.
3Internal forces can change: (a) The linear momentum but not the kinetic energy (b) The kinetic energy but not the linear momentum (c) Linear momentum as well as kinetic energy (d) Neither the linear momentum nor the kinetic energy — (Level-1)Internal forces: KE yes, momentum noInternal forces can change (a) the linear momentum but not the kinetic energy (b) the kinetic ene...🟢 ExactbThe kinetic energy but not the linear momentum
Concept This is the general-purpose version of the previous question — instead of just an explosion, it asks about internal forces in general (springs releasing, muscles, chemical reactions, friction between parts of the same system). Newton's third law makes internal forces cancel in pairs for momentum bookkeeping, but there is no such cancellation for kinetic energy — internal forces routinely convert stored energy into KE or drain KE into heat.
Formula Σ F⃗_internal,pairs = F⃗_12 + F⃗_21 = F⃗_12 − F⃗_12 = 0 (Newton's third law) ⇒ total momentum p⃗ is unaffected by internal forces. But work done by internal forces, W_internal = ∫F⃗_internal · dr⃗, is generally non-zero because the two bodies can move through different displacements — so KE = ΣW can change.
Solution
1. Take any pair of particles i, j inside a system. By Newton's third law, F⃗_ij = −F⃗_ji at every instant.
2. Summed over the whole system, all such internal-force pairs cancel exactly: Σ F⃗_internal = 0.
3. Since dp⃗_total/dt = Σ F⃗_external + Σ F⃗_internal = Σ F⃗_external + 0, internal forces alone (no external force) leave p⃗_total unchanged — momentum is protected.
4. Now consider work: W_ij = F⃗_ij · dr⃗_i and W_ji = F⃗_ji · dr⃗_j = −F⃗_ij · dr⃗_j. Adding: W_ij + W_ji = F⃗_ij · (dr⃗_i − dr⃗_j), which is generally non-zero because particle i and particle j don't move by the same displacement.
5. So internal forces CAN do net work on the system, changing its total KE — e.g. a compressed spring inside a system releases and speeds up both halves; an explosive's chemical energy becomes fragment KE (previous question); friction inside a system converts KE to heat.
6. Combine: internal forces change KE but never change total linear momentum — matching (b).
   internal force pair:  F_ij ◂──●───▸ F_ji = −F_ij
                              i     j
                          dr_i      dr_j  (generally dr_i ≠ dr_j)

   momentum:  F_ij + F_ji = 0                → p_total unchanged
   work:      F_ij·dr_i + F_ji·dr_j ≠ 0       → KE_total CAN change
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise this as a pair rule: internal forces are momentum-neutral but not energy-neutral, always. It settles "spring release", "explosion", "man walks on a cart", "muscles contract" questions instantly — momentum of the isolated system stays fixed, KE is fair game to change.
Answer: (b) The kinetic energy but not the linear momentum.
4A body at rest breaks into two pieces of equal masses. The parts will move: (a) in same direction (b) a long different lines (c) in opposite directions with equal speeds (d) in opposite directions with unequal speeds — (Level-1)Momentum conservation, equal-mass splitA body at rest breaks into two pieces of equal masses. Concept This is the same physics as the disintegration question earlier in this level, just reworded — "breaks into two pieces of equal masses" is identical to "disintegrates into two equal-mass particles". Momentum conservation from rest, combined with equal masses, forces the pieces apart in exactly opposite directions at exactly equal speeds. It is worth recognising this repeated pattern instantly rather than re-deriving it each time.
Formula m v⃗_1 + m v⃗_2 = 0 (initial momentum zero, equal masses m) ⇒ v⃗_1 = −v⃗_2. Units: kg·m/s cancel identically on both sides, leaving a pure vector equation for v⃗_1 and v⃗_2.
Solution
1. The body starts at rest, so total momentum before breaking = 0⃗.
2. No external force acts during the (presumably brief, internal) breaking process, so total momentum after must also be 0⃗: m v⃗_1 + m v⃗_2 = 0⃗.
3. Since both pieces have the same mass m, divide through: v⃗_1 = −v⃗_2.
4. A vector equality v⃗_1 = −v⃗_2 means: same magnitude (|v⃗_1| = |v⃗_2|) and exactly opposite direction (180° apart) — nothing looser is allowed.
5. Eliminate the distractors: (a) same direction would give total momentum 2mv ≠ 0 (impossible unless both are at rest, contradicting "the parts will move"); (b) "along different lines" is vague and doesn't follow from the physics — the pieces must move along the SAME line (opposite senses), not different lines; (d) unequal speeds would break the momentum balance since the masses are equal.
6. Only (c) — opposite directions, equal speeds — survives all three checks.
   before:  ⬤  (mass 2m, at rest)

   after:   ◂── v ──┤  piece 1 (m)      piece 2 (m)  ├── v ──▸
                     splits along a single straight line

   m·v (left) + m·v (right, opposite sign) = 0   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick "At rest, breaks/disintegrates/explodes into two EQUAL masses" is a fixed template — the answer is always "opposite directions, equal speeds", every single time, regardless of how the question dresses up the scenario (body, particle, bomb, nucleus). Spend zero time re-deriving it once you spot the template; just verify the masses really are stated equal before applying it.
Answer: (c) in opposite directions with equal speeds.
5A bullet in motion hits and gets embedded in a solid block resting on a frictionless table. Which of the following is conserved? (a) Momentum and KE (b) Kinetic energy alone (c) Neither KE nor momentum (d) Momentum alone — (Level-1)Perfectly inelastic collisionA bullet in motion hits and gets embedded in a solid block resting on a frictionless table. What...🟢 ExactdMomentum alone
Concept "Gets embedded" is the standard signature of a perfectly inelastic collision — the bullet and block move off together with one common velocity after impact. In every collision with no external horizontal force (here, the table is frictionless), momentum is conserved regardless of type. But kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions; embedding is the most inelastic case possible, so KE loss here is maximal, not zero.
Formula Momentum conservation (always, frictionless table ⇒ no external horizontal force): m_bullet·u = (m_bullet + m_block)·v. KE before/after: KE_i = ½m_bullet u², KE_f = ½(m_bullet+m_block)v², and one can show KE_f < KE_i whenever m_block > 0.
Solution
1. The table is frictionless, so the only horizontal forces during the impact are internal to the bullet–block system (their mutual contact force). External horizontal force = 0.
2. Zero net external force ⇒ total momentum is conserved throughout the collision, by Newton's second law applied to the whole system: dp⃗_total/dt = 0.
3. "Embedded" means the bullet does not bounce or pass through — it sticks, so bullet and block share one final common velocity v. This is the defining feature of a perfectly inelastic collision.
4. In any inelastic collision, kinetic energy converts partly into heat, sound, and permanent deformation as the bullet burrows into the block — this deformation work is exactly what "embedding" costs energetically.
5. Perfectly inelastic is the extreme case: the maximum possible KE is lost (subject to momentum conservation) while the two bodies still end up moving together. So KE is emphatically NOT conserved here.
6. Momentum conserved + KE not conserved ⇒ (d) Momentum alone.
   before:  u ▸ ●bullet          ▨▨▨ block (at rest)

   after:            ▨▨▨●▸ v   (bullet embedded, moving together)

   m_bullet·u = (m_bullet+m_block)·v     ✓ momentum conserved
   ½m_bullet u²  >  ½(m_bullet+m_block)v²   (KE lost to deformation/heat)
⚡ JEE Trick Keywords "embeds", "sticks", "coalesce", "come to rest together" all flag a perfectly inelastic collision: momentum conserved, KE conserved NEVER (unless one mass is literally zero). Contrast with "elastic collision" or "collide and separate" language, which needs an extra check before assuming KE conservation.
Answer: (d) Momentum alone.

Concept This is numerically the mirror image of the "+4ĵ" version of this same question in Level-3 — only the constant ĵ-component's SIGN differs (−4 here instead of +4), and since that component doesn't change with time either way, it cancels identically from the impulse calculation in both versions. Recognising two questions as "the same underlying computation" saves real time on an exam.
Formula J⃗ = m(v⃗(2) − v⃗(0)) — m in kg, v in m/s, J in kg·m/s (N·s).
Solution
1. Write the velocity function: v⃗(t) = 2t î − 4ĵ (m/s).
2. Evaluate at t = 0: v⃗(0) = 2(0)î − 4ĵ = 0î − 4ĵ m/s.
3. Evaluate at t = 2: v⃗(2) = 2(2)î − 4ĵ = 4î − 4ĵ m/s.
4. Find the change: Δv⃗ = v⃗(2) − v⃗(0) = (4î − 4ĵ) − (0î − 4ĵ) = 4î + 0ĵ m/s.The −4ĵ term is present at BOTH t=0 and t=2 with the exact same value, so it subtracts away to zero — its sign (positive or negative) never mattered for this calculation.
5. Multiply by mass: J⃗ = m Δv⃗ = 2 × (4î + 0ĵ) = 8î + 0ĵ kg·m/s.
6. Magnitude and direction: |J⃗| = 8 kg·m/s (N·s), directed entirely along +î; zero ĵ-component in the impulse.Same magnitude and direction as the earlier "+4ĵ" version — confirming that only the coefficient of t (here, 2) and the mass (2 kg) determine the answer, not the sign of the constant term.
   v(t) = 2t i − 4j  (m/s)

   t=0:  v = (0, −4)          t=2:  v = (4, −4)
                    Δv = (4, 0)   ← constant −4j cancels regardless of sign

   J = m·Δv = 2 × (4, 0) = (8, 0) kg·m/s   ⇒  |J| = 8 kg·m/s along +î
⚡ JEE Trick If a velocity-vector component has no "t" in it at all, it is CONSTANT and will always cancel out of any Δv calculation — its printed sign is a total red herring for impulse questions, though it would matter if the question instead asked for total kinetic energy or momentum at a specific instant. Spot this pattern to skip needless recomputation.
Answer: Impulse = 8 kg·m/s (N·s), directed along +î.
10The net force versus time graph of a rocket is shown in the figure (F rises linearly from 0 to 20 kN over t = 0 to 2 s, stays constant at 20 kN from t = 2 s to t = 10 s, then falls linearly from 20 kN to 0 from t = 10 s to t = 16 s). The mass of the rocket is 1200 kg. Calculate the velocity of the rocket 16 seconds after starting from rest. Neglect gravity. — (Level-5)Impulse = area under F-t graphThe net force versus time graph of a rocket is shown in figure The mass of the rocket is `1200kg...🟢 Exact200 m/s
Concept With gravity neglected and no other force mentioned, the ONLY force on the rocket is the one plotted — so total impulse equals the entire area under the F–t graph, and impulse equals momentum change directly (mass is constant, so Δp = mΔv). The graph is a trapezoid built from three simple pieces (rising triangle, flat rectangle, falling triangle), each of which has an elementary area formula.
Formula Impulse = ∫F dt = Area under F–t graph. Triangle area = ½×base×height; rectangle area = base×height. Then Δv = Impulse/m (mass constant, starting from rest).
Solution
1. Break the graph into three regions and compute each area (F in kN, t in s, so each area is in kN·s = 1000 N·s):   • 0 to 2 s (rising triangle, 0→20 kN): area = ½ × 2 × 20 = 20 kN·s.   • 2 to 10 s (flat rectangle at 20 kN): area = 8 × 20 = 160 kN·s.   • 10 to 16 s (falling triangle, 20→0 kN): area = ½ × 6 × 20 = 60 kN·s.
2. Total impulse = 20 + 160 + 60 = 240 kN·s.
3. Convert to SI: 240 kN·s = 240,000 N·s.
4. Apply the impulse–momentum theorem: since gravity is neglected and this is the only force, J = Δp = m·Δv (mass constant throughout, per the problem's assumptions).
5. Rocket starts from rest, so Δv is simply the final velocity: Δv = J/m = 240,000 / 1200.
6. Compute: 240,000/1200 = 200 m/s.Dividing by 1200 is easiest as 240,000/1200 = 2400/12 = 200 — a clean whole number, a good sign the trapezoid areas were split correctly.

Answer: Velocity of the rocket after 16 s = 200 m/s.
