1 (i)Fill in the blank with appropriate term.
The quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of the system by one degree is called ……… .		Definition of heat capacityHeat capacity | Thermodynamics | AP Chemistry | Khan Academy🟡 Similarheat capacity
Concept Different bodies warm up by different amounts when given the same heat. The property that measures how much heat a particular object needs for one degree of temperature rise is its heat capacity. It is an extensive quantity — it belongs to the whole body as it stands, so doubling the sample doubles it. That is exactly what the phrase "of the system" in the blank signals.
Key relation C = q/ΔT, with units J K−1. Rearranged, q = CΔT — the working equation for every calorimetry problem in this lecture. Note there is no mass or mole term: C already has the amount of substance baked into it.
Solution
1. Read what the blank is being defined by: "the quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of the system by one degree".
2. Note there is no "per gram" and no "per mole" in the wording. That rules out specific heat and molar heat capacity respectively.Those three definitions differ only by what they are normalised to — nothing, mass, or moles — so the give-away is always the phrase that follows "required to raise".
3. A quantity defined for the whole system, with no normalisation, is the heat capacity, symbol C.
4. Check the units: q in joules divided by ΔT in kelvin gives J K−1, with no mass or mole in the denominator. Consistent with an unnormalised quantity.
5. Confirm with a lecture example: a calorimeter is quoted as having a heat capacity of, say, 20.7 kJ K−1 — that is a property of that one apparatus, not of a gram or a mole of anything.
6. So the blank is filled by heat capacity (also called thermal capacity).
 quantity              per what?    symbol   units
 heat capacity         whole body   C        J/K
 specific heat         per gram     c or s   J/(g.K)
 molar heat capacity   per mole     C_m      J/(mol.K)
 relations: C = m x c = n x C_m
⚡ JEE Trick Read the denominator words before answering any heat-capacity definition question. "Of the system / of the body / of the calorimeter" ⇒ heat capacity; "per gram / of 1 g" ⇒ specific heat; "per mole / of 1 mol" ⇒ molar heat capacity. The three are linked by C = mc = nCm, which converts between them in one step.
Answer: heat capacity (thermal capacity), C = q/ΔT.

Concept This blank carries the words "per mole", which is the normalisation that turns heat capacity into an intensive property. Molar heat capacity is the heat needed to raise one mole of a substance by one degree, so it is a genuine property of the material rather than of the particular lump of it you happen to have.
Key relation Cm = q/(nΔT) = C/n, units J K−1 mol−1. For gases it splits into two versions depending on what is held constant, Cv,m and Cp,m, related by Mayer's Cp − Cv = R.
Solution
1. Identify the normalisation in the wording: "heat given per mole to the system for per unit rise in temperature".
2. "Per mole" plus "per unit rise in temperature" gives units of J mol−1 K−1 — the signature of molar heat capacity.
3. So the blank is molar heat capacity, written Cm (or Cv,m / Cp,m when the condition is specified).The subscript matters for gases: heating at constant volume puts every joule into internal energy, while heating at constant pressure must also pay for expansion work, so Cp,m > Cv,m.
4. Relate it back to the previous blank: Cm = C/n, so molar heat capacity is heat capacity divided by the number of moles.
5. Typical magnitudes to sanity-check against: for a monatomic ideal gas Cv,m = 3/2 R ≈ 12.5 J K−1 mol−1; for a diatomic gas 5/2 R ≈ 20.8.
6. Hence the answer is molar heat capacity.
 C_m = q / (n . dT)          units J/(mol.K)
 C_m = C / n                 (C = heat capacity)
 C_m = c x M            (c = sp. heat, M = molar mass)

 monatomic gas   Cv,m = 3/2 R   Cp,m = 5/2 R
 diatomic gas    Cv,m = 5/2 R   Cp,m = 7/2 R

1 (iii)Instrument used to measure heat of combustion of organic substance is ……… .Bomb calorimeterBomb Calorimeter & Tricks to solve Bomb Calorimeter questions easily🟡 Similarbomb calorimeter
Concept Burning an organic compound in oxygen releases a large amount of heat and usually changes the number of gas molecules. To capture all of that heat cleanly, the combustion is carried out inside a sealed, rigid steel vessel — the bomb — immersed in a known mass of water. Because the walls cannot move, no expansion work escapes, and every joule released shows up as a temperature rise.
Key relation Rigid vessel ⇒ ΔV = 0 ⇒ w = 0, so the first law reduces to qv = ΔU, measured as q = CcalΔT. This is why a bomb calorimeter gives ΔU directly, and why you must add ΔngRT afterwards to convert it to ΔH.
Solution
1. Identify the measurement: heat of combustion of an organic substance, i.e. a strongly exothermic reaction with oxygen.
2. Recognise the practical requirement: the sample must be burned completely in excess O2, and the products (CO2, H2O) must be contained.
3. The apparatus that does this is the bomb calorimeter — a thick-walled sealed steel container holding the sample and pressurised oxygen, sitting in a stirred water bath.The name comes from the vessel's ability to withstand the pressure surge when the sample ignites.
4. Note the thermodynamic consequence: rigid walls mean constant volume, so w = 0 and the measured heat is ΔU, not ΔH.
5. Contrast with the simple (coffee-cup / water) calorimeter, which is open to the atmosphere and therefore measures qp = ΔH.
6. So the blank is bomb calorimeter.
 bomb calorimeter     sealed, RIGID   V const   q = dU
 simple calorimeter   open to air     P const   q = dH

 working equation:  q = C_cal x dT
 convert:           dH = dU + dn(gas).R.T
⚡ JEE Trick The word "bomb" in a question is a hard signal for two things at once: ΔV = 0, w = 0 and q measured = ΔU. If the question then asks for ΔH, you must convert with ΔngRT — and if Δng happens to be zero (as for C + O2 → CO2), the two are numerically equal and no conversion is needed.
Answer: bomb calorimeter.
1 (iv)Molar heat capacity at constant pressure = Molar heat capacity at constant ……… + PΔV.Cp = Cv + PΔVThermodynaimcs 07 || Derivation of Cp - Cv = R , Mayer's relation important for School Exams ||🟡 Similarvolume
Concept Heat a gas at constant volume and every joule goes into internal energy, because the gas cannot push anything. Heat the same gas at constant pressure and it expands, so part of the heat is spent doing work on the surroundings — you must supply extra heat for the same temperature rise. That extra is exactly the expansion work term.
Key relation Cp = Cv + PΔV per degree, which for an ideal gas becomes Cp − Cv = R. Substituting PV = nRT for one mole and one kelvin gives PΔV = R, turning the general statement into Mayer's relation.
Solution
1. Read the printed identity: molar heat capacity at constant pressure = molar heat capacity at constant ______ + PΔV.
2. There are only two heat capacities in play for a gas, Cp and Cv. Since the left-hand side is already the constant-pressure one, the blank must be the other: volume.
3. Confirm it physically. At constant volume, qv = ΔU, so Cv accounts for internal energy alone.No boundary movement means no PΔV work, so nothing is lost to the surroundings.
4. At constant pressure the gas also expands, so qp = ΔU + PΔV. Per degree, that is Cp = Cv + PΔV — exactly the printed statement.
5. Evaluate the extra term for one mole of an ideal gas warmed by 1 K: PΔV = RΔT = R. Hence Cp − Cv = R, Mayer's relation.
6. So the blank is volume, and the identity is the origin of Cp > Cv for every gas.
 constant V:  q_v = dU            -> C_v
 constant P:  q_p = dU + P.dV     -> C_p
 subtract:    C_p - C_v = P.dV
 ideal gas, 1 mol, dT = 1 K:  P.dV = R
 therefore    C_p - C_v = R       (Mayer)
⚡ JEE Trick Cp is always the bigger of the two, for every substance — the gas has to pay for expansion on top of warming up. For solids and liquids ΔV is tiny, so Cp ≈ Cv and the distinction is usually dropped; it only matters for gases.
Answer: volume — Cp = Cv + PΔV, giving Cp − Cv = R for an ideal gas.
1 (v)For triatomic gases such as CO2, the ratio Cp/Cv is equal to ……… .γ for a triatomic gasThermodynamics 03 || Heat : Specific heat Capacities Of Gases : Cp and Cv JEE MAINS/NEET||🟡 Similar1.33 (4/3)
Concept The heat-capacity ratio γ = Cp/Cv follows from how many ways a molecule can store energy. Equipartition gives each active degree of freedom ½R per mole to Cv. A monatomic gas has 3 (translation only), a diatomic gas 5 (3 translation + 2 rotation), and the book treats a triatomic gas as having 6 (3 translation + 3 rotation), which is what sets its γ.
Key relation Cv = (f/2)R, Cp = Cv + R, so γ = 1 + 2/f. With f = 6 for a triatomic, γ = 1 + 2/6 = 1.33 — the number the book quotes.
Solution
1. Count the degrees of freedom the book assigns a triatomic molecule: 3 translational + 3 rotational = f = 6.
2. Compute Cv: Cv = (6/2)R = 3R.
3. Add R for the constant-pressure version: Cp = 3R + R = 4R.
4. Form the ratio: γ = Cp/Cv = 4R/3R = 4/3 = 1.33.Equivalently γ = 1 + 2/f = 1 + 2/6 = 1.33, which is faster once you have f.
5. Cross-check the pattern: monatomic f = 3 gives γ = 1.67; diatomic f = 5 gives γ = 1.40; triatomic f = 6 gives γ = 1.33. γ falls as the molecule gains ways to store energy.
6. A point worth knowing: CO2 is a linear triatomic, so strictly it has only 2 rotational degrees of freedom (f = 5, γ = 1.40); the value 1.33 belongs to a non-linear triatomic such as H2O. The book uses 1.33 as the standard triatomic figure, and that is the expected answer here.
 f    example        Cv      Cp      gamma = 1 + 2/f
 3    monatomic Ar   1.5R    2.5R    1.67
 5    diatomic  O2   2.5R    3.5R    1.40
 6    triatomic      3R      4R      1.33   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Learn the one-liner γ = 1 + 2/f and you never need to memorise the three ratios separately. Also bank the reverse use: a question that hands you γ is really handing you f, and hence Cv = R/(γ−1) — the form needed in the adiabatic problems later in this lecture.

1 (vi)Molar heat capacity of iron (at. mass 56) will be ……… times its specific heat.Molar heat capacity = specific heat × molar massHeat Capacity & Specific Heat🔴 Chemistry for Class 11🟡 Similar56
Concept Specific heat is quoted per gram; molar heat capacity is quoted per mole. One mole of iron weighs 56 g, so a mole contains 56 times as much material as a gram — and therefore needs 56 times as much heat for the same one-degree rise. The conversion factor between the two is simply the molar mass.
Key relation Cm = c × M, where c is the specific heat in J g−1K−1 and M the molar mass in g mol−1. Check the units: (J g−1K−1)(g mol−1) = J mol−1K−1 — the grams cancel exactly, which is why the factor is the molar mass and nothing else.
Solution
1. Write the two definitions side by side: c = q/(mΔT) per gram, and Cm = q/(nΔT) per mole.
2. Convert moles to grams using n = m/M, so Cm = qM/(mΔT) = (q/(mΔT)) × M = c × M.
3. Substitute the atomic mass of iron given in the question: M = 56 g mol−1.
4. Therefore Cm = 56 × c — the molar heat capacity is 56 times the specific heat.The blank asks for the multiplier, not for a value with units, so the answer is the bare number 56.
5. Sanity-check with real data: iron's specific heat is about 0.45 J g−1K−1, so Cm ≈ 0.45 × 56 = 25.2 J mol−1K−1.
6. That value is close to 3R = 24.9, which is the Dulong–Petit prediction for a solid element — a neat confirmation that the conversion was done correctly.

 C_m = q / (n . dT)     J/(mol.K)   per MOLE
 n = m/M  =>  C_m = c x M
 for iron, M = 56  =>  C_m = 56 x c    <-- answer 56

⚡ JEE Trick Dulong–Petit is a free sanity check on any solid-element heat-capacity question: specific heat × atomic mass ≈ 25 J mol−1K−1 (≈ 6.4 cal). It is also run in reverse in older problems to estimate an unknown atomic mass from a measured specific heat.
Answer: 56 times.

1The exchange of heat energy during chemical reaction at constant temperature and pressure occurs in form of: (Level-1)
(a) free energy   (b) internal energy   (c) enthalpy   (d) bond energy		qp = ΔHRelation between ∆H and ∆U|| Class 12th|| Chemical thermodynamics|| NEET||JEE.🟡 Similarcenthalpy
Concept Heat is not a state function on its own, but under a fixed constraint it becomes equal to the change in one. At constant volume the heat exchanged equals ΔU; at constant pressure — which is how virtually every reaction in an open flask runs — it equals ΔH. The question specifies constant temperature and pressure, so the heat appears as an enthalpy change.
Key relation H = U + PV, so at constant P, qp = ΔH. Enthalpy was defined precisely so that the awkward PΔV work term is absorbed into a state function, letting the measured heat of a constant-pressure reaction be quoted as a property of the reaction itself.
Solution
1. Identify the constraint in the stem: constant temperature and constant pressure.
2. Start from the first law, ΔU = q + w, with w = −PΔV for pressure–volume work only.
3. Rearrange: qp = ΔU + PΔV. At constant P this is exactly Δ(U + PV) = ΔH.The step only works because P is constant — otherwise PΔV could not be folded inside the difference.
4. So the heat exchanged is the enthalpy change — option (c).
5. Eliminate (b) internal energy: that is q at constant volume, the bomb-calorimeter case, not this one.
6. Eliminate (a) free energy and (d) bond energy: ΔG measures spontaneity (and equals the maximum non-PV work), while bond energy is a specific enthalpy term for breaking bonds — neither is the heat of a constant-pressure reaction.
 constraint        heat equals   name
 constant volume   q_v = dU      internal energy change
 constant pressure q_p = dH      ENTHALPY change   <-- (c)

 why: dU = q - P.dV  =>  q_p = dU + P.dV = d(U+PV) = dH
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the pair "constant volume → ΔU, constant pressure → ΔH". Almost every thermochemistry question opens by telling you which one applies — "in a bomb", "in a sealed rigid vessel" for the first; "in an open beaker", "at 1 atm" for the second — and that single reading decides which quantity you are being asked for.
Answer: (c) enthalpy.

3Which of the following is correct? (Level-3)
(a) CV = (∂U/∂T)P   (b) CP = (∂H/∂T)V   (c) CP − CV = R   (d) (∂U/∂V)T = −a/V2		Mayer's relation and the correct partial-derivative definitionsThermodynaimcs 07 || Derivation of Cp - Cv = R , Mayer's relation important for School Exams ||🟡 SimilarcC<sub>P</sub> − C<sub>V</sub> = R
Concept Three of the four options are standard relations with one detail sabotaged — usually the variable held constant in a partial derivative, or a sign. The safe method is to write out the correct form of each from memory and compare character by character.
Key relation The correct set is CV = (∂U/∂T)V, CP = (∂H/∂T)P, CP − CV = R (ideal gas), and for a van der Waals gas (∂U/∂V)T = a/V2. The subscript on a partial derivative is not decoration — it names what is held fixed, and swapping it changes the quantity entirely.
Solution
1. Test (a): CV = (∂U/∂T)P. Wrong — CV is defined at constant volume, so the subscript should be V, not P.
2. Test (b): CP = (∂H/∂T)V. Wrong for the mirror-image reason — CP is defined at constant pressure, so the subscript should be P.Options (a) and (b) are a matched pair of subscript swaps, a favourite examiner device.
3. Test (d): (∂U/∂V)T = −a/V2. For a van der Waals gas the internal pressure is +a/V2: pulling molecules apart against their mutual attraction raises the internal energy, so the derivative is positive. The minus sign makes this wrong.
4. Test (c): CP − CV = R. This is Mayer's relation, exact for an ideal gas, and it is stated correctly. Correct.
5. Confirm (c) from first principles: H = U + PV = U + nRT, so differentiating with respect to T for one mole gives CP = CV + R.
6. Only one option survives, so the answer is (c).

 (a)  Cv = (dU/dT)_P      (dU/dT)_V      wrong subscript
 (b)  Cp = (dH/dT)_V      (dH/dT)_P      wrong subscript

Concept "Water in equilibrium with ice" means the system is sitting exactly at the melting point, with both phases present. Add heat there and it does not raise the temperature at all — it converts ice into water while the thermometer stays pinned at 0°C. A finite heat input producing zero temperature rise makes the heat capacity blow up.
Key relation C = q/ΔT with ΔT = 0 and q ≠ 0, so C → ∞. During any phase change at constant pressure the added heat goes entirely into latent heat — breaking the intermolecular ordering — not into the kinetic energy that temperature measures.
Solution
1. Recognise the state: ice and water coexisting at constant pressure means the system is at the melting point, 0°C.
2. Recall the behaviour of a phase change: heat supplied is used as the enthalpy of fusion, converting solid to liquid at fixed temperature.
3. Substitute into the definition: C = q/ΔT. Here q is a real, positive quantity but ΔT = 0.Dividing a non-zero number by zero sends the ratio to infinity — the mathematical statement of "no amount of heat changes the temperature".
4. Therefore C = ∞, option (b).

6. Reject (c) and (d): 75.48 J K−1mol−1 is the ordinary molar heat capacity of liquid water away from any phase transition, planted as the tempting "remembered value". It does not apply while ice is still present.

Concept Enthalpy is a state function, so reversing a process reverses the sign of its ΔH without changing the magnitude. Vaporisation absorbs heat (ΔH positive); condensation, its exact reverse, releases the same amount (ΔH negative). The only other work is scaling the per-mole value to the 100 g actually condensed.
Key relation ΔHcondensation = −ΔHvaporisation and ΔHtotal = n × ΔHmolar. Enthalpy is extensive, so once you have the per-mole figure the total scales linearly with the amount — no other correction is needed at constant T and P.
Solution
1. Find the molar mass of ethyl alcohol, C2H5OH: 2(12) + 6(1) + 16 = 46 g mol−1.
2. Convert the mass to moles: n = 100/46 = 2.174 mol.
3. Fix the sign. Evaporation is given as +38.6 kJ mol−1 (heat absorbed); condensation is the reverse, so its molar enthalpy is −38.6 kJ mol−1.Condensing vapour to liquid re-forms the intermolecular attractions that vaporisation had to break, releasing that energy back to the surroundings.
4. Scale to the actual amount: ΔH = 2.174 × (−38.6) = −83.9 kJ.
5. Do the arithmetic in stages as a check: 2 × 38.6 = 77.2, and 0.174 × 38.6 = 6.72, giving 83.9 kJ in total. ✓

Concept The sign of ΔH is fixed by one convention: it is always measured from the system's point of view. Energy entering the system counts as positive, energy leaving counts as negative. For which reaction will ΔH = ΔU? Assume each reaction is carried out in an open container:
(a) 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)   (b) H2(g) + Br2(g) → 2HBr(g)   (c) C(s) + 2H2O(g) → 2H2(g) + CO2(g)   (d) PCl5(g) → PCl3(g) + Cl2(g)		ΔH = ΔU requires Δng = 0In which of the following Delta H = Delta U ? | CLASS 12 | THERMODYNAMICS | CHEMISTRY | Doubtnut🟢 ExactbH<sub>2</sub>(g) + Br<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2HBr(g)
Concept ΔH and ΔU differ by ΔngRT, so they are equal precisely when the reaction produces and consumes the same number of gaseous moles. The task is therefore a counting exercise across four equations — find the one with Δng = 0.
Key relation ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT, so ΔH = ΔU ⇔ Δng = 0. RT is never zero at any real temperature, so the only way to kill the correction term is to make Δng vanish.
Solution
1. Test (a) 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g): gaseous moles 2 out, 3 in, so Δng = −1. Not equal.
2. Test (b) H2(g) + Br2(g) → 2HBr(g): 2 out, 2 in, so Δng = 0. ΔH = ΔU. This is the answer.Both bromine and hydrogen are written as gases here; had Br2 been given as a liquid, Δng would be +1 and the equality would fail.
3. Test (c) C(s) + 2H2O(g) → 2H2(g) + CO2(g): 3 out, 2 in (the solid carbon does not count), so Δng = +1. Not equal.
4. Test (d) PCl5(g) → PCl3(g) + Cl2(g): 2 out, 1 in, so Δng = +1. Not equal.
5. Only (b) gives Δng = 0, so only there does the PΔV work vanish and ΔH coincide with ΔU.
6. Note the phrase "open container" simply confirms the reactions run at constant pressure, which is the condition under which the ΔH–ΔU distinction is meaningful at all.
