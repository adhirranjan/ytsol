1Paramagnetism is shown by the molecules which have (Level-1)Paramagnetism & unpaired e⁻Paramagnetism is shown by the molecules which have — solved🟢 Exactbunpaired electrons
Concept Magnetic behaviour in MO theory is decided by one count only: how many molecular orbitals end up singly occupied after you fill them by Aufbau + Hund. An electron is a tiny magnet; when two electrons share an orbital they must have opposite spins (Pauli), so their magnetic moments cancel exactly. Only an unpaired electron leaves a net spin magnetic moment, and a species with a net moment is dragged into a magnetic field — that is paramagnetism. If every MO is doubly occupied the moments cancel in pairs and the species is diamagnetic (weakly repelled).
Key relation μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons. n = 0 ⇒ μ = 0 ⇒ diamagnetic; n = 1 ⇒ μ = 1.73 BM; n = 2 ⇒ μ = 2.83 BM. So "paramagnetic" is exactly the statement n ≥ 1. Note that bond order does not enter this formula at all.
Solution
1. Fill the MOs of the species and count singly-occupied orbitals — that count is n.
2. n ≥ 1 ⇒ net magnetic moment ⇒ paramagnetic; this is option (b).
3. Eliminate (a) paired electrons — paired spins cancel, giving diamagnetism, the exact opposite.N₂ has all 14 electrons paired and is diamagnetic even though its bond order is a maximal 3.
4. Eliminate (c) lone pair of electrons — a lone pair is by definition a pair, so it contributes zero net moment.
5. Eliminate (d) bond order more than one — bond order measures strength, not spin. N₂ (BO 3) is diamagnetic while B₂ (BO 1) is paramagnetic, which kills any link between the two.

 N₂        π2p_x² π2p_y² σ2p_z²                           0          DIAMAGNETIC
 O₂        π*2p_x¹ π*2p_y¹  (Hund: one each)              2          PARAMAGNETIC
 B₂        π2p_x¹ π2p_y¹    (Hund: one each)              2          PARAMAGNETIC
 F₂        π*2p_x² π*2p_y²                                0          DIAMAGNETIC
 rule: paramagnetic  ⇔  n ≥ 1  ⇔  UNPAIRED electrons     <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Never reason about paramagnetism from the formula or the bond order — fill the MOs and look for a lone arrow. Fastest route: count total electrons, and remember that only the last, partly-filled degenerate set can carry unpaired spins (π2p or π*2p). Trap: option (d) is the classic bait — students link "more bonds ⇒ more magnetism", but N₂ (BO 3, diamagnetic) versus B₂ (BO 1, paramagnetic) shows the two properties are completely independent.
Answer: (b) unpaired electrons.

Concept O₂ is the showpiece of MO theory: valence-bond theory draws O=O with every electron paired and therefore wrongly predicts a diamagnetic molecule, whereas liquid oxygen visibly sticks to the poles of a magnet. MO theory gets it right because the last two of the 16 electrons are forced into the degenerate π*2p pair, and Hund's rule spreads them one per orbital with parallel spins. So exactly two molecular orbitals are singly occupied.
Key relation For O₂ and heavier (no s–p mixing): σ1s σ*1s σ2s σ*2s σ2p_z (π2p_x = π2p_y) (π*2p_x = π*2p_y) σ*2p_z the 2s–2p gap in O is 15.0 eV — far too large for mixing, so σ2p_z sits below the π2p pair.

4Which of the following best defines an antibonding molecular orbital, according to Molecular Orbital Theory? (Level-4)   (a) An orbital formed by constructive overlap of atomic orbitals, increasing stability.   (b) An orbital formed by destructive overlap of atomic orbitals, with a node between nuclei.   (c) An orbital that always contains paired electrons.   (d) An orbital that lowers the potential energy of the molecule.Antibonding MO definitionBonding vs antibonding molecular orbitals — LCAO, addition and subtraction of waves🟡 SimilarbAn orbital formed by destructive overlap of atomic orbitals, with a node between nuclei
Concept MOs are built by LCAO — linear combination of atomic orbitals — and because orbitals are waves, two atomic orbitals can be combined in exactly two ways: added (in phase) or subtracted (out of phase). Addition is constructive interference: the wave amplitudes reinforce between the nuclei, electron density piles up there, it screens the two nuclei from each other and the energy drops — that is the bonding MO. Subtraction is destructive interference: the amplitudes cancel, producing a nodal plane exactly between the nuclei where ψ² = 0, the nuclei repel each other more strongly and the energy rises — that is the antibonding MO.
Key relation ψbonding = ψ_A + ψ_B (no node between nuclei, energy lowered by Δ) and ψ*antibonding = ψ_A − ψ_B (one node between nuclei, energy raised by slightly more than Δ). n atomic orbitals always give n molecular orbitals — they are conserved, half bonding and half antibonding for a homonuclear pair.
Solution
1. The defining test for an antibonding MO is the node between the nuclei produced by out-of-phase (destructive) overlap — exactly what option (b) states.
2. (a) describes the bonding MO — constructive overlap, stabilisation. It is the opposite of what is asked.
3. (c) is false: occupancy has nothing to do with the label. π*2p_x¹π*2p_y¹ in O₂ is an antibonding set carrying unpaired electrons.
4. (d) is false: an antibonding MO raises the potential energy of the molecule; that is precisely why filling it cancels bonding and lowers bond order.
5. Note the asymmetry worth remembering: the antibonding level is destabilised slightly more than the bonding level is stabilised, which is why He₂ (σ1s²σ*1s²) is net repulsive rather than merely neutral.

 out of phase (−) ψ_A − ψ_B       CANCELLED (ψ² = 0)       YES     raised   → σ* , π*  (antibonding)

 bond order = (N_b − N_a)/2  ⇒ every antibonding electron subtracts ½ a bond
⚡ JEE Trick Read every option for the word node: "node between the nuclei" ⇒ antibonding, star (*) orbital, higher energy; "electron density between the nuclei" ⇒ bonding, no star, lower energy. Counting nodal planes also answers the standard follow-up: σ2s has 0, π2p has 1 (the plane containing the internuclear axis), π*2p has 2, σ*2p_z has 1 perpendicular node. Trap: option (c) sounds plausible because most filled antibonding orbitals are paired — but "always" is what makes it false.
Answer: (b) An orbital formed by destructive overlap of atomic orbitals, with a node between the nuclei.

(Level-5) [MULTIPLE CORRECT]   (a) The MO configuration of He₂ gives equal number of bonding and antibonding electrons.   (b) The bond order of He₂ is zero, so the molecule is unstable and does not exist.   (c) He atoms do not form any molecular orbitals.   (d) Formation of He₂ requires unpaired electrons, which He does not possess.He₂ — zero bond orderMO diagram for He₂ — does the molecule exist? bond order worked out🟢 Exacta, bequal bonding and antibonding electrons; bond order zero, so He₂ does not exist
Concept Two He atoms bring 4 electrons. LCAO of the two 1s orbitals produces exactly two MOs, σ1s and σ*1s, and 4 electrons fill both of them completely. The stabilisation gained by the two σ1s electrons is cancelled by the destabilisation of the two σ*1s electrons — in fact slightly over-cancelled, since the antibonding level rises more than the bonding level falls. Net result: bond order 0, no net attraction, and helium stays monoatomic. This is the cleanest demonstration that MO theory predicts non-existence as confidently as existence.
Key relation Bond order = ½(N_b − N_a). N_b > N_a ⇒ stable molecule; N_b = N_a ⇒ BO = 0 ⇒ molecule does not exist; N_b < N_a ⇒ negative BO, even more strongly repulsive.
Solution
1. He₂ has 2 × 2 = 4 electrons; MO configuration σ1s² σ*1s².
2. N_b = 2, N_a = 2, so bond order = ½(2 − 2) = 0. Both (a) — "equal bonding and antibonding electrons" — and (b) — "bond order zero, unstable" — are correct, and they are the same fact stated at two levels.
3. (c) is wrong: the 1s orbitals of the two He atoms do combine and genuinely form σ1s and σ*1s. MOs exist; they are simply all filled, so no net bonding results.Proof that MOs form: He₂⁺ (3 electrons, σ1s²σ*1s¹) has BO = ½ and is observed in discharge tubes.
4. (d) is wrong: bond formation in MO theory needs a net excess of bonding electrons, not unpaired electrons on the free atoms. H₂ forms from two atoms that do have unpaired electrons, but Li₂, C₂ and N₂ all form perfectly well without that being the criterion.
5. So the answer set is (a) and (b).

Concept In MOT the two oxygen atoms are not treated separately: all 16 electrons are poured into a single set of molecular orbitals belonging to the whole molecule, using Aufbau, Pauli and Hund exactly as for an atom. Because oxygen's 2s–2p energy gap is large (15.0 eV) there is no s–p mixing, so σ2pz lies below the degenerate π2p pair. The filling then leaves two electrons to share the two degenerate π*2p orbitals, and Hund's rule puts them in singly with parallel spins — which is the whole explanation of oxygen's paramagnetism, something Lewis and valence-bond structures cannot deliver.
Key relation Energy order for O₂ (and heavier): σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < σ2p_z < (π2p_x = π2p_y) < (π*2p_x = π*2p_y) < σ*2p_z; Bond order = ½(N_b − N_a); μ = √(n(n+2)) BM. "Doubly degenerate" means π2p_x and π2p_y have identical energy, so Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity governs their filling.

1. Each O atom is 1s²2s²2p⁴; the molecule therefore has 16 electrons to place.
2. Write the MO configuration: σ1s² σ*1s² σ2s² σ*2s² σ2p_z² (π2p_x² = π2p_y²) (π*2p_x¹ = π*2p_y¹).The final two electrons meet a degenerate antibonding pair, so they occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins rather than pairing up.

Concept Fluorine has the same MO ordering as oxygen (its 2s–2p gap is even larger, 20.5 eV, so no s–p mixing), but two extra electrons. Those two extra electrons complete the π*2p pair. Completing an antibonding set has two consequences at once: it wipes out the π contribution to bonding, leaving only the σ2pz bond (hence bond order 1, a weak single bond), and it pairs up every spin (hence diamagnetism). 3Explain covalent character of an ionic bond.Fajans' rulesFajans' rules — polarisation and covalent character in ionic compounds🟢 Exact—
Concept No bond is 100% ionic. When a cation sits next to an anion, the cation's positive charge pulls the anion's electron cloud towards itself — this distortion is called polarisation. Distorted electron density is shared electron density in the internuclear region, and shared density is covalent character. So the extent of covalent character in an "ionic" bond is just the extent to which the cation polarises the anion. Fajans' rules list the four structural factors that make this polarisation large.
Statement Fajans' rules — covalent character increases with (i) small cation, (ii) large anion, (iii) high charge on either ion, (iv) cation with pseudo–noble-gas (18 e⁻, ns²np⁶nd¹⁰) configuration. (i)–(iii) are captured by the cation's polarising power ∝ charge/(radius)², and by the anion's polarisability ∝ size and charge; (iv) works because d-electrons screen the nuclear charge poorly, so an 18-electron cation such as Cu⁺ polarises far more strongly than a same-sized 8-electron cation such as Na⁺.
Solution
1. Size of the cation: smaller cation ⇒ higher charge density ⇒ stronger polarising power ⇒ more covalent character.LiCl is appreciably covalent (soluble in organic solvents) whereas KCl is almost purely ionic, because Li⁺ (76 pm) is far smaller than K⁺ (138 pm).
2. Size of the anion: larger anion ⇒ its outer electrons are held loosely ⇒ more polarisable ⇒ more covalent character.Down the halide series AgF < AgCl < AgBr < AgI covalent character rises, which is why AgI is the least soluble and most coloured.
3. Charge on the ions: higher charge on either ion increases the electrostatic distortion.AlCl₃ (Al³⁺) is a covalent, low-melting, dimeric solid, while NaCl (Na⁺) is a high-melting ionic lattice.
4. Electronic configuration of the cation: for the same size and charge, a cation with an 18-electron shell polarises more than one with an 8-electron shell.Cu⁺ (3d¹⁰) and Na⁺ have nearly the same radius, yet CuCl is covalent and insoluble while NaCl is ionic and freely soluble.
5. Observable consequences of increasing covalent character: lower melting and boiling point, lower solubility in water but higher solubility in organic solvents, and deepening colour.

4Explain the phenomenon of resonance.ResonanceThe phenomenon of resonance — canonical forms, resonance hybrid and stabilisation🟢 Exact—
Concept Resonance is the admission that a single Lewis structure is sometimes not good enough. For species such as O₃, CO₃²⁻ and NO₃⁻ we can draw several equally valid Lewis structures that differ only in the placement of electrons, never in the positions of the nuclei. The real molecule is not any one of them, nor does it flip between them; it is a single unchanging structure — the resonance hybrid — that is a weighted blend of all of them. The consequence is that the π electrons are delocalised, which lowers the energy of the real molecule below that of any drawn form.
Definition & relations Canonical (resonating) structures must have the same skeleton, the same number of unpaired electrons, and comparable energies. Resonance energy = E(most stable canonical form) − E(actual hybrid) it is always positive: delocalisation stabilises. For benzene it is about 150 kJ mol⁻¹. Bond order = (total bonds between the two atoms in all canonical forms) ÷ (number of canonical forms).
Solution
1. Definition: when a molecule cannot be represented by one Lewis structure, it is described by two or more structures differing only in electron distribution; the molecule is the hybrid of them. This is resonance.
2. Conditions: all canonical forms must have identical atomic positions, the same number of paired/unpaired electrons, and roughly equal energy to contribute significantly.
3. Worked case, CO₃²⁻: three equivalent forms, each with one C=O and two C—O⁻. Total C—O bonds = 2 + 1 + 1 = 4 in every form, spread over 3 linkages ⇒ bond order = 4/3 = 1.33, and all three C—O bonds are found experimentally to be identical (129 pm) — which no single Lewis structure predicts.
4. Same for NO₃⁻: 4 bonds over 3 resonance forms ⇒ average N—O bond order 1.33, all three bonds equal.
5. Consequences: (i) bond lengths become equal and intermediate between single and double; (ii) the molecule is stabilised by the resonance energy; (iii) the hybrid is more stable the greater the number of contributing structures.

Concept H₂ is the simplest possible MO problem and the template for every other one. Concept Lithium is 1s²2s¹, so Li₂ has 6 electrons. Concept Degenerate means "identical in energy". In a diatomic molecule the internuclear axis (call it z) is a special direction, but the two directions perpendicular to it — x and y — are completely equivalent by symmetry. The 2px orbitals of the two atoms overlap sideways to give π2px and π*2px; the 2py orbitals do exactly the same thing rotated by 90°. Concept N₂ is the benchmark of the second period: it has the highest bond order (3), the largest bond energy (945 kJ mol⁻¹) and the shortest bond (110 pm) of any homonuclear diatomic in the row, which is exactly why atmospheric nitrogen is so inert. Nitrogen has a small 2s–2p gap (6.0 eV) so s–p mixing operates, putting the degenerate π2p pair below σ2pz. The order does not change the bond order here, but it does change which orbital is the HOMO — a favourite follow-up.
Formula BO = ½(N_b − N_a). Configuration of N₂: σ1s² σ*1s² σ2s² σ*2s² (π2p_x² = π2p_y²) σ2p_z². Bonding MOs: σ1s(2) + σ2s(2) + π2p(4) + σ2p_z(2) = 10. Antibonding MOs: σ*1s(2) + σ*2s(2) = 4.
Solution
1. Place all 14 electrons in the B/C/N ordering (π2p before σ2pz).
2. Count bonding electrons: N_b = 2 (σ1s) + 2 (σ2s) + 4 (π2p_x², π2p_y²) + 2 (σ2p_z) = 10.
3. Count antibonding electrons: N_a = 2 (σ*1s) + 2 (σ*2s) = 4. No π* orbital is occupied.
4. BO = ½(10 − 4) = ½ × 6 = 3 — a triple bond (one σ + two π), matching the Lewis structure N≡N. Option (b).
5. Magnetic check: every MO is doubly filled ⇒ n = 0 ⇒ N₂ is diamagnetic, and the distractors (a) 2, (c) 1 and (d) 0 correspond to O₂, F₂ and Be₂/Ne₂ respectively.

Trap: with N₂ the HOMO is σ2pz (not π2p) because of s–p mixing — that matters for questions about ionisation and about N₂⁺.
Answer: (b) 3.

Concept Going from N₂ to O₂ adds two electrons, and because all the bonding orbitals are already full those two electrons are forced into the antibonding π*2p pair. Two antibonding electrons subtract exactly one whole bond, dropping the order from 3 to 2. This is why oxygen has a double bond, a bond energy roughly half that of nitrogen, and a longer bond — and, because those two electrons go in singly by Hund's rule, why it is paramagnetic. Oxygen's 2s–2p gap is 15.0 eV, so there is no s–p mixing and σ2pz lies below π2p.
Formula BO = ½(N_b − N_a) with O₂ = σ1s² σ*1s² σ2s² σ*2s² σ2p_z² (π2p_x² = π2p_y²) (π*2p_x¹ = π*2p_y¹). Every additional pair of antibonding electrons reduces bond order by exactly 1, which is the fastest way to walk from N₂ to Ne₂.
Solution
1. Fill the 16 electrons in the O₂/F₂ ordering (σ2pz before π2p).
2. N_b = 2 (σ1s) + 2 (σ2s) + 2 (σ2p_z) + 4 (π2p) = 10.
3. N_a = 2 (σ*1s) + 2 (σ*2s) + 2 (π*2p_x¹ + π*2p_y¹) = 6.
4. BO = ½(10 − 6) = 2 ⇒ a double bond O=O. Option (a).
5. Bonus facts the examiner may chain on: n = 2 unpaired ⇒ paramagnetic, μ = 2.83 BM; bond energy 494 kJ mol⁻¹; bond length 121 pm — all consistent with BO 2 sitting between N₂ (3) and F₂ (1).

Concept B₂ has 10 electrons and is the smallest molecule in which the π2p orbitals are occupied. Boron's 2s–2p gap is only 4.5 eV, so s–p mixing is strong and the degenerate π2p pair sits below σ2pz. The last two electrons therefore enter the π2p pair singly (Hund), giving bond order 1 and two unpaired electrons. Remarkably, the single B—B bond in B₂ is made of two half π bonds rather than one σ bond — a direct, testable consequence of s–p mixing.
Formula B₂ = KK σ2s² σ*2s² (π2p_x¹ = π2p_y¹) where KK = σ1s² σ*1s²; BO = ½(6 − 4) = 1. Counting valence electrons only: 6 valence electrons, of which 4 are bonding (σ2s² and the two π2p) and 2 antibonding (σ*2s²) ⇒ ½(4 − 2) = 1.
Solution
1. Z(B) = 5 ⇒ B₂ has 10 electrons; the configuration is σ1s² σ*1s² σ2s² σ*2s² π2p_x¹ π2p_y¹.
2. N_b = 2 (σ1s) + 2 (σ2s) + 2 (the two single π2p electrons) = 6.
3. N_a = 2 (σ*1s) + 2 (σ*2s) = 4.
4. BO = ½(6 − 4) = 1, option (b) — B₂ exists and is a genuine, if weak, molecule.
5. Do not miss the magnetic consequence: the two π2p electrons are in separate degenerate orbitals with parallel spins ⇒ n = 2 ⇒ paramagnetic, which is the experimental proof that s–p mixing is real (without it B₂ would be σ2p_z² and diamagnetic).

Concept With only two MOs available (σ1s and σ*1s) and four electrons to place, He₂ fills both completely. Bonding and antibonding contributions cancel term for term, so the bond order is exactly zero and no molecule forms. This is MO theory's cleanest negative prediction, and it is why the noble gases are monoatomic. The nearby ions He₂⁺ and He₂⁻, each with bond order ½, do exist transiently — proof that the orbitals themselves are perfectly real.
Formula BO = ½(N_b − N_a) = ½(2 − 2) = 0 for He₂ = σ1s² σ*1s². Bond order zero (or negative) ⇒ the species does not exist; a fractional positive bond order means a weak bond, but the species does exist.
Solution
1. He is 1s², so He₂ carries 4 electrons and only two MOs are available.
2. Aufbau fills σ1s² first, then σ*1s² ⇒ configuration σ1s² σ*1s².
3. N_b = 2 and N_a = 2 ⇒ BO = ½(2 − 2) = 0. Option (b).
4. Interpret it: zero bond order means the stabilisation from σ1s is entirely cancelled, so helium remains monoatomic. Number of unpaired electrons is also 0.
5. Trace the distractors: (c) 0.5 belongs to He₂⁺ (3 electrons, σ1s²σ*1s¹) and to He₂⁻ (5 electrons); (a) 1 is H₂; (d) 2 would need N_b − N_a = 4, impossible with only 4 electrons in this two-orbital system.

4. Cross-check by isoelectronic reasoning: 14 electrons in a first-row diatomic framework ⇒ same configuration as N₂, CO and CN⁻, all of which have bond order 3 and are diamagnetic.

The nitrate ion has three equivalent canonical structures: in each one, nitrogen forms one N=O double bond and two N—O single bonds, and the double bond simply moves to a different oxygen. The real ion is the hybrid of all three, so each N—O linkage carries the same, fractional, bond order. Experimentally all three N—O bonds in NO₃⁻ are identical in length — direct confirmation of the hybrid picture.
Formula Bond order = (total number of bonds between the two atoms in all resonating forms) ÷ (total number of resonating forms), equivalently BO ∝ 1 / (number of resonating forms). 1Which of the following bond orders is indication of existence of a molecule?   (a) Zero bond order   (b) Negative bond order   (c) Positive bond order   (d) All of theseBond order & existenceWhat bond order actually tells you about whether a molecule can exist🟡 SimilarcPositive bond order
Concept Bond order is not just a label — it is a net energy balance. Every electron placed in a bonding MO lowers the total energy of the pair of atoms by roughly Δ, and every electron placed in an antibonding MO raises it by roughly Δ (in fact slightly more). The molecule can only survive if the bonding stabilisation beats the antibonding destabilisation, i.e. if Nb > Na. That single inequality is exactly what "positive bond order" encodes, which is why the book states it as rule (c) on the bond-order page.
Key relation BO = ½(Nb − Na). BO > 0 ⇒ net stabilisation ⇒ the molecule exists (it may be weak if BO is fractional, but it exists). BO = 0 ⇒ stabilisation exactly cancelled ⇒ no bond. BO < 0 ⇒ net destabilisation ⇒ the atoms are pushed apart, and in practice such a species is never even formed.
Solution
1. Translate each option into the Nb vs Na language before choosing.
2. (a) Zero bond order ⇒ Nb = Na. He₂, Be₂ and Ne₂ all sit here and none of them exists — so zero cannot be the indicator.
3. (b) Negative bond order ⇒ Na > Nb, an even worse case than (a); the book explicitly says bond order is "always positive" in practice because a negative value simply means no molecule.
4. (c) Positive bond order ⇒ Nb > Na ⇒ net bonding ⇒ the species exists. This covers integral values (H₂ = 1, O₂ = 2, N₂ = 3) and fractional ones (H₂⁺ = ½, He₂⁺ = ½, O₂⁻ = 1.5), which is the point the option is testing. Correct.

Concept Magnetic moment is a direct headcount of unpaired electrons, so this is really an MO-filling question wearing a magnetism costume. Superoxide, O₂⁻, has 17 electrons — one more than O₂. 3. Locate the unpaired electrons: only π*2py is singly occupied ⇒ n = 1. (In neutral O₂ both π* orbitals were singly occupied, n = 2.)

8[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following elucidate molecular orbital theory rightly?   (a) Number of MO's are equal to the number of AO's from which they are formed   (b) BMO's increase electron density between the nuclei   (c) ABMO's have a region of zero electron density   (d) Number of electrons in BMO's are more than in ABMO'sPostulates of MO theoryWhich of the following elucidate molecular orbital theory rightly? — solved🟢 Exacta, b, cMO count = AO count; BMOs concentrate density; ABMOs have a node
Concept MO theory is built on the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO). Combining two atomic orbitals produces exactly two molecular orbitals: the in-phase (additive) combination piles electron density between the nuclei and is bonding; the out-of-phase (subtractive) combination cancels the wavefunction midway, creating a nodal plane of zero electron density between the nuclei, and is antibonding. Statements (a), (b) and (c) are these three facts restated. Statement (d) is not a postulate at all — it is a property that happens to hold for molecules that exist, and fails for those that do not.
Key relation ψbonding = ψA + ψB and ψantibonding = ψA − ψB. Orbitals are conserved: N atomic orbitals in ⇒ N molecular orbitals out. The subtractive combination vanishes at the midpoint, which is precisely what a node is.
Solution
1. (a) Number of MOs = number of AOs combined. Two 1s orbitals give σ1s and σ*1s; two sets of three 2p orbitals give six MOs (σ2pz, π2px, π2py, σ*2pz, π*2px, π*2py). TRUE ✓
2. (b) Bonding MOs increase electron density between the nuclei — that build-up of negative charge between two positive nuclei is exactly what holds them together. TRUE ✓
3. (c) Antibonding MOs have a nodal plane between the nuclei where the electron density is zero, which is why the lecture defines an antibonding MO as "formed by destructive overlap of atomic orbitals, with a node between nuclei". TRUE ✓
4. (d) Not always. He₂ has 2 bonding and 2 antibonding electrons (equal, not more); H₂⁻ has 2 bonding and 1 antibonding, which is more, but F₂'s margin is only 10 vs 8. The statement describes a condition for existence, not a general rule of the theory — and every species with BO ≤ 0 violates it. FALSE ✗
5. Correct set: (a), (b) and (c).The distinction being tested is between what MO theory says (postulates, true always) and what MO theory predicts for stable molecules (Nb > Na, true only when the molecule exists).
