1The moon does not fall to Earth because: (A) The net force on it is zero (B) It is beyond the pull of Earth’s gravity (C) It is being pulled by the Sun and planets as well as by Earth (D) The statement is wrong: the moon is in a state of free fallFree fall / orbitWhy the moon does not fall to Earth — it is in free fall (orbit)🟡 SimilarDsimilarP6 · L7 · Level 2 · Q3
Concept Earth’s gravity does act on the moon — that pull is exactly what keeps it in orbit. An orbiting body is in a permanent state of free fall: it is continuously falling toward Earth, but its large tangential velocity means the curved Earth “falls away” underneath it at the same rate, so it never gets closer.
Formula Gravity supplies the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r. The force is non-zero and points toward Earth; it does not vanish, it curves the path.
Solution
1. (A) is false — the net force is the gravitational pull, which is not zero (else the moon would fly off in a straight line).
2. (B) is false — gravity has infinite range (1/r²), the moon is well within it.
3. (C) is a distractor — other bodies pull weakly, but they are not the reason it stays.
4. (D) is correct — the moon is genuinely falling toward Earth every instant; it just keeps missing.Same physics as an astronaut “floating” in the ISS: both are in free fall.
⚡ JEE Trick “Why doesn’t X fall?” for any satellite/moon → it is falling. Orbit = free fall + sideways speed. Reject any option claiming zero force or no gravity.
Answer: (D) — the moon is in a state of free fall.
2An astronaut floating weightlessly in space shakes a large iron block rapidly back and forth, and compares it to moving it on a smooth horizontal surface on Earth. She finds that: (A) the shaking costs no effort because the block has no weight (B) the shaking costs some effort but considerably less than on Earth (C) although weightless, the effort required is the same (D) the shaking costs more effort than on EarthInertia vs weightLaw of Inertia — Newton’s First Law🟡 SimilarCsimilarP6 · L1 · Level 1 · Q2
Concept Two different properties are being confused. Weight (mg) is the gravitational pull and vanishes in free-floating space. Inertia (mass m) is the resistance to a change in motion and is an intrinsic property that does not depend on gravity. Shaking = repeatedly accelerating the block, which fights inertia, not weight.
Formula F = ma — horizontal shaking. On Earth’s smooth surface, weight is balanced by the normal force, so the horizontal effort there is also just F = ma.
Solution
1. To shake the block with acceleration a you need force F = ma in both places.
2. m (the iron’s mass) is identical in space and on Earth.Mass is invariant; only weight changed.
3. On the smooth Earth surface, gravity acts vertically and is cancelled by the normal reaction — it never opposed the horizontal shake anyway.
4. Hence the effort to shake it is the same in both cases.
⚡ JEE Trick “Weightless” kills mg, never m. Any horizontal/shaking task is governed by inertia (mass) → unchanged in space. Vertical lifting would differ; shaking does not.
Answer: (C) — the effort is the same.
3A ball is thrown vertically upward. Observer A films the motion and plays the tape backwards (tape starts at the highest point and ends at the release point). Observer B watches from a frame moving at constant velocity equal to the ball’s initial velocity. Concept Acceleration under gravity is g downward. Two operations are tested: (i) time reversal (playing the tape backward) and (ii) viewing from an inertial frame (constant velocity). Both leave the acceleration unchanged and pointing down.
Formula a = d²x/dt². Under t → −t this second derivative is unchanged. In a frame moving at constant velocity, a′ = a. Velocity flips on reversal, but acceleration (an even function of t) does not.
Solution
1. Observer A (reversed tape): the reversed clip shows the ball descending from the top. Reversing time flips velocity but keeps a = −g. So A sees downward acceleration.
2. Observer B (constant-velocity frame): a frame moving uniformly is inertial; accelerations are identical to the ground frame → g downward.Only accelerating frames introduce a difference (pseudo-force); B’s frame does not accelerate.
3. Both A and B measure a downward acceleration.
⚡ JEE Trick Time-reversal keeps acceleration (even in t); constant-velocity boost keeps acceleration (Galilean invariance). Only the velocity looks different, never g.
Answer: (A) — both A and B.

5Ball A is released from rest when the spring is at its natural length. Ball A hangs over a pulley; the other side connects through a spring to block B (mass M) resting on the ground. For B to leave the ground at some instant, the minimum mass of A must be: (A) M/2 (B) M (C) 2M (D) A function of M and the spring constant kSpring–pulley (SHM)Block A (m) on a light spring, other end to block B (2m) via a string🟡 SimilarAsimilarP6 · L6 · Level 6 · Q8
Concept Released from the natural length, A performs SHM about its own equilibrium point. The spring’s maximum stretch is therefore twice the equilibrium extension. Block B lifts off the instant the spring’s upward pull equals Mg.
Formula Equilibrium of A: k x₀ = m₃g. Max stretch in SHM from natural length: xₓₐₓ = 2x₀. Lift-off of B: k xₓₐₓ = Mg. Starting at rest away from equilibrium, amplitude = distance to equilibrium, so it overshoots to double.
Solution
1. Let A have mass m₃. Its equilibrium extension: x₀ = m₃g/k.
2. From rest at natural length, A swings down to a maximum stretch xₓₐₓ = 2x₀ = 2m₃g/k.This is the key SHM fact — the spring force peaks at twice the static value.
3. Peak spring force on B = k xₓₐₓ = 2m₃g. For B to leave ground: 2m₃g ≥ Mg.
4. ⇒ m₃ ≥ M/2. Minimum mass of A = M/2.k cancels — the answer is independent of the spring constant, so (D) is wrong.
⚡ JEE Trick “Released from natural length” ⇒ max spring force = 2× the static force. Set 2m₃g = Mg → m₃ = M/2. The factor 2 (not energy integrals) is the whole trick.
Answer: (A) M/2.

10The linear momentum p vs time t curve for an object is an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve, steepest at t = t₀. Which is true? (A) Rate of change of momentum is highest at t = t₀ (B) Net force first increases then decreases (C) If it started from rest at t = 0, it has maximum velocity at t = t₀ (D) More than one option is trueMomentum–time graphMomentum vs time graph — net force from the slope🟡 SimilarDexactP6 · L1 · Level 5 · Q2
Concept The slope of a p–t graph is the net force (F = dp/dt). A sigmoid is steepest at its inflection point t₀, where the slope is maximal; before t₀ the slope grows, after t₀ it shrinks.
Formula F = dp/dt = slope; v = p/m. Velocity tracks momentum, so v is largest wherever p is largest — at the end of the rising curve, not at t₀.
Solution
1. (A): slope (= force = rate of change of p) is maximal at the inflection t₀. True.
2. (B): slope rises up to t₀ then falls, so net force first increases then decreases. True.
3. (C): p keeps increasing past t₀ (curve still rising), so v is not maximum at t₀. False.t₀ is where p rises fastest, not where p is largest.
4. Two statements (A and B) are true ⇒ option (D).
⚡ JEE Trick On a p–t graph: force = slope. Inflection = maximum force, not maximum momentum. Both “highest rate” and “force up-then-down” hold → “more than one true.”
Answer: (D) more than one option is true.
