Key Facts, Reactions & Formulas

Position of HAnomalous — resembles alkali metals (1s1, forms H+, +1 state, electropositive, reducing) and halogens (1 e- short of inert gas He, forms H-, -1 state, diatomic H2, non-metal). Placed separately — fits neither group fully.

★ Golden ruleEvery H2O2 numerical reduces to one chain: 10 mL of "x volume" → x·(10/1000) L of O2 at NTP, and 68 g H2O2 gives 22.4 L O2 (22.7 L at STP). From there get mass, g/L, %, N or M. Remember Vol = 5.6 N = 11.2 M.
💡 Reactivity ladderMetal + water: very active (Na, K, Ca) react with cold water; Zn, Mg, Al need boiling water/steam; Fe, Sn, Ni need red-hot steam; Cu, Ag, Au, Hg, Pt do not react. Metals above H in the electrochemical series liberate H2 from dilute acids; those below do not.
L-1

Position, Isotopes & Preparation of Dihydrogen

Position of hydrogen in the periodic table

Hydrogen is the first element; its position is debatable because it resembles both the alkali metals and the halogens, yet differs from both.

Resembles alkali metalsResembles halogens
1 electron in outermost shell (1s1, like Li 2s1, Na 3s1)1 electron short of nearest inert gas (He); like F 2s22p5
Electropositive; loses e- to form H+; +1 oxidation state (H+Cl-)Can gain e- to form H- (hydride); -1 oxidation state
Combines with non-metals to form oxides, halides, sulphides; strong reducing agent (Fe2O3+4H2→3Fe+4H2O); liberated at cathode on electrolysis of HCl; forms metallic hydrides with Na, CaDiatomic (H2), typical non-metal; ionisation enthalpy close to halogens (much higher than alkali metals); liberated at anode on electrolysis of fused NaH; forms covalent compounds (CH4, NH3); replacement reactions like halogens
💡 Why anomalousH is less electropositive than alkali metals and less electronegative than halogens. It has only 1 proton + 1 electron (no inner shell, no unshared pair in H2), its oxide (water) is neutral (alkali oxides basic, halogen oxides acidic), and H+ is extremely small — it never exists free but as hydronium H3O+ (hydrated as [H9O4]+). So it is placed separately.

⚠ TrapAll three isotopes have the same electronic configuration (1s1) and same chemical properties; they differ only in mass (hence in physical properties and in reaction rates — kinetic isotope effect). Tritium is widely used as a radioactive tracer.

Preparation of dihydrogen

  • From water: cold water + Na/K/Ca (2Na+2H2O→2NaOH+H2); boiling water + Zn/Mg/Al; steam + Fe/Sn/Ni (3Fe+4H2O →(1023–1073 K) Fe3O4+4H2). High-purity H2 by electrolysis of acidified/alkaline water (H2 at cathode, O2 at anode).
  • From alkalies: amphoteric metals react with hot NaOH — Zn+2NaOH →(Δ) Na2ZnO2+H2; 2Al+2NaOH+2H2O →(Δ) 2NaAlO2+3H2.
  • From acids: metals above H in the electrochemical series (Zn, Fe, Mg) + dil. HCl/H2SO4 → H2. Lab method: Zn + dil. H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2 (Woulfe bottle, collected over water on a beehive shelf).
  • Commercial sources: Bosch process / coal gasification — C(s)+H2O(g) →(1270 K, Ni) CO+H2 (water gas/syngas); water-gas shift CO+H2O →(673 K, FeCrO4) CO2+2H2. Steam reforming of hydrocarbons/natural gas: CH4+H2O →(1270 K, Ni) CO+3H2. Lane's process (steam over hot Fe, alternating oxidation/reduction stages).
    • Industrial split & by-products
    About 77% of industrial H2 comes from petrochemicals, 18% from coal, 4% from electrolysis, 1% from other sources. It is also a by-product of the chlor-alkali process (electrolysis of brine): at cathode 2H2O+2e-→H2+2OH-, at anode 2Cl-→Cl2+2e-; overall 2NaCl+2H2O→Cl2+H2+2NaOH.

    Properties & uses of dihydrogen

    • Physical: colourless, tasteless, odourless; lightest substance (1 L at NTP weighs 0.0899 g, density ≈ 1/14 of air); slightly soluble (non-polar); liquefies at low T / high P.
    • Chemical: neutral to litmus; highly combustible (2H2+O2→2H2O, ΔH = -285.9 kJ/mol) but not a supporter of combustion. With metals → ionic hydrides (H is -1): 2Na+H2→2NaH; with halogens → HX (reactivity F2>Cl2>Br2>I2); with N2 → NH3 (Haber, Fe+Mo); reduces metal oxides/ions (CuO+H2→Cu+H2O); hydrogenation of oils (Ni, 473 K) → vanaspati.
    • Uses: refining fuels, reducing agent for ore treatment, manufacture of NH3/urea/HCl/HNO3/methanol, hydro-cracking; clean fuel and fuel-cell feedstock.

    Concentrated H2SO4 cannot be used for drying H2. Why?

    Book Example · L-1
    Electrolysis of water for making H2 is carried out in presence of acid (H2SO4) or alkali (KOH), yet no SO42- or K+ is discharged. Why?
    Discharge depends on the discharge potential of each ion at the electrode.
    At the anode, SO42- has a higher discharge potential than OH-, so OH- is preferentially discharged → O2. At the cathode, K+ has a higher discharge potential than H+, so H+ is preferentially discharged → H2.
    Only H2 (cathode) and O2 (anode) are evolved; the acid/alkali merely raises conductivity

    Forms of Hydrogen & Hydrides

    Allotropic / special forms of hydrogen

    • Ortho & para hydrogen: depending on whether the two nuclear (proton) spins are parallel (ortho) or antiparallel (para). Ordinary hydrogen is a 3:1 ortho:para mixture at room temperature.
    • Nascent hydrogen: freshly produced "newly born" H (e.g. Zn+H2SO4→ZnSO4+2[H]); more reactive than ordinary H2 — reduces KClO3→KCl, AsCl3→AsH3.
    • Adsorbed / occluded hydrogen: metals (Pd, Pt, Au, Fe, Ni) adsorb large volumes (1 vol Pd adsorbs ~935 vol H2) — released on heating (occlusion, Graham).
    • Active & atomic hydrogen: ordinary H2 under silent electric discharge (>30 000 V) → active hydrogen; through a tungsten arc → atomic hydrogen (life ~0.3 s, strongly reducing).

    Hydrides — classification

    Binary compounds of H with other elements. Type depends on the electronegativity / bonding of the partner element.

    TypeFormed byKey points & examples
    Ionic / salineGroups 1 & 2 (except Be, Mg)Crystalline solids, high mp/bp, denser than parent metal, conduct in molten state (H-→anode). Thermal stability: LiH > NaH > KH > RbH > CsH. Prep: 2Na+H2→2NaH; Ca+H2→CaH2.
    Covalent / molecularp-block & some s-blockDiscrete molecules held by van der Waals forces (+ H-bonding) → low mp/bp, non-conducting. Sub-types: electron-deficient (B2H6, AlH3), electron-precise (CH4, SiH4, GeH4), electron-rich (NH3, H2O, HF — lone pairs).
    Metallic / interstitiald- and f-blockH in the interstices of the metal lattice; often non-stoichiometric (LaH2.87, TiH1.8, PdH0.7); hard, metallic lustre, conducting, magnetic.
    Complex / polymericGroups 1, 2, 13LiAlH4, NaBH4, Li[AlH4] — versatile reducing agents. Polymeric: (BeH2)n, (AlH3)n (3-centre-2-electron H bridges).
    💡 Trends in covalent hydridesThermal stability falls with metallic character of the central atom: NH3>PH3>AsH3>SbH3>BiH3, and CH4<NH3<H2O<HF. Lewis-base (lone-pair) character: NH3>H2O>HF (period 2) and NH3>PH3>AsH3>SbH3 (group 15).
    ⚠ Trap"Electron-rich" means extra lone pairs (NH3, H2O, HF), not extra bonds. "Electron-deficient" (B2H6) has fewer electrons than needed for normal 2-centre bonds. "Electron-precise" (CH4) has exactly enough.

    Book Example · L-2
    Find the nuclear spin of (i) ortho-hydrogen and (ii) para-hydrogen.

    (i) Ortho: (1/2) + (1/2) = 1. (ii) Para: (1/2) − (1/2) = 0.
    (i) Ortho nuclear spin = 1 · (ii) Para nuclear spin = 0
    Book Example · L-2
    What is the hybrid state of the oxygen atom in H2O and the shape of the molecule?
    O has 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs = 4 electron domains → sp3; two lone pairs compress the angle to 104.5°.
    O is sp3 hybridised; H2O is bent / V-shaped
    Book Example · L-2
    Which has higher density — water or ice?
    Ice has an open cage-like H-bonded lattice with vacant spaces; on melting these collapse and molecules pack closer.
    Water (density max at 4 °C); ice is less dense and floats
    L-3

    Water — Properties, Hardness & Softening

    Properties of pure water

    • Physical: colourless, tasteless, odourless; freezes at 0 °C, boils at 100 °C; max density 1.00 g cm-3 at 4 °C; polar, V-shaped, bond angle 104.5°; high dielectric constant (78.39) → excellent solvent for polar/ionic substances; poor conductor; strongly associated through H-bonding (high specific heat, latent heats).
    • Neutral & amphoteric: pH = 7, [H+] = [OH-] = 10-7 M at 25 °C. Acts as an acid with NH3 (H2O+NH3⇌NH4++OH-) and a base with H2S (H2O+H2S⇌H3O++HS-). Auto-ionisation: 2H2O⇌H3O++OH-.
  • Reactions: with active metals → H2; with acidic oxides → oxoacids (SO3+H2O→H2SO4, N2O5+H2O→2HNO3); with basic oxides → alkalies (CaO+H2O→Ca(OH)2); with hydrides/carbides/nitrides/phosphides → H2/C2H2/NH3/PH3; hydrolysis of salts and non-metal halides (PCl5+4H2O→H3PO4+5HCl); forms hydrates (CuSO4·5H2O).
    • 💡 Why ice floatsEach O is tetrahedrally bonded to 4 H (2 covalent + 2 H-bonds). The H-bonds are longer than covalent bonds, giving an open cage structure with vacant spaces → ice is less dense. On melting (0→4 °C) H-bonds break, molecules fill the holes, density rises, peaking at 4 °C. This keeps lakes liquid below a floating ice layer, protecting aquatic life.

    Hard & soft water

    Soft water lathers readily with soap; hard water forms an insoluble scum first because Ca2+/Mg2+ precipitate the soap (e.g. Ca2+ + 2C17H35COO- → (C17H35COO)2Ca↓), wasting soap.

    Type of hardnessCauseRemoval
    TemporaryBicarbonates of Ca & Mg: Ca(HCO3)2, Mg(HCO3)2Boiling: Ca(HCO3)2→CaCO3↓+H2O+CO2; Mg(HCO3)2→Mg(OH)2↓+2CO2. Clark's process (calculated Ca(OH)2): Ca(HCO3)2+Ca(OH)2→2CaCO3↓+2H2O.
    PermanentSulphates & chlorides of Ca & MgCannot be boiled off. Use water softeners: washing soda (Na2CO3), permutit (Na-zeolite, Na2Al2Si2O8·xH2O), Calgon Na2[Na4(PO3)6], ion-exchange resins.
    Water softeners in detail
    Washing soda: CaCl2+Na2CO3→CaCO3↓+2NaCl (removes both kinds). Permutit (Na-zeolite): Na2Al2Si2O8·xH2O + Ca2+ → CaAl2Si2O8·xH2O + 2Na+; regenerated with 1% NaCl brine. Calgon: 2CaSO4+Na2[Na4(PO3)6]→Na2[Ca2(PO3)6]+2Na2SO4 (boiler water). Ion-exchange resins: a cation exchanger (−SO3H / −COOH) plus an anion exchanger (amine-based) removes all soluble minerals → fully demineralised water (most efficient).
    ⚠ TrapAdd only the calculated amount of Ca(OH)2 in Clark's process — excess slaked lime itself introduces fresh (artificial) hardness.

    L-4

    Heavy Water & Hydrogen Peroxide

    Heavy water (D2O)

    • Chemically deuterium oxide; ordinary water contains traces of D2O. Prepared by prolonged electrolysis or fractional distillation of H2O (H2O electrolyses faster, enriching the residue in D2O).
    • Colourless, tasteless, odourless; all physical constants are higher than those of ordinary water.
    • Chemistry mirrors water with D in place of H: with Na → NaOD+D2; with SO3 → D2SO4; with CaC2 → Ca(OD)2+DC≡CD; with Mg3N2 → Mg(OD)2+ND3. Exchange with protic compounds (NaOH+D2O→NaOD+HDO).
    • Uses: neutron moderator in nuclear reactors, preparation of deuterium, tracer compound.

    Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

    • Structure: non-planar, open-book. Gas phase dihedral 111.5° (O-O-H 94.8°, O-O 147.5 pm, O-H 95.0 pm); solid (110 K) dihedral 90.2° due to H-bonding. O is sp3; oxidation state of O = -1.
    • Preparation: Na2O2+H2SO4 →(ice-cold) Na2SO4+H2O2; BaO2·8H2O+H2SO4 →(ice-cold) BaSO4+H2O2+8H2O; Mark's (Merck) process BaO2+CO2+H2O→BaCO3↓+H2O2. Convenient lab prep of (deuterated) peroxide uses peroxodisulphate K2S2O8.
    • Chemical properties: decomposes 2H2O2→2H2O+O2; weakly acidic (H2O2+2NaOH→Na2O2+2H2O); strong oxidising agent in both acidic and alkaline media (provides nascent [O]); also a reducing agent toward stronger oxidisers; bleaches via H2O2→H2O+[O].
    • Uses: antiseptic/germicide ("perhydrol"), restoring old paintings, lab oxidant, "antichlor" to remove traces of Cl2/hypochlorite.

    Strength of H2O2

    percentage & volume strength% strength = g of H2O2 per 100 mL · Volume strength = litres of O2 at NTP that 1 L of the solution gives on heating
    master relationsVolume strength = 5.6 × Normality = 11.2 × Molarity ; Eq. mass of H2O2 = 34/2 = 17 ; 68 g H2O2 → 22.4 L O2 (NTP)
    💡 "10 volume""10 volume" H2O2 gives 10 volumes of O2 per volume of solution. It equals 3.035% = 30.35 g/L = 1.785 N = 0.893 M. Example: 10 mL of a "20 volume" solution liberates 10 × 20 = 200 mL O2 at NTP.
    Worked examples from the module
    Book Example · L-4
    Calculate the strength (g/L) and normality of a 10 volume solution of H2O2.
    "10 volume" ⇒ 1 L gives 10 L O2 at NTP. From 2H2O2→2H2O+O2: 68 g H2O2 → 22.4 L O2.
    Mass giving 10 L O2 = (68/22.4) × 10 = 30.357 g ⇒ strength = 30.357 g/L.
    Normality = strength / eq. mass = 30.357 / 17 = 1.79 N.
    Strength ≈ 30.36 g/L; Normality ≈ 1.79 N (vol strength = 5.6 × 1.79 ≈ 10 ✓)
    Book Example · L-4
    Calculate the volume strength of a solution containing 30.36 g/L of H2O2.
    68 g H2O2 → 22.4 L O2 at NTP, so 30.36 g → (22.4/68) × 30.36 = 10 L O2.
    1 L of the solution therefore releases 10 L O2.
    Volume strength = 10 volume
    Book Example · L-4
    30 mL of H2O2, after acidification, needs 30 mL of N/10 KMnO4 for complete oxidation. Find the % and volume strength.
    N1V1 = N2V2 ⇒ N1 × 30 = (1/10) × 30 ⇒ N1 = 0.1 N.
    Strength = N × eq. mass = 0.1 × 17 = 1.7 g/L ⇒ % = (1.7/1000) × 100 = 0.17%.
    Volume strength = 5.6 × N = 5.6 × 0.1 = 0.56 volume.
    0.1 N · 0.17% · 0.56 volume
    Book Example · L-4
    0.5 L each of three H2O2 samples labelled 10 vol, 15 vol and 20 vol are mixed, then diluted with an equal volume of water. Find the strength of the resultant solution.
    Normality = (vol strength)/5.6. For 500 mL each: N1V1+N2V2+N3V3 = (10+15+20)/5.6 × 500 = (45 × 500)/5.6 meq.
    Total volume after dilution = 1500 + 1500 = 3000 mL ⇒ N4 = (45 × 500)/(5.6 × 3000) = 1.339 N.
    Volume strength = 5.6 × 1.339 = 7.5 (equivalently: mean 15 vol, halved on doubling the volume).
    Resultant ≈ 1.34 N ≡ 7.5 volume

    Calculation problems (Level-7)
    Strong Box · Level-7
    What mass of CaO is required to remove the hardness of 1000 L of water containing 1.62 g of calcium bicarbonate per litre?
    M(Ca(HCO3)2) = 40 + 2(1+12+48) = 162 g/mol ⇒ 1.62 g/L = 0.01 mol/L; in 1000 L = 10 mol.
    Clark: Ca(HCO3)2 + Ca(OH)2 → 2CaCO3 + 2H2O, and CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2, so 1 mol bicarbonate needs 1 mol CaO.
    Mass CaO = 10 mol × 56 g/mol = 560 g.
    560 g CaO
    Strong Box · Level-7
    100 mL of tap water containing Ca(HCO3)2 was titrated with N/50 HCl (methyl orange). 30 mL of HCl was required. Find the temporary hardness as parts of CaCO3 per 106 parts of water.
    Ca(HCO3)2 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + 2H2O + 2CO2; equivalents of HCl = equivalents of CaCO3.
    meq HCl = (1/50) × 30 = 0.6 meq ⇒ mass CaCO3 = 0.6 × 50 = 30 mg = 0.030 g in 100 g of water.
    ppm = (0.030/100) × 106 = 300.
    300 ppm (300 parts CaCO3 per 106 parts of water)
    Strong Box · Level-7
    25 mL of an H2O2 solution was added to excess acidified KI; the liberated iodine needed 20 mL of 0.1 N Na2S2O3. Find its normality, percentage and volume strength.
    meq Na2S2O3 = 20 × 0.1 = 2 meq = meq I2 = meq H2O2.
    N(H2O2) = 2/25 = 0.08 N ⇒ strength = 0.08 × 17 = 1.36 g/L ⇒ % = 0.136%.
    Volume strength = 5.6 × 0.08 = 0.448 volume.
    0.08 N · 0.136% · 0.448 volume

    Assertion A: PH3 has a lower boiling point than NH3. Reason R: In the liquid state NH3 is associated by van der Waals forces, but PH3 by hydrogen bonding. Choose the correct option.

    R is FALSE — it is reversed: NH3 is H-bonded (hence higher bp), PH3 only has weak van der Waals forces.

    Commercial H2: C(s)+H2O(g) →(T1) CO+H2, and CO(g)+H2O(g) →(T2, cat.) CO2+H2. How are T1, T2 related?
    Water-gas formation is strongly endothermic ⇒ needs high T (~1270 K). The exothermic water-gas shift is run at lower T (~673 K) over a catalyst.
    (d) T1 > T2

    But being light/Z = 1 is not why it is clean — the clean-fuel reason is that combustion yields only water.
    (b) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A

    Assertion A: Physical properties of the isotopes of hydrogen are different. Reason R: The mass difference between the isotopes of hydrogen is very large.
    A TRUE. R TRUE — D is 2× and T is 3× the mass of H, the largest relative mass difference of any element's isotopes, which is exactly why their physical properties differ.
    (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    Strong Box · PYQ · JEE Main 2023 (13 Apr, Sh-1)
    Statement I: Permutit process is more efficient than the synthetic-resin method for softening water. Statement II: The synthetic-resin method results in the formation of soluble sodium salts.
    Statement I FALSE — the ion-exchange (synthetic-resin) method removes all ions (demineralised water) and is the more efficient one.
    Statement II FALSE — it is the permutit (Na-zeolite) process that leaves sodium salts; the resin method gives pure water.
    (a) Both statements I and II are incorrect
    Strong Box · PYQ · JEE Main 2023 (13 Apr, Sh-2)
    Assertion A: Isotopes of hydrogen have almost the same chemical properties but differ in their rates of reaction. Reason R: Isotopes of hydrogen have different enthalpies of bond dissociation.
    A TRUE (same chemistry; D reacts slower — kinetic isotope effect). R TRUE (D–D bond is stronger than H–H).
    The different bond-dissociation enthalpy is precisely what changes the reaction rate, so R explains A.
    (b) Both A and R are correct and R is the correct explanation of A
    Strong Box · PYQ · JEE Main 2023 (30 Jan, Sh-2)
    The strength of a "50 volume" solution of H2O2 is ___ g/L (nearest integer). (M = 34 g/mol, molar volume at STP = 22.7 L.)

    Mass = (68/22.7) × 50 = 149.8 g/L ≈ 150.
