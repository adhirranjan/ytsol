1At the maximum point of a curve, the slope of the tangent is: (Zero) — (Level-1)Slope at maximaMinima, Maxima, Slope & tangent · Class 11 Maxima & Minima numericals🟢 ExactcZero
Concept A tangent points along the curve's instantaneous direction. Climbing toward a peak the curve rises (positive slope); just past the peak it falls (negative slope); at the summit the rise momentarily stops, so the tangent lies flat — horizontal. A horizontal line has slope zero, and slope is exactly what dy/dx measures, so dy/dx = 0 at every maximum (and every minimum). A flat tangent defines a stationary point; whether it is a max or min is settled afterwards by the second derivative.
Formula Slope of tangent m = dy/dx; stationary point dy/dx = 0; maximum confirmed by d²y/dx² < 0. A horizontal line makes 0° with the x-axis, and m = tan 0° = 0.
Solution
1. Take any smooth curve that has a peak, e.g. y = 6x − x², to watch the general rule work.
2. Slope function by the power rule: dy/dx = 6 − 2x.d(6x)/dx = 6, d(−x²)/dx = −2x.
3. At the peak the tangent is horizontal, so set the slope to zero: 6 − 2x = 0 ⇒ x = 3.
4. Slope there = 6 − 2(3) = 0 — a flat tangent, exactly as claimed.
5. Confirm it is a maximum: d²y/dx² = −2 < 0 ⇒ peak. The slope came out 0 independent of which curve we picked.

     /    \____/       \
⚡ JEE Trick "Maximum", "minimum", "turning point", "tangent horizontal" and "dy/dx = 0" are five names for one condition — spot any one and the rest come free. Trap: confusing the slope (which is 0) with the value of y (which is large) at the peak — the question asks for the slope of the tangent, not the height of the curve.
Answer: (c) Zero — the tangent is horizontal at a maximum, so dy/dx = 0.

The derivative here is a cubic that looks intimidating but factors cleanly. "Minimum value" means the smallest output f, not the location x — a common slip.
Formula Stationary points: f′(x) = 0; minimum test f″(x) > 0. Power rule d/dx xⁿ = n·xⁿ⁻¹. f(x) = x⁴ − x² − 2x + 6 is dimensionless throughout.
Solution

3Car velocity v = (2a − bx)x; find the maximum velocity — (Level-3)Maximise v(x)Maximum velocity from position function · Finding maximum velocity (method)🟡 Similara²/b
Concept Here velocity is given as a function of position x, not of time — so the maximum is found by differentiating with respect to x, not t. Expanding the bracket shows v(x) = 2ax − bx² is a downward-opening parabola in x (the −bx² term dominates for large x, and b > 0), so it must have exactly one interior maximum. The physics: the car speeds up while the 2a term wins, then the −bx² retardation term takes over and it slows down. Assumption: a and b are positive constants, and we look only at x ≥ 0 (distance from the starting point).
Formula Stationary point: dv/dx = 0; confirm a maximum with d²v/dx² < 0. Units: v is in m s⁻¹ and x in m, so the bracket (2a − bx) must carry s⁻¹ ⇒ [a] = s⁻¹ and [b] = m⁻¹ s⁻¹. This lets us dimension-check the final answer.
Solution
1. Expand the product so it can be differentiated term by term: v = (2a − bx)·x = 2ax − bx².2a·x = 2ax and (−bx)·x = −bx²; units m s⁻¹ throughout.
2. Differentiate with respect to x using the power rule: dv/dx = 2a − 2bx (units s⁻¹).d(2ax)/dx = 2a; d(−bx²)/dx = −2bx.
3. Set dv/dx = 0 for the stationary point: 2a − 2bx = 0 ⇒ 2bx = 2a ⇒ x = a/b.Units: (s⁻¹)/(m⁻¹ s⁻¹) = m ✓ — a genuine distance, and positive since a, b > 0.
4. Second-derivative test: d²v/dx² = d(2a − 2bx)/dx = −2b, which is negative because b > 0 ⇒ this stationary point is a maximum.
5. Substitute x = a/b back into v = 2ax − bx², keeping every factor: v_max = 2a·(a/b) − b·(a/b)².

  • Second term: b·(a²/b²) = a²/b

4Orifice range R = 2√(h(H−h)); find max range & corresponding h (R_max = H at h = H/2) — (Level-4)Maximise R(h)When is orifice range maximum? · Max range R — orifice at depth h🟢 ExactH, H/2
Concept Range from an orifice at depth h in a tank of liquid depth H is R = 2√(h(H − h)). Since the square root is an increasing function, R is largest exactly when the quantity inside it, the product h(H − h), is largest — so we can drop the root while optimising and restore it only at the end. Physically, drill too near the top and the jet is fast but has little height to fall; too near the bottom and it barely spurts — the sweet spot is the middle. Maximising √g(h) and g(h) give the same h because √ is monotonic increasing.
Formula R = 2√(h(H − h)); maximise the inside g(h) = hH − h² via g′(h) = 0, confirm with g″ < 0. H is a fixed tank depth (constant); h is the variable.
Solution
1. Expand the inside: g(h) = h(H − h) = hH − h².
2. Differentiate w.r.t. h: g′(h) = H − 2h.H is constant so d(hH)/dh = H; d(−h²)/dh = −2h.
3. Set g′ = 0: H − 2h = 0 ⇒ h = H/2 — drill at mid-depth.
4. Confirm a maximum: g″(h) = −2 < 0 ⇒ maximum (not minimum).
5. Value of the inside there: g(H/2) = (H/2)(H − H/2) = (H/2)(H/2) = H²/4.
6. Restore the root and factor 2: R_max = 2√(H²/4) = 2·(H/2) = H.√(H²/4) = H/2; the leading 2 cancels the ½, giving a clean R_max = H.
 g(h) = h(H-h)      symmetric parabola, zeros at h=0, h=H
   peak midway  ⇒  h = H/2 ,  g = H²/4
 R = 2√g  ⇒  R_max = 2·(H/2) = H     <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Two positive numbers with a fixed sum (here h and H − h sum to H) have the largest product when they are equal (AM–GM) ⇒ h = H − h ⇒ h = H/2 instantly, no calculus. Trap: forgetting the outside factor 2 and reporting R_max = H/2, or confusing the depth h = H/2 (where to drill) with the range R = H (how far the jet lands).
Answer: R_max = H, attained at h = H/2 (orifice at mid-depth).
5Ball in horizontal tube: R = x + √((u² − 2ax)·2H/g); find x for maximum R — (Level-5)Maximise R(x)Class 11 Maxima & Minima numericals · Maxima & Minima + Integration (Physics)🟡 Similaru²/(2a) − aH/g
Concept This is a trade-off problem. The ball travels a distance x inside the tube (that distance counts directly toward the range), but the uniform retardation a bleeds off speed, so it exits slower and its horizontal projectile flight from height H is shorter. Travel too little inside and you waste tube distance; travel too much and you leave almost stopped. The total range R(x) therefore has a genuine interior maximum, found by dR/dx = 0. The given R already bundles the physics: exit speed from v² = u² − 2ax, and fall time from H = ½gt² ⇒ t = √(2H/g).
Formula R(x) = x + √((u² − 2ax)·2H/g), all lengths in m. Inside the root: (m² s⁻²)·(m ÷ m s⁻²) = (m² s⁻²)(s²) = m², so the root gives m and adds legitimately to x ✓. Maximise with dR/dx = 0, using the chain rule d(√f)/dx = f′ ÷ (2√f). Assumptions: a > 0 uniform, ball leaves the tube horizontally, g downward, air resistance neglected.
Solution
1. Name the inside of the root: f(x) = (u² − 2ax)·(2H/g), which equals v²·(2H/g) where v is the exit speed (m²).
2. Differentiate f: df/dx = (−2a)·(2H/g) = −4aH/g.2H/g is a constant (units s²), so only the (u² − 2ax) factor differentiates; units m² per m = m ✓.
3. Apply the chain rule to R = x + √f: dR/dx = 1 + (df/dx) ÷ (2√f) = 1 − (4aH/g) ÷ (2√f) = 1 − 2aH/(g√f).Both terms are dimensionless, as dR/dx (m per m) must be.
4. Set dR/dx = 0: 1 = 2aH/(g√f) ⇒ √f = 2aH/g.Units of 2aH/g: (m s⁻²·m)÷(m s⁻²) = m ✓ — matches √f, which is a length.
5. Square both sides: f = 4a²H²/g², i.e. (u² − 2ax)·(2H/g) = 4a²H²/g².
6. Multiply both sides by g/(2H) to isolate the bracket: u² − 2ax = (4a²H²/g²)·(g/2H) = 2a²H/g.Units: (m² s⁻⁴·m²)÷(m² s⁻⁴) × … reduces to m² s⁻², matching u² ✓.
7. Solve for x: 2ax = u² − 2a²H/g ⇒ x = u²/(2a) − aH/g.Dimension check: u²/(2a) = (m² s⁻²)÷(m s⁻²) = m ✓; aH/g = (m s⁻²·m)÷(m s⁻²) = m ✓.
8. Confirm it is a maximum: as x increases, f decreases, so √f decreases and 2aH/(g√f) increases — hence dR/dx = 1 − 2aH/(g√f) is strictly decreasing (d²R/dx² < 0) ⇒ maximum, not minimum.

  u -->   retardation a      v = √(u²-2ax)
                       |                      \
                     H |                       \  projectile

⚡ JEE Trick Don't square the whole of R — that creates a cross-term and a mess. Instead isolate the root first (write R − x = √f), or differentiate directly and only square after setting dR/dx = 0, as done above. Sanity check the answer physically: a bigger retardation a shrinks u²/(2a) and enlarges aH/g, so the optimal x falls — exactly what you'd expect. Trap: if u²/(2a) < aH/g the formula gives x < 0, meaning the true optimum is the boundary x = 0 (don't enter the tube at all).
Answer: Key point: R is maximum at x = u²/(2a) − aH/g (metres), obtained from dR/dx = 0 ⇒ √((u² − 2ax)·2H/g) = 2aH/g; d²R/dx² < 0 confirms a maximum.

1The second derivative test for maxima requires: (d²y/dx² < 0)Second derivative testSecond Derivative Test — introduction · How to use the Second Derivative Test🟢 Exactbd²y/dx² < 0
Concept The second derivative measures concavity — how the slope itself is changing. At a maximum the curve is a hill: the slope goes from positive, through zero, to negative, i.e. it is decreasing, so its rate of change d²y/dx² is negative. That downward concavity (∩-shape) is the fingerprint of a maximum. A minimum is the mirror image (∪-shape) with d²y/dx² > 0. First derivative zero only says "stationary"; the sign of the second derivative decides max vs min.
Formula Second-derivative test: at a stationary point where dy/dx = 0, it is a maximum if d²y/dx² < 0 and a minimum if d²y/dx² > 0. Sign of d²y/dx² = sign of the curvature.
Solution
1. Model a hill with y = −x² (its peak is at x = 0).
2. Slope: dy/dx = −2x ⇒ zero at x = 0, so x = 0 is stationary.
3. Curvature: d²y/dx² = −2, which is < 0 everywhere ⇒ the test declares a maximum.
4. Cross-check the slope's behaviour: for x < 0 slope = −2x > 0 (rising), for x > 0 slope < 0 (falling) — the slope is decreasing, matching d²y/dx² < 0.
5. Hence "requires d²y/dx² < 0" is the correct maximum condition; d²y/dx² > 0 would instead flag a minimum.
 condition at a stationary point (dy/dx = 0):
   d²y/dx² < 0   ∩  concave down  ⇒ MAXIMUM   <-- answer
   d²y/dx² > 0   ∪  concave up    ⇒ minimum
   d²y/dx² = 0        test inconclusive
⚡ JEE Trick Read the symbol as a mouth: ∩ (frown, opens down) = maximum, second derivative negative; ∪ (smile, opens up) = minimum, positive. Trap: memorising the reverse — students often pair "maximum" with "positive" because a max sounds big, but the concavity, not the height, sets the sign.
Answer: (b) d²y/dx² < 0 — negative second derivative (concave down) marks a maximum.
2At a minimum, the second derivative of the function is: (Positive)Second derivative testSecond Derivative Test — introduction🟢 ExactcPositive
Concept At a minimum the curve is a valley (∪-shape): approaching the bottom the slope is negative, at the bottom it is zero, and beyond it the slope is positive — so the slope is increasing. The rate at which the slope increases is the second derivative, so d²y/dx² comes out positive at a minimum. Positive curvature = concave up = valley. This is the exact opposite of the maximum case, where the slope decreases and d²y/dx² < 0.
Formula Minimum test: at a stationary point dy/dx = 0, it is a minimum when d²y/dx² > 0. A positive second derivative means the tangent slope is on the rise.
Solution
1. Model a valley with y = x² (its lowest point is at x = 0).
2. Slope: dy/dx = 2x ⇒ zero at x = 0 ⇒ stationary point.
3. Curvature: d²y/dx² = 2, which is > 0 everywhere ⇒ minimum by the test.
4. Slope check: for x < 0 slope = 2x < 0 (falling into the valley), for x > 0 slope > 0 (climbing out) — the slope increases through zero, i.e. d²y/dx² > 0.
5. So at a minimum the second derivative is positive.
 valley y = x²:   \       /
                   \     /
                    \___/   bottom: dy/dx = 0
   d²y/dx² = +2 > 0  ⇒ concave up ⇒ MINIMUM   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick ∪ smile = minimum = second derivative positive; ∩ frown = maximum = negative. For any parabola y = ax² + bx + c the second derivative is just 2a, so its sign is the sign of the x² coefficient — no work needed. Trap: assuming a minimum (a "low" point) must go with a negative number; it is the upward concavity that makes d²y/dx² positive.
Answer: (c) Positive — d²y/dx² > 0 at a minimum (concave-up valley).
3The inverse operation of differentiation is: (Integration)Integration as inverseIntegration as inverse of differentiation · Anti-derivative — opposite process🟢 ExactcIntegration
Concept Differentiation and integration are inverse operations: differentiating finds the rate of change (slope) of a function, while integrating reverses that step to recover the original function from its rate. Integration is therefore also called anti-differentiation. The reversal is only up to an additive constant C, because differentiating any constant gives zero and so wipes out that information — which is why an indefinite integral always carries "+ C". In physics this is how you climb back from acceleration → velocity → position.
Formula If d/dx[F(x)] = f(x) then ∫f(x) dx = F(x) + C. Power rule pair: d/dx xⁿ = n xⁿ⁻¹ and ∫xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1) + C. Each formula undoes the other.
Solution
1. Start with a function, say F(x) = x².
2. Differentiate it: d/dx[x²] = 2x — this is the "forward" operation giving the slope.
3. Now integrate the result: ∫2x dx = x² + C — the "reverse" operation gives the function back.
4. The two steps cancel (up to C), confirming integration is the inverse of differentiation.C appears because d/dx of any constant is 0, so integration cannot know the original constant.
5. Hence the inverse operation of differentiation is integration.
        differentiate  (find slope)
   F(x) --------------------------> f(x)
   x²                               2x
        <--------------------------
        integrate  (add C back)    ⇒ integration is the inverse  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick To verify any integral in one line, just differentiate your answer — if you get back the integrand, it is correct (this "check by differentiating" catches most sign and coefficient slips). Trap: options like "double differentiation" or "partial differentiation" describe more differentiation, not its reverse — only integration undoes a derivative.
Answer: (c) Integration — the anti-derivative reverses differentiation.
4If dy/dx = 0 and d²y/dx² = 0, then: (Test fails)Test failure caseWhen the Second Derivative Test fails — 2 examples🟢 ExactcTest fails
Concept The second-derivative test classifies a stationary point (dy/dx = 0) by the sign of d²y/dx²: negative ⇒ maximum, positive ⇒ minimum. But when d²y/dx² is also zero, the curvature vanishes at that instant and the test can no longer tell which way the curve bends — it is inconclusive. The point could be a maximum, a minimum, or a point of inflection; you must fall back on the first-derivative sign check or higher derivatives. "Test fails" = the second-derivative test gives no verdict, not that the point is nothing.
Formula At dy/dx = 0: if d²y/dx² ≠ 0 the sign decides max/min; if d²y/dx² = 0 the test is inconclusive — use a sign chart of dy/dx around the point. Zero curvature carries no sign to read.
Solution
1. Case A — y = x⁴: y′ = 4x³ = 0 at x = 0, and y″ = 12x² = 0 at x = 0, yet x = 0 is a genuine minimum (x⁴ ≥ 0).
2. Case B — y = x³: y′ = 3x² = 0 at x = 0 and y″ = 6x = 0 at x = 0, but x = 0 is an inflection, not an extremum.
3. Same second-derivative reading (0) gave two different outcomes ⇒ the test cannot distinguish them.
4. Therefore when both dy/dx = 0 and d²y/dx² = 0 the second-derivative test fails (is inconclusive).
5. Resolve it by the sign of dy/dx just left and right of the point, or by the next non-zero derivative.
   dy/dx = 0 and d²y/dx² = 0  ⇒ TEST INCONCLUSIVE   <-- answer
     y = x⁴ : min      y = x³ : inflection
   both look identical to the 2nd-derivative test
⚡ JEE Trick A zero second derivative is a red flag, not an answer — switch immediately to the first-derivative sign chart (does the slope change − → + , + → − , or keep its sign?). Trap: concluding "it is a point of inflection" whenever d²y/dx² = 0 — x = x⁴ shows that can be false; the honest verdict is simply that this test fails.

5In calculus, slope of a tangent is mathematically given by: (dy/dx)Derivative = slopeDerivative as slope of a tangent line (Khan Academy)🟢 Exactbdy/dx
Concept The slope of a curve at a point is the slope of the tangent line there, and that is precisely what the derivative computes. Formally the slope is the limit of the rise-over-run of a chord as the two points merge, and this limit is written dy/dx. So "slope of the tangent" and "the derivative dy/dx" are the same object. Δy/Δx is the average slope over an interval; dy/dx is its limiting (instantaneous) value.
Formula slope = dy/dx = lim(Δx→0) Δy/Δx = tan θ, where θ is the angle the tangent makes with the x-axis. The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change of y with x.
Solution
1. Take a sample curve y = x² and find the tangent slope at x = 2.
2. Average slope over [2, 2+Δx]: Δy/Δx = ((2+Δx)² − 4)/Δx = (4Δx + Δx²)/Δx = 4 + Δx.
3. Let Δx → 0: slope → 4. This limit is the derivative dy/dx = 2x evaluated at x = 2 ⇒ 2·2 = 4. ✓The chord slope 4 + Δx collapses to the tangent slope 4.
4. So the tangent slope is delivered by the derivative operation dy/dx, matching the general rule.
5. Hence, mathematically, slope of a tangent = dy/dx.
   chord slope = Δy/Δx  --(Δx→0)-->  dy/dx = tangent slope
   y = x², at x=2:  4+Δx  ------->    4   (= 2x at x=2)   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Keep the ladder of meanings straight: dy/dx is slope / rate of change, ∫y dx is area, and d²y/dx² is curvature. Trap: picking Δy/Δx — that is the average slope of a chord; the tangent's slope is its limit dy/dx. The quantity dx/dy (the reciprocal) is the slope with respect to the y-axis, not what is asked.
Answer: (b) dy/dx — the derivative is the slope of the tangent.

Given the derivative dy/dx we recover y by integrating — but integration can only recover y up to an unknown additive constant C, because differentiating a constant gives zero and so erases all trace of it. That is why a physical condition (here the problem simply hands us C = 0) is always needed to pin down the particular curve. In kinematics this is exactly how you go from velocity back to position.
Formula Power rule for integration: ∫xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1) + C for n ≠ −1. Check by differentiating back: d/dx[xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)] = (n+1)xⁿ/(n+1) = xⁿ ✓. Also the constant-multiple rule ∫k·f(x) dx = k∫f(x) dx. Units: if y is in metres and x in seconds, dy/dx is in m s⁻¹ and C carries y's units (m).
