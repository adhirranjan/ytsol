1 (i)Fill in the blank: A ______ solution is one that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.Buffer solution definitionBuffer Solutions - Equilibrium | Class 11 Chemistry Chapter 6 | CBSE🟡 Similarbuffer
Concept A solution that keeps its pH almost constant when a little strong acid or strong base is added is a buffer. It works because it holds a reservoir of two partners: one that eats added H+ and one that eats added OH−. The trap is to answer “neutral” or “saturated” — resisting pH change is the defining property of a buffer only.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) Added acid or base only nudges the ratio [A−]/[HA], and a log of a ratio near 1 barely moves.
Solution
1. Take 1 L of 0.1 M CH3COOH + 0.1 M CH3COONa (pKa = 4.74). Its pH = pKa + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74.
2. Add 0.01 mol HCl. The acetate ion consumes it: CH3COO− + H+ → CH3COOH. The strong acid is converted into the weak acid.
3. New amounts: acetate = 0.1 − 0.01 = 0.09 mol, acid = 0.1 + 0.01 = 0.11 mol.
4. pH = pKa + log(0.09/0.11) = 4.74 + log 0.818 = 4.74 − 0.09 = 4.65, a change of only 0.09 unit.
5. Compare 1 L of pure water: 0.01 mol HCl gives [H+] = 10−2 M, so pH = −log 10−2 = 2, a fall of 5 units from 7 (Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C).
6. Added OH− is handled the same way by the weak acid: CH3COOH + OH− → CH3COO− + H2O.
7. A solution with this resistance is called a buffer solution.
 1 L : 0.1 M AcOH + 0.1 M AcONa     pH = 4.74
 + 0.01 mol HCl : AcO- + H+ -> AcOH
   AcO- = 0.09 , AcOH = 0.11
   pH = 4.74 + log(0.09/0.11) = 4.65    dpH = 0.09
 pure water + 0.01 mol HCl : pH 7 -> 2  dpH = 5
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = buffer                    <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Spot a buffer by its pair: weak acid + its salt, weak base + its salt, or a salt of a weak acid and weak base (CH3COONH4). HCl + NaCl is not a buffer — Cl− cannot take up H+, so there is no partner to absorb added acid.
Answer: buffer solution.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: The pH of a buffer solution is nearly independent of dilution and depends on the ______ of the weak acid and its conjugate base or weak base and its conjugate acid.Buffer pH depends on ratiopH and pKa relationship for buffers | Chemistry | Khan Academy🟡 Similarratio
Concept The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation shows that a buffer’s pH depends on pKa (fixed at a given temperature) and on the ratio [salt]/[acid]. Dilution divides both concentrations by the same factor, so the ratio and hence the pH stay put. The trap is to answer “concentration”: the absolute concentrations change on dilution, the ratio does not.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) ; pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) Valid when both partners are present in comparable, not extremely small, amounts.
Solution
1. Acidic buffer: 0.2 M CH3COONa + 0.1 M CH3COOH, pKa = 4.74.
2. pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) = 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04.
3. Dilute it ten times with water: [salt] = 0.02 M, [acid] = 0.01 M.
4. pH = 4.74 + log(0.02/0.01) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04, unchanged. The factor 10 cancels inside the ratio.
5. Basic buffer works the same way: 0.1 M NH4OH + 0.1 M NH4Cl gives pOH = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74, pH = 14 − 4.74 = 9.26 at any moderate dilution.
6. Only at extreme dilution (around 10−5 M or below) does the water’s own ionisation and the extra dissociation of the weak acid start to shift the pH — hence “nearly independent”.
7. So the pH depends on the ratio of the weak acid to its conjugate base (or weak base to conjugate acid).
 pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid])
 before : 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1)   = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04
 x10 dil: 4.74 + log(0.02/0.01) = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04
 ratio 2 : 1 kept  -> pH kept
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = ratio                     <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Dilution leaves pH unchanged but lowers buffer capacity: fewer moles are available to absorb added acid. If a question asks which of two buffers with the same ratio resists better, choose the more concentrated one, not the one with a different pH.
Answer: ratio (of concentrations).
1 (iii)Fill in the blank: An acidic buffer is made of a weak ______ and its salt with a strong ______.Acidic buffer compositionBuffer || Acidic Buffer and its mechanism || IONIC EQUILLIBRIUM🟡 Similaracid, base
Concept An acidic buffer pairs a weak acid with its salt formed from a strong base, e.g. CH3COOH + CH3COONa (from NaOH). The salt ionises completely and supplies the conjugate base A−. The trap is “strong acid + its salt”: HCl + NaCl cannot buffer because Cl− has no affinity for H+.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) The weak acid neutralises added OH−; the A− from the salt neutralises added H+.
Solution
1. Weak acid: CH3COOH ⇒ CH3COO− + H+, only slightly ionised (Ka = 1.8×10−5, pKa = 4.74).
2. Salt with a strong base: CH3COONa → CH3COO− + Na+, fully ionised; Na+ (from NaOH) is a spectator. The common ion suppresses the acid’s ionisation, so [HA] ≈ Cacid, [A−] ≈ Csalt.
3. Added H+: CH3COO− + H+ → CH3COOH.
4. Added OH−: CH3COOH + OH− → CH3COO− + H2O.
5. With 0.1 M acid and 0.1 M salt: pH = pKa + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74, an acidic pH.
6. Such a buffer can also be made by half-neutralising the weak acid with NaOH: 0.2 mol CH3COOH + 0.1 mol NaOH gives 0.1 mol each of acid and salt.
7. So the blanks read: weak acid and its salt with a strong base.
 AcOH  <=> AcO- + H+      (weak, pKa = 4.74)
 AcONa  -> AcO- + Na+     (salt of strong base)
 + H+  : AcO- + H+  -> AcOH
 + OH- : AcOH + OH- -> AcO- + H2O
 pH = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74

 blanks = acid , base              <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick “Weak acid + excess strong base” is not a buffer (no acid left); “excess weak acid + strong base” is. In mixing questions, do the neutralisation first, then check that both the weak acid and its salt remain.
Answer: weak acid; strong base.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: A basic buffer is made of a weak ______ and its salt with a strong ______.Basic buffer compositionBasic Buffer: pOH Derivation Made Easy | Ionic Equilibrium | NEET🟡 Similarbase, acid
Concept A basic buffer is the mirror image of an acidic one: a weak base with its salt formed from a strong acid, e.g. NH4OH + NH4Cl (from HCl). The salt supplies the conjugate acid NH4+. The trap is “strong base + its salt” (NaOH + NaCl), which has nothing to absorb added OH−.
Key relation pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) ; pH = 14 − pOH The weak base takes up added H+; NH4+ takes up added OH−.
Solution
1. Weak base: NH4OH ⇒ NH4+ + OH−, slightly ionised (Kb = 1.8×10−5, pKb = 4.74).
2. Salt with a strong acid: NH4Cl → NH4+ + Cl−, fully ionised; Cl− (from HCl) is a spectator. The common ion NH4+ suppresses the base’s ionisation, so [base] ≈ Cbase.
3. Added H+: NH4OH + H+ → NH4+ + H2O.
4. Added OH−: NH4+ + OH− → NH4OH.
5. With 0.1 M base and 0.1 M salt: pOH = pKb + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74, so pH = 14 − 4.74 = 9.26 (Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C), a basic pH.
6. It can also be made by half-neutralising the weak base: 0.2 mol NH4OH + 0.1 mol HCl leaves 0.1 mol each of base and salt.
7. So the blanks read: weak base and its salt with a strong acid.
 NH4OH <=> NH4+ + OH-    (weak, pKb = 4.74)
 NH4Cl  -> NH4+ + Cl-    (salt of strong acid)
 + H+  : NH4OH + H+  -> NH4+ + H2O
 + OH- : NH4+  + OH- -> NH4OH
 pOH = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74 ; pH = 9.26
 -----------------------------------------
 blanks = base , acid              <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick For a basic buffer, Henderson gives pOH, not pH — forgetting the final 14 − pOH step is the most common slip. Also note the salt term stays in the numerator in both forms: [salt]/[acid] for pH, [salt]/[base] for pOH.
Answer: weak base; strong acid.
1 (v)Fill in the blank: Buffer solutions are particularly important in maintaining the ______ of biological fluids.Biological buffers maintain pHPhysiologic pH and buffers - acid-base physiology🟡 SimilarpH
Concept Enzymes and proteins work only in a narrow pH window, so body fluids are buffered. Blood is held at pH 7.35–7.45 mainly by the carbonic acid/bicarbonate pair, and cell fluid by the phosphate pair. The blank is therefore pH; the trap is to write “temperature” or “concentration”, which buffers do not control.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([HCO3−]/[H2CO3]) Blood is simply an acidic buffer whose salt-to-acid ratio is kept near 20 : 1.
Solution
1. Main blood buffer: H2CO3 ⇒ H+ + HCO3−, with pKa ≈ 6.1 at body temperature.
2. Normal ratio [HCO3−]/[H2CO3] ≈ 20.
3. pH = pKa + log(ratio) = 6.1 + log 20 = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40, exactly normal blood pH.
4. Acid entering blood (e.g. lactic acid after exercise) is absorbed: HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O, and the lungs breathe out the CO2.
5. Base entering is absorbed by H2CO3 + OH− → HCO3− + H2O. Same two-partner action as any lab buffer.
6. Inside cells the H2PO4−/HPO42− pair (pKa ≈ 7.2) does the same job; proteins also buffer.
7. A fall below 7.35 (acidosis) or a rise above 7.45 (alkalosis) is dangerous, so the property maintained is the pH.
 H2CO3 <=> H+ + HCO3-        pKa ~ 6.1
 [HCO3-]/[H2CO3] ~ 20
 pH = 6.1 + log 20 = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40
 normal blood : 7.35 - 7.45
 cells : H2PO4- / HPO4(2-)   pKa ~ 7.2
 -----------------------------------------
 blank = pH                        <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick log 20 = log 2 + 1 = 1.30 is worth memorising for the blood-pH question. If asked which buffer controls blood pH, choose H2CO3/HCO3−, not the phosphate pair (that dominates inside cells).
Answer: pH.
2 (i)True or false: A buffer solution consists of a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base.Buffer compositionBuffer Solution | Acidic Buffers | Basic Buffers🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept A buffer needs one species to neutralise added H+ and another to neutralise added OH−. A weak acid HA and its conjugate base A− supply exactly that pair, so the statement is true. It describes the acidic buffer; the weak base + conjugate acid pair is the other common type, but that does not make this statement false.
Key relation HA ⇒ H+ + A− ; pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]) Both terms of the ratio must be present in real amounts for the pH to be buffered.
Solution
1. Take HA = CH3COOH and A− = CH3COO− (from CH3COONa).
2. Added acid: A− + H+ → HA. The conjugate base mops up H+.
3. Added base: HA + OH− → A− + H2O. The weak acid mops up OH−.
4. Numerical check, 1 L of 0.1 M HA + 0.1 M A−, pKa = 4.74: pH = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74.
5. Add 0.01 mol NaOH: pH = 4.74 + log(0.11/0.09) = 4.74 + 0.09 = 4.83, a rise of only 0.09.
6. Without the conjugate base (acid alone) there is nothing to absorb H+; without the acid (salt alone) nothing absorbs OH−. Both partners are needed, which is what the statement says.
7. Hence the statement is True.
 HA  = CH3COOH , A- = CH3COO-
 + H+  : A- + H+  -> HA
 + OH- : HA + OH- -> A- + H2O
 pH  = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1)   = 4.74
 +0.01 mol NaOH : 4.74 + log(0.11/0.09) = 4.83

 statement = True                  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Conjugate pairs differ by exactly one H+. Check that before calling a mixture a buffer: H3PO4 + NaH2PO4 is a buffer, but H3PO4 + Na3PO4 first reacts, and HCl + NaCl never buffers.
Answer: True.
2 (ii)True or false: The pH of a buffer solution changes drastically when small amounts of acid or base are added.Buffer resists pH changeBuffer Action | Acidic and basic buffer Action | how buffer resist pH change🟡 SimilarFalse
Concept The whole point of a buffer is that its pH changes only slightly when a small amount of acid or base is added. A drastic change is what happens in pure water or an unbuffered salt solution. So the statement is false; the trap is to mix up a buffer with a solution of a strong acid.
Key relation ΔpH = log([A−]new/[HA]new) − log([A−]/[HA]) Only the ratio shifts, and a small shift in a ratio near 1 gives a tiny change of log.
Solution
1. Buffer: 1 L of 0.1 M CH3COOH + 0.1 M CH3COONa, pH = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74.
2. Add 0.01 mol HCl: acetate falls to 0.09 mol, acid rises to 0.11 mol.
3. pH = 4.74 + log(0.09/0.11) = 4.74 − 0.09 = 4.65, so ΔpH = −0.09.
4. Add instead 0.01 mol NaOH: pH = 4.74 + log(0.11/0.09) = 4.74 + 0.09 = 4.83, ΔpH = +0.09.
5. Same 0.01 mol HCl in 1 L of pure water: pH goes from 7 to −log 10−2 = 2, ΔpH = −5 (Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C).
6. Same 0.01 mol NaOH in water: pOH = 2, pH = 12, ΔpH = +5. The unbuffered change is about 55 times larger.
7. A buffer’s pH therefore changes very little, not drastically: the statement is False.
 buffer 0.1/0.1 acetate       pH = 4.74
 + 0.01 mol HCl  : 4.74 + log(0.09/0.11) = 4.65
 + 0.01 mol NaOH : 4.74 + log(0.11/0.09) = 4.83
 water + same HCl  : 7 -> 2   (dpH = 5)
 water + same NaOH : 7 -> 12  (dpH = 5)
 -----------------------------------------
 statement = False                 <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick The resistance is limited: once added acid exceeds the moles of A− (here 0.1 mol), the buffer is exhausted and pH does crash. “Small amounts” in the statement is the key phrase — within capacity, ΔpH stays below about 0.1–0.3.
Answer: False.
2 (iii)True or false: Buffers can either be acidic or basic.Types of buffers17 Buffer Solution | Acidic Suffer | Basic Buffer | IIT Advanced | JEE Main | Chemistry Class 11🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Buffers are classified by what they are made of and the pH range they hold. An acidic buffer (weak acid + its salt with a strong base) holds pH below 7; a basic buffer (weak base + its salt with a strong acid) holds pH above 7. So the statement is true. A salt of a weak acid and weak base such as CH3COONH4 is sometimes called a simple buffer.
Key relation acidic : pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) ; basic : pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) One equation for each type, mirror images of each other.
Solution
1. Acidic buffer example: 0.1 M CH3COOH + 0.1 M CH3COONa, pKa = 4.74.
2. pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74 (acidic).
3. Basic buffer example: 0.1 M NH4OH + 0.1 M NH4Cl, pKb = 4.74.
4. pOH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74, so pH = 14 − 4.74 = 9.26 (basic), with Kw = 10−14 at 25 °C.
5. Simple buffer: CH3COONH4, pH = 7 + (1/2)(pKa − pKb) = 7 + (1/2)(4.74 − 4.74) = 7.0; both ions are weak partners, so it too resists pH change.
6. Each type has a useful range of about pK ± 1: acetate 3.74–5.74, ammonia pH 8.26–10.26. Outside that the ratio exceeds 10 : 1 and capacity is poor.
7. Since both acidic and basic buffers exist, the statement is True.
 acidic : AcOH + AcONa  pH  = 4.74 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.74
 basic  : NH4OH + NH4Cl pOH = 4.74 -> pH = 9.26
 simple : CH3COONH4     pH  = 7 + (1/2)(0) = 7.0
 range  : pK +/- 1
 -----------------------------------------
 statement = True                  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick To pick a buffer for a target pH, choose the pair whose pKa (or 14 − pKb) is closest to it: pH 5 → acetate, pH 9 → ammonia. A buffer is best (maximum capacity) when [salt] = [acid], i.e. pH = pKa.
Answer: True.
2 (iv)True or false: An acidic buffer solution has a pH below 7.Acidic buffer pH rangeAcidic Buffer Definition and Examples / Buffer Solutions Part-2 /🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept An acidic buffer is built on a weak acid, and its pH sits near that acid’s pKa. For the acids used in practice (acetic, formic, benzoic, pKa 3–5) that is well below 7, so the statement is true in the book’s sense. The fine print: the label “acidic” really refers to the composition, and the pH is pKa ± 1.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]), useful range pKa − 1 to pKa + 1 The pH can never move far from pKa while both partners are present.
Solution
1. Acetate buffer, pKa = 4.74: lowest useful pH = 4.74 + log(1/10) = 3.74.
2. Highest useful pH = 4.74 + log(10/1) = 4.74 + 1 = 5.74, still below 7.
3. Formic acid buffer (pKa ≈ 3.75) covers about 2.75–4.75; benzoic acid buffer (pKa ≈ 4.2) about 3.2–5.2. All below 7.
4. Even an extreme ratio of 100 : 1 for acetate gives pH = 4.74 + log 100 = 6.74 < 7. So for typical weak acids the pH cannot reach 7.
5. Caveat: a very weak acid such as HCN (pKa ≈ 9.3) with NaCN gives pH ≈ 9.3, above 7, though by composition it is “weak acid + salt”. Textbooks usually treat such a mixture as a basic-range buffer.
6. Within the book’s definition (acidic buffer = buffer that maintains acidic pH), the statement holds.
7. Hence the statement is True.
 range = pKa +/- 1
 acetate : 4.74 - 1 = 3.74 ... 4.74 + 1 = 5.74
 formic  : 2.75 ... 4.75
 100:1 acetate : 4.74 + log 100 = 6.74 < 7
 caveat  : HCN/NaCN  pH ~ 9.3

⚡ JEE Trick “Acidic buffer pH < 7, basic buffer pH > 7” is the standard one-liner and the intended answer. If an assertion-reason question pits HCN/NaCN against it, use pKa to decide — the pH always lies near pKa, whatever the label.

2 (v)True or false: In biological systems, buffers are important for maintaining a stable pH.Biological role of buffersBicarbonate Buffer System🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Life chemistry is pH-sensitive: enzyme activity, oxygen binding by haemoglobin and protein shape all depend on pH. Blood, cell fluid and saliva are therefore buffered, so the statement is true. The trap is to think the body keeps pH steady only by excreting acid; the instant protection comes from buffers.
Key relation pH = pKa + log([HCO3−]/[H2CO3]) Blood is a carbonic acid/bicarbonate acidic buffer, with the lungs and kidneys topping it up.
Solution
1. Blood buffer: H2CO3 ⇒ H+ + HCO3−, pKa ≈ 6.1, ratio [HCO3−]/[H2CO3] ≈ 20.
2. pH = 6.1 + log 20 = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40, the normal value.
3. Suppose acid shifts the ratio to 16 : 1. pH = 6.1 + log 16 = 6.1 + 1.20 = 7.30 — acidosis, which the body corrects by breathing out more CO2.
4. Without the buffer the same acid load would drop pH by several units, which is fatal. The buffer turns a large change into a small one.
5. Inside cells H2PO4−/HPO42− (pKa ≈ 7.2) keeps pH near 7; proteins (amino acid side chains) also buffer.
6. Enzymes like pepsin (stomach, pH ≈ 2) and trypsin (intestine, pH ≈ 8) each need their own stable pH.
7. So buffers are indeed important for a stable pH in biological systems: True.

 normal : 6.1 + log 20 = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40
 acid load : 6.1 + log 16 = 6.1 + 1.20 = 7.30
 cells : H2PO4- / HPO4(2-)   pKa ~ 7.2
 normal blood window 7.35 - 7.45

 statement = True                  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick log 16 = 4 log 2 = 1.20 and log 20 = 1.30: these let you compute blood-pH shifts in seconds. Remember the order: blood → bicarbonate, cells → phosphate.
