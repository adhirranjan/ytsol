1 (i)Fill in the blank: Conjugate of 4 + i is ____Conjugate of a complex numberComplex Number - Properties of Conjugate and Modulus🟡 Similar4 &minus; i
Concept The conjugate of a complex number is formed by reversing the sign of the imaginary part only; the real part is left untouched. Geometrically z̄ is the mirror image of z in the real axis of the Argand plane, so the point (4, 1) reflects to (4, −1). This one operation is what makes complex arithmetic usable, because z z̄ is always a real number and that is how a complex denominator is cleared. The hidden step here is reading the bare i as 1·i so that its coefficient can be seen before the sign is flipped.
Formula z = x + iy ⇒ z̄ = x − iy. Conjugation fixes exactly the real numbers: z̄ = z holds if and only if y = 0.
Solution
1. Put z in standard form: z = 4 + i = 4 + 1·i, so x = 4, y = 1.
2. Apply the rule: z̄ = x − iy = 4 − 1·i = 4 − i. Only the sign of y changes; the 4 is never touched.
3. Product test: z z̄ = (4 + i)(4 − i) = 16 − i² = 16 + 1 = 17, a real number.
4. Sum and difference tests: z + z̄ = 8 = 2 Re(z) and z − z̄ = 2i = 2i·Im(z), both as expected.
5. Modulus test: |z| = √(4² + 1²) = √17 = |z̄|, and |z|² = 17 agrees with step 3. z and z̄ always have the same modulus, being reflections of each other.
6. Hence the conjugate of 4 + i is 4 − i.

 z*zbar = 16 - i^2 = 16 + 1 = 17   (real)
 z+zbar = 8  = 2*Re(z)
 z-zbar = 2i = 2i*Im(z)
 |z| = |zbar| = sqrt(17)

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is negating the whole number and writing −4 − i, which is −z, not z̄. Remember conjugation is a reflection in the real axis, while negation is a rotation through 180° about the origin — different operations that coincide only for purely imaginary numbers. A fast self-check: if z z̄ does not come out real and positive, the conjugate written down is wrong.
Answer: 4 − i.

Concept The square of a modulus is the cleanest quantity in the whole chapter because it never carries a surd: |x + iy|² = x² + y². So a relation between two squared moduli is just a ratio of two integers, and k is that ratio. There is also a structural reason for the tidy answer: 4 − 2i = 2(2 − i), and 2 − i is the conjugate of 2 + i, which has exactly the same modulus.
Formula |x + iy|² = x² + y² and |cz| = |c||z| for real c. |z̄| = |z|, so conjugating a factor never changes a modulus.
Solution
1. Left side: |4 − 2i|² = 4² + (−2)² = 16 + 4 = 20.
2. Right side factor: |2 + i|² = 2² + 1² = 4 + 1 = 5.
3. Substitute into 20 = k·5, so k = 20/5 = 4. Both sides are plain real numbers now, so this is ordinary division.
4. Structural check: 4 − 2i = 2(2 − i), hence |4 − 2i| = 2|2 − i| = 2|2 + i|.
5. Squaring that relation gives |4 − 2i|² = 2²|2 + i|² = 4|2 + i|², so k = 4 with no arithmetic at all. The 4 is 2², the square of the real factor pulled out, not the 4 in 4 − 2i.

1 (iii)Fill in the blank: z z̄ = ____Modulus & conjugateComplex Number - Properties of Conjugate and Modulus🟡 Similar|z|&sup2;
Concept Multiplying a complex number by its own conjugate is the one product that is guaranteed to land on the real axis: the cross terms in i cancel and the i² term turns into a plus. What survives is x² + y², which is precisely the square of the distance of z from the origin. So z z̄ is not merely real, it is non-negative, and it vanishes only for z = 0. This identity is the engine behind rationalising denominators and behind every "multiply by the conjugate" step later in the chapter.
Formula z z̄ = |z|² = x² + y². Equivalently z̄ = |z|²/z for z ≠ 0, which is how the reciprocal of a complex number is built.
Solution
1. Let z = x + iy, so that z̄ = x − iy with x, y real.
2. Expand as a difference of squares: z z̄ = (x + iy)(x − iy) = x² − (iy)².
3. Since (iy)² = i²y² = −y², this gives z z̄ = x² + y². The minus of the identity and the minus of i² combine into a plus.
4. But |z| = √(x² + y²), so x² + y² = |z|², and therefore z z̄ = |z|².
5. Numerical check with z = 3 + 4i: z z̄ = (3 + 4i)(3 − 4i) = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5² = |z|². Any test value works; the identity has no exceptions.
6. Hence z z̄ = |z|², a real number that is never negative.

⚡ JEE Trick Do not confuse z z̄ = |z|² with z², which is generally complex: for z = 3 + 4i, z² = −7 + 24i while z z̄ = 25. Also note |z²| = |z|² is true, but z² = |z|² is false unless z is a non-negative real. In any problem containing z̄, replacing z̄ by |z|²/z is often the move that turns a two-variable question into a one-variable one.
Answer: z z̄ = |z|².

Concept Two complex numbers are equal only when their real parts agree and their imaginary parts agree, so an identity like this becomes two real equations. But those comparisons are legal only after every power of i has been reduced — i² and i³ are not independent symbols, they are −1 and −i. The sign asymmetry is the point of the question: the left side carries −i³ while the right side carries +i³, and that single difference is what makes q come out as +1 rather than the −1 a careless termwise match would give.
Formula i² = −1, i³ = −i; and a + ib = c + id ⇔ a = c, b = d. Reduce first, compare second — never the other way round.
Solution
1. Reduce the left side: 3 − i + 2i² − i³ = 3 − i + 2(−1) − (−i) = 3 − i − 2 + i = 1.
2. So the left side is the real number 1 + 0i; the two i-terms have cancelled. −i³ = +i, which is what kills the −i.
3. Reduce the right side: p + qi + ri² + i³ = p + qi − r − i = (p − r) + (q − 1)i.
4. Compare imaginary parts: q − 1 = 0, hence q = 1.
5. Compare real parts: p − r = 1; matching the printed terms one for one gives p = 3 and r = 2, and indeed 3 − 2 = 1. Only the difference p − r is pinned by the equation, so this pairing is a reading of the question, not a deduction.
6. Verify: 3 + 1·i + 2i² + i³ = 3 + i − 2 − i = 1, equal to the left side.

Formula |z|² = x² + y² and |z₁ ± z₂|² = |z₁|² + |z₂|² ± 2Re(z₁z̄₂). The two signs add to give the parallelogram law, in which the cross term cancels.

Concept Conjugation acts on the imaginary part only, and it does not care that the coefficients happen to be trigonometric functions. Here the real part is cosθ and the imaginary part is sinθ, so only the sign in front of sinθ flips. What makes this particular number special is that cos²θ + sin²θ = 1, so it is unimodular — it sits on the unit circle at angle θ, and its conjugate sits at angle −θ, the reflection in the real axis.

Solution
1. Identify the parts: Re(z) = cosθ and Im(z) = sinθ, both real.

If the line iz + z̄ + 1 + i = 0 is tangent to this circle C, then its radius is : (a) 3/√2 (b) 1/(2√2) (c) 3√2 (d) 3/(2√2)Lines & circles in Argand planeL12: Complex Number (Straight Line and Circle ) | Maths for IIT-JEE | Om Sharma🟡 Similard3/(2&radic;2)
Concept A normal to a circle always passes through its centre, so two distinct normals meet exactly at the centre. Each given equation is linear in z and z̄, so writing z = x + iy converts it to an ordinary straight line in the Argand plane. Once the centre is known, the radius is just the perpendicular distance from it to the tangent line. So the whole problem is three conversions and one distance formula.
Formula z = x + iy, z̄ = x − iy; r = |ax₀ + by₀ + c|/√(a²+b²). A tangent touches once, so its distance from the centre equals the radius exactly.
Solution
1. First normal: (2−i)(x+iy) = 2x + y + i(2y − x) and (2+i)(x−iy) = 2x + y + i(x − 2y). Equating gives 2y − x = x − 2y, i.e. x − 2y = 0. The real parts agree automatically, so only the imaginary parts carry information.
2. Second normal: (2+i)(x+iy) = (2x − y) + i(x + 2y) and (i−2)(x−iy) = (y − 2x) + i(x + 2y); adding kills the real part and leaves 2i(x + 2y).
3. So 2i(x+2y) − 4i = 0, giving x + 2y = 2.
4. Centre = intersection: adding the two, 2x = 2 so x = 1, then y = 1/2. Centre is (1, 1/2), i.e. z₀ = 1 + i/2.
5. Tangent: iz + z̄ + 1 + i = i(x+iy) + (x−iy) + 1 + i = (x − y + 1) + i(x − y + 1) = 0, so x − y + 1 = 0. Real and imaginary parts coincide here, so one single line results, not two conditions.
6. Radius = distance from (1, 1/2) to x − y + 1 = 0 = |1 − 1/2 + 1|/√(1²+1²) = (3/2)/√2 = 3/(2√2). Equivalently 3√2/4 ≈ 1.06.
  normal 1 :  x - 2y = 0
  normal 2 :  x + 2y = 2
  ------------------------------
  centre   :  (1, 1/2)
  tangent  :  x - y + 1 = 0
  r = |1 - 0.5 + 1| / sqrt(2) = 1.5/1.4142
  r = 3/(2 sqrt 2) = 3 sqrt2 / 4 = 1.0607
⚡ JEE Trick The key insight is one line long: normals concur at the centre, so you never need the circle's equation at all. When expanding az + bz̄, always split into real and imaginary parts and check which part is identically zero — here the first line's real parts cancel and the third line's two parts are identical, which is what collapses each equation to a single straight line. The distractor 3√2 is the value you get by forgetting the 2 in the denominator, and 1/(2√2) comes from using |1 − 1/2| = 1/2 and dropping the +1 of the tangent line.
Answer: (d) 3/(2√2).
9If the area of the triangle on the complex plane formed by the points z, iz and z + iz is 50 square units, then |z| is (a) 5 (b) 10 (c) 15 (d) None of theseArea in Argand planeIf the area of the triangle on the complex plane formed by the points z, iz and z+iz is 50 squar...🟢 Exactb10
Concept Multiplying by i rotates a vector through 90° without changing its length, so z and iz are perpendicular and both have length |z|. The four points 0, z, iz and z + iz are therefore the corners of a square of side |z|. The triangle asked for uses three of those corners — z, iz and the far corner z + iz — which is exactly half of that square, cut along the diagonal from z to iz. So its area is half the square's area, and setting that equal to 50 fixes |z|.
Formula |iz| = |z|, arg(iz) = arg z + π/2; area of right triangle = ½ × leg × leg. Both legs meet at the corner z + iz, where the sides are the vectors −z and −iz, still mutually perpendicular.
Solution
1. Let A = z, B = iz, C = z + iz. Then C − A = iz and C − B = z. So the two sides meeting at C are copies of iz and z.
2. Their lengths are |C − A| = |iz| = |i||z| = |z| and |C − B| = |z|, so the triangle is isosceles.
3. The angle at C between them is arg(iz/z) = arg(i) = π/2, so the triangle is right-angled at C with two equal legs of length |z|.
4. Area = ½ × |z| × |z| = |z|²/2. Same result as ½|z̄₁z₂| type formulas, but obtained without coordinates.
5. Set |z|²/2 = 50, so |z|² = 100 and |z| = 10 (modulus is non-negative, so the root −10 is rejected).
6. Check with z = 10: vertices 10, 10i and 10 + 10i give legs 10 and 10, area = 50. Consistent for every argument of z, since rotating the whole picture cannot change an area.
  A = z        B = iz       C = z + iz
  C - A = iz   ->  length |z|
  C - B = z    ->  length |z|
  angle at C = arg(iz) - arg(z) = 90 degrees
  area = (1/2)|z||z| = |z|^2 / 2
  |z|^2 / 2 = 50  =>  |z|^2 = 100  =>  |z| = 10
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is treating 0, z, iz as the triangle instead of z, iz, z + iz — both happen to be half the same square, so the area formula |z|²/2 is unchanged, but students who instead take the whole square get |z|² = 50 and the non-option √50. The distractor 5 comes from solving |z|² = 50 with area = |z|² and then mis-rooting, while 15 fits nothing. Whenever i multiplies a complex number, immediately record the two facts — same length, 90° turn — and most Argand-plane area questions collapse to one line.
Answer: (b) 10.
10If |z - 1| + |z + 3| ≤ 8 then the range of values of |z - 4|, (where i = √-1) is (a) (0, 7) (b) (1, 8) (c) [1, 9] (d) [2, 5]Range of modulusIf `|z-1|+|z+3| lt=8` then the range of value of `|z-4|` is🟢 Exactc[1, 9]
Concept |z − 1| + |z + 3| is the sum of distances from z to the two fixed points 1 and −3, so the condition ≤ 8 describes an ellipse together with its interior, with those points as foci. Its centre is the midpoint −1, the sum 2a = 8 gives a = 4, and the focal separation 2c = 4 gives c = 2. The quantity |z − 4| is then the distance from a point of that filled ellipse to the external point 4 on the real axis. Such a distance is extreme at the two ends of the major axis, so the answer is a closed interval.
Formula b² = a² − c²; (x+1)²/16 + y²/12 ≤ 1. The region is closed and bounded, so the continuous function |z − 4| genuinely attains both its minimum and its maximum.
Solution
1. Foci are (1, 0) and (−3, 0); centre (−1, 0), 2c = 4 so c = 2, and 2a = 8 so a = 4, giving b² = 16 − 4 = 12.
2. Region: (x+1)²/16 + y²/12 ≤ 1, so the major axis runs along y = 0 from (−5, 0) to (3, 0). The point (4, 0) lies outside, just 1 unit beyond the right vertex.
3. Write f = |z − 4|² = (x−4)² + y² and substitute the boundary value y² = 12 − ¾(x+1)².
4. f = x² − 8x + 16 + 12 − ¾(x² + 2x + 1) = 0.25x² − 9.5x + 27.25, a parabola with vertex at x = 19, far to the right of the range −5 ≤ x ≤ 3. So f is strictly decreasing across the whole allowed span and its extremes sit at the two endpoints.
5. At x = −5: f = 6.25 + 47.5 + 27.25 = 81, so |z − 4| = 9. At x = 3: f = 2.25 − 28.5 + 27.25 = 1, so |z − 4| = 1.
6. Interior points give values strictly between these, and both vertices satisfy the ≤ sign, so |z − 4| ∈ [1, 9]. Both endpoints are attained, hence a closed interval, which rules out the open-interval options.
  foci 1 and -3 : centre -1, 2c = 4, 2a = 8
  a = 4, c = 2, b^2 = 16 - 4 = 12
  region: (x+1)^2/16 + y^2/12 <= 1 ,  -5 <= x <= 3
  |z-4|^2 = (x-4)^2 + y^2 = x^2/4 - 9.5x + 27.25
  x = -5 -> 81 -> |z-4| = 9   (farthest vertex)
  x =  3 ->  1 -> |z-4| = 1   (nearest vertex)
  range = [1, 9]
⚡ JEE Trick Because 4 lies on the major axis produced, you can skip the algebra entirely: the nearest and farthest points of the filled ellipse are its vertices (3, 0) and (−5, 0), giving 4 − 3 = 1 and 4 + 5 = 9. The ≤ sign is what makes the interval closed, so options (a) and (b) with open brackets are eliminated on inspection. Option (a) (0, 7) is the trap for anyone who forgets the region is bounded on the left at x = −5, and (d) [2, 5] comes from measuring from the centre −1 instead of from the point 4.
Answer: (c) [1, 9].
