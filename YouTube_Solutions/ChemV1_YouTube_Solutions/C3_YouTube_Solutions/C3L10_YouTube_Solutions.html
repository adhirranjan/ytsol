1Calculate the molality of a solution prepared by dissolving 5 g of urea (molar mass = 60 g mol⁻¹) in 95 g of water. (Level-1)Molality of ureaMolality of a urea solution (M = 60) in water🟡 Similarb0.87 m
Concept Molality counts the moles of solute riding with one kilogram of solvent — the denominator is the solvent alone, not the solution. That one word separates it from molarity, which divides by the whole solution's volume. It rests on a mass rather than a volume for a reason: masses don't change when the flask is heated, so molality is temperature-independent — hence colligative formulas use it.
Formula n = w ÷ M turns grams into moles; m = n ÷ W(kg) is the definition (unit mol kg⁻¹), with w = mass of solute, M = its molar mass, W = mass of solvent. A gram of urea and a gram of water hold very different numbers of molecules, so the mole is the common currency. Combined: m = (w × 1000) ÷ (M × Wg). M(urea) = 60 is given.
Solution
1. Urea is the solute, water the solvent.The urea dissolves in the water, so the 95 g is the denominator — settle this first.
2. Moles of urea = w ÷ M = 5 ÷ 60 = 0.0833 mol.Keep 4 decimals; 0.08 shifts the answer by over 4%.
3. Solvent to kilograms: W = 95 ÷ 1000 = 0.095 kg.Molality is per kg; the question gave grams. Skipping this is the classic 1000× error.
4. Molality = n ÷ W = 0.0833 ÷ 0.095 = 0.877 mol kg⁻¹.The 95 g enters unchanged — do not add the 5 g of urea.
5. Rounded to the printed precision, m ≈ 0.87 m — option (b).
6. Check: 0.877 × 0.095 = 0.0833 mol ✓ — back to the moles we started with.
  urea  (SOLUTE)    5 g  -> n = 5/60    = 0.0833 mol
  water (SOLVENT)  95 g  -> W = 95/1000 = 0.095 kg
  solution        100 g  <- NOT used by molality
  m = n / W = 0.0833 / 0.095 = 0.877 ~ 0.87 m -> (b)
⚡ JEE Trick Fix the denominators once: molality → SOLVENT mass (kg), molarity → SOLUTION volume (L). Distractor (d) 0.13 is the killer — it is 5 ÷ 40, the trap for dividing grams by grams without converting to moles; (a) and (c) are fillers. Note the planted near-miss: dividing by the solution mass (100 g) gives 0.833, which still rounds toward 0.87 and hides the error — it won't on harder numbers.
Answer: (b) 0.87 m.
2A solution contains 10 g of glucose (molar mass = 180 g mol⁻¹) dissolved in 90 g of water. Calculate the mole fraction of glucose. (Level-2)Mole fraction of glucoseMole fraction of glucose (M = 180) dissolved in water🟡 Similarb0.0110
Concept Mole fraction is a head-count: of every 100 molecules, how many are glucose? Both parts of the ratio must be in moles, never grams, and the denominator is the total moles, since the solute's own molecules belong to the crowd being counted. No volume appears, so it is dimensionless and temperature-independent — hence its use in Raoult's and Dalton's laws.
Formula n = w ÷ M and χglucose = nglucose ÷ (nglucose + nwater). 10 g of glucose and 10 g of water are not equal numbers of molecules — glucose is ten times heavier per molecule, so only after dividing by M can the two be added. M(glucose) = 180 (given); M(H₂O) = 18 g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Glucose (10 g) is the solute, water (90 g) the solvent; both are counted.
2. nglucose = w ÷ M = 10 ÷ 180 = 0.0556 mol.Keep 4 sig figs — (b) 0.0110 and (c) 0.0120 differ by under 10%.
3. nwater = w ÷ M = 90 ÷ 18 = 5.00 mol.
4. ntotal = 0.0556 + 5.00 = 5.0556 mol.Not 5.00 — the glucose counts itself. The classic wrong denominator.
5. χglucose = 0.0556 ÷ 5.0556 = 0.0110 — option (b).
6. Check: χwater = 5.00 ÷ 5.0556 = 0.9890; sum = 1.0000 ✔.
  BEAKER (head-count, not weigh-in)
    glucose (SOLUTE)  10 g -> 10/180 = 0.0556 mol
    water   (SOLVENT) 90 g -> 90/18  = 5.0000 mol
                             TOTAL   = 5.0556 mol
    X = 0.0556/5.0556 = 0.0110 -> (b)
    check: 0.0110 + 0.9890 = 1.0000  ok
⚡ JEE Trick Burn in the denominators: mole fraction → TOTAL moles, molality → kg solvent, molarity → L solution. (c) 0.0120 is the rounding trap — chop 0.0556 to 0.06: 0.06 ÷ 5.06 = 0.0119; (d) 0.0185 mangles the water's moles (0.0556 ÷ 3); (a) 0.0080 is filler. Near-miss: dividing by the solvent's moles gives 0.0111, which still rounds to (b) and hides the error — but not in a concentrated solution.
Answer: (b) 0.0110.
3A solution contains 4 g of NaOH (molar mass = 40 g mol⁻¹) dissolved in 76 g of water. Calculate the mole fraction of NaOH. (Level-3)Mole fraction of NaOHMole fraction of NaOH and water in a NaOH solution🟡 Similarb0.023
Concept Mole fraction is a census of particles: χNaOH is the share of everything in the beaker that is NaOH, so all fractions sum to 1. The trap is that NaOH is ionic and does split into Na⁺ and OH⁻, so students reflexively apply i = 2. Do not: mole fraction, like molality, counts the solute as supplied. The van't Hoff factor belongs only to colligative properties (ΔTf, ΔTb, π).
Formula n = w ÷ M and χNaOH = nNaOH ÷ (nNaOH + nwater). You cannot add 4 g of NaOH to 76 g of water and call it "80 of something" — masses are not particle counts, and the solute's own moles sit in the denominator too. M(NaOH) = 40 (given); M(H₂O) = 18 g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. NaOH (4 g) is the solute, water (76 g) the solvent.The solution's 80 g is dangled but never used.
2. Count NaOH as supplied — no i = 2.Doubling the numerator to 0.20 mol is the biggest trap here.
3. nNaOH = w ÷ M = 4 ÷ 40 = 0.10 mol.
4. nwater = w ÷ M = 76 ÷ 18 = 4.222 mol.Keep decimals: 4.2 finishes at 0.0233, 4.222 at 0.0231, and (b) sits close to (c).
5. ntotal = 0.10 + 4.222 = 4.322 mol.The solute counts itself.
6. χNaOH = 0.10 ÷ 4.322 = 0.0231 ≈ 0.023 — option (b).
7. Check: χwater = 4.222 ÷ 4.322 = 0.9769; sum = 1.0000 ✔.
  BEAKER (count formula units AS SUPPLIED)
    NaOH  (SOLUTE)   4 g -> 4/40  = 0.100 mol
    water (SOLVENT) 76 g -> 76/18 = 4.222 mol
    solution = 80 g <- NOT used  TOTAL = 4.322
    X = 0.100/4.322 = 0.0231 -> (b)
    i = 2 does NOT apply here (that is Tf, Tb, pi)
⚡ JEE Trick For a dilute solution, χsolute ≈ nsolute ÷ nwater = 0.10 ÷ 4.22 ≈ 0.0237 in a second — eliminate with it, then divide by ntotal for the mark. (c) 0.027 is the ionic trap dressed up: push the numerator toward 2 × 0.10 and you drift there; (d) 0.031 is that pushed further. (a) 0.018 is the reverse — an inflated denominator, e.g. the solution's 80 g not the water's 76 g.
Answer: (b) 0.023.
4A solution has density 1.20 g mL⁻¹ and contains 20 g of solute (molar mass = 50 g mol⁻¹) per 100 g of solution. Calculate the molarity of the solution. (Level-4)Molarity from densityMolarity from density and mass percent of a solution🟡 Similarc4.8 M
Concept Molarity is moles of solute per litre of solution — the denominator is the volume of the whole solution after mixing, not of the solvent you started with. But the composition arrives in grams, so something must convert mass to volume: density, the exchange rate between the two. A volume sits in the denominator and volumes expand on heating, so molarity is temperature-dependent.
Formula n = w ÷ M; V = mass ÷ d; M = n ÷ V(L). The composition is by mass but molarity demands a volume, and density is the only bridge — 1.20 g mL⁻¹ is the density of the solution, so it may act only on the solution's 100 g. Rolled up: M = (10 × %w/w × d) ÷ Msolute.
Solution
1. Read it precisely: 20 g of solute per 100 g of solution, so the solvent is only 80 g.Not 20 g added to 100 g of water — this decides the problem.
2. Take 100 g of solution as the basis.Molarity is a ratio, so the quoted 100 g stays clean.
3. n(solute) = w ÷ M = 20 ÷ 50 = 0.40 mol.
4. V(solution) = mass ÷ d = 100 ÷ 1.20 = 83.33 mL.The 100 g goes in, not the 20 g; d > 1, so it takes less than 100 mL.
5. In litres: V = 83.33 ÷ 1000 = 0.08333 L.Skip this, land 1000× off.
6. M = n ÷ V(L) = 0.40 ÷ 0.08333 = 4.8 mol L⁻¹ — option (c).
7. Shortcut: (10 × 20 × 1.20) ÷ 50 = 4.8 ✔.
  100 g SOLUTION (basis)  d = 1.20 g/mL (SOLUTION)
    solute  20 g -> 20/50 = 0.40 mol
    solvent 80 g
  V = 100/1.20 = 83.33 mL = 0.08333 L
    denser than water: 100 g takes up < 100 mL
    M = 0.40/0.08333 = 4.8 M -> (c)
    planted wrong turn: V = 20/1.20 -> 24 M
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise M = (10 × %w/w × d) ÷ Msolute — the 10 is just 1000 mL/L ÷ 100 g of basis. (b) 4.0 M is the density-ignored trap: pretend 100 g is 100 mL and get 0.40 ÷ 0.100; the most seductive option. (d) 6.0 M inverts the density; (a) 3.0 M is the reverse — multiplying 100 g by 1.20 gives 120 mL and 0.40 ÷ 0.120 = 3.3. If d > 1, M must exceed the density-ignored value: 4.8 > 4.0 kills both.
Answer: (c) 4.8 M.
5An aqueous solution has molality m = 2.00 mol kg⁻¹. Calculate the mole fraction of the solute. (Level-5)Molality → mole fractionMole fraction of solute in a 2.0 molal aqueous solution🟢 Exactb0.0347
Concept Converting molality to mole fraction looks like it needs the solution's mass — it doesn't. Molality is a recipe: "2.00 mol of solute per 1 kg of water", so simply adopt 1 kg of water as your basis and both mole counts appear at once. The trap is the denominator: mole fraction divides by the total moles (solute + solvent), not by the water's moles alone — the solute counts itself in the crowd it is measured against.
Formula nwater = 1000 ÷ 18 = 55.56 mol per kg of water; Xsolute = n ÷ (n + nwater) = m ÷ (m + 55.56). Valid for any aqueous molality question — the 55.56 mol/kg of water is a constant worth memorising. M(H₂O) = 18 g mol⁻¹.
Solution
1. Choose the basis: 1 kg of water.Molality hands you this for free — n(solute) = m = 2.00 mol by definition.
2. Moles of water in that kilogram: n = 1000 ÷ 18 = 55.56 mol.
3. Total moles = 2.00 + 55.56 = 57.56 mol.The solute joins the head-count — the step most students skip.
4. Xsolute = 2.00 ÷ 57.56 = 0.03475 ≈ 0.0347.
5. Check: Xwater = 55.56 ÷ 57.56 = 0.9653, and 0.0347 + 0.9653 = 1.000 ✔.
6. Price the slip: 2.00 ÷ 55.56 = 0.0360 — dividing by the solvent alone; close, but wrong on principle and off in the third digit.
  BASIS: 1 kg water (molality = 2.00 says 2.00 mol solute rides here)
  solute   2.00 mol
  water    1000/18   = 55.56 mol
  total              = 57.56 mol
  X = 2.00 / 57.56   = 0.0347   <-- answer
  slip: 2.00 / 55.56 = 0.0360 (solvent-only denominator)
⚡ JEE Trick For any aqueous molality m, jump straight to X = m ÷ (m + 55.56) — one line, no basis-building each time. The planted trap in this family is the denominator: dividing by 55.56 alone gives 0.0360, which sits close enough to 0.0347 to survive a careless glance. When the third significant figure decides the option, the "+ m" in the denominator is exactly where the examiner looks.
Answer: (b) Xsolute ≈ 0.0347.

1Calculate the mass of (1) one nitrogen atom and (2) one nitrogen molecule in kg. (N = 14)Mass of one atom/moleculeMass of one nitrogen molecule (N₂) in kg🟡 Similar—
Concept The periodic table's N = 14 is not the mass of one atom in grams — it is the mass of one mole, of 6.022×10²³ atoms together, because a single atom is far too light to weigh. To reach one particle you divide by NA, sharing the bundle's mass among its members. Nitrogen the element is atomic (N, 14 u), but in a gas jar it is the diatomic molecule N₂ (28 u), which must weigh exactly twice the atom.
Formula mass of one particle = M ÷ NA, NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹. The units prove it: g mol⁻¹ ÷ mol⁻¹ = g per particle — one mole weighs M grams and holds NA particles. M(N) = 14, M(N₂) = 2 × 14 = 28 g mol⁻¹. The answer is wanted in kilograms: 1 g = 10⁻³ kg.
Solution
1. Part (1) — one N ATOM. m = 14 ÷ (6.022×10²³) = 2.325×10⁻²³ g.Mantissa and exponent separately: 14 ÷ 6.022 = 2.325.
2. To kg: 2.325×10⁻²³ × 10⁻³ = 2.325×10⁻²⁶ kg — answer (1).−23 + (−3) = −26. The word "kg" is where the mark hides.
3. Part (2) — one N MOLECULE. Nitrogen gas is N₂: M = 2 × 14 = 28.Using 14 here answers for an atom twice — the entire trap.
4. m = 28 ÷ (6.022×10²³) = 4.650×10⁻²³ g.
5. To kg: 4.650×10⁻²³ × 10⁻³ = 4.650×10⁻²⁶ kg — answer (2).
6. Check: 4.650×10⁻²⁶ ÷ 2.325×10⁻²⁶ = 2 exactly ✔.Two identical atoms must weigh twice one atom.
  1 mol N atoms = 14 g = 6.022e23 atoms
    14 / 6.022e23 = 2.325e-23 g = 2.325e-26 kg
  1 mol N2 = 28 g = 6.022e23 molecules
    28 / 6.022e23 = 4.650e-23 g = 4.650e-26 kg

  ATOM |----|   N2 |----|----|  (exactly 2x)  OK
⚡ JEE Trick Mass of one particle = M ÷ 6.022×10²³ g; for kg push the exponent three lower. Error 1 — using 14 for the molecule: the free element is N₂ at 28, and the ratio test (molecule ÷ atom = 2) catches it. Error 2 — forgetting g → kg, losing the mark to a factor of 1000. Error 3 — multiplying by NA: one atom must be absurdly light, so the exponent must be strongly negative — 8.4×10²⁴ kg is a planet.
Answer: mass of one N atom = 2.325×10⁻²⁶ kg; mass of one N₂ molecule = 4.650×10⁻²⁶ kg.
2Calculate the percentage loss of weight when one mole potassium nitrate is completely decomposed by heating. (Atomic masses: K = 39, N = 14, O = 16)Percentage loss on decompositionPercentage loss in weight on decomposition of a salt🟡 Similar—
Concept "Loss of weight" on heating is mass escaping as a gas. KNO₃ decomposes on strong heating to KNO₂ plus oxygen; the O₂ bubbles out while the solid stays, so the balance reads lighter by exactly the mass of O₂ that left. Since mass is conserved, the loss follows two ways — the stoichiometry of the escaping gas, or solid before − solid after — and they must agree. The percentage is taken against the original KNO₃.
Formula 2KNO₃ → 2KNO₂ + O₂↑. KNO₃ → KNO₂ + O₂ cannot be right — 3 oxygens left, 4 right. One O atom is lost per formula unit and oxygen leaves as diatomic O₂, so two units decompose per O₂ — hence the 2's. From K = 39, N = 14, O = 16: M(KNO₃) = 101, M(KNO₂) = 85, M(O₂) = 32. Then w = n × M.
Solution
1. Balance: 2KNO₃ → 2KNO₂ + O₂↑ — K: 2 = 2, N: 2 = 2, O: 6 = 2×2 + 2 ✔.The ↑ marks the only species that leaves, so loss = mass of O₂.
2. Basis: n(KNO₃) = 1 mol, so w = 1 × 101 = 101 g — your 100%.
3. Mole ratio: 2 mol KNO₃ ≡ 1 mol O₂, so n(O₂) = 1 × (1 ÷ 2) = 0.5 mol.Half a mole, not one — this fraction is the whole question.
4. w(O₂) = n × M = 0.5 × 32 = 16 g — the mass lost.
5. Residue route: 1 mol KNO₂ = 85 g, so loss = 101 − 85 = 16 g ✔.Both routes must agree, or it isn't balanced.
6. % loss = (16 ÷ 101) × 100 = 15.84 %.Denominator is the 101 g you started with — not 85, not 16.
  BALANCED: 2 KNO3 --(heat)--> 2 KNO2 + O2 (up)
  MOLE LEDGER (basis: 1 mol KNO3)
    KNO3 in  : 1.0 x 101 = 101 g  <- the 100%
    KNO2 out : 1.0 x  85 =  85 g  (stays)
    O2   out : 0.5 x  32 =  16 g  (escapes)
    check: 85 + 16 = 101 g OK (conserved)
    % loss = 16/101 x 100 = 15.84 %
⚡ JEE Trick For any "loss on heating", skip the gas: M(reactant) − M(residue) over M(reactant): 101 − 85 = 16, so 16/101 = 15.84% in one line. Error 1 — not balancing: KNO₃ → KNO₂ + O₂ gives a whole mole of O₂ = 32 g and 31.7%, exactly double; a 15.84 that "should have been" 31.7 is an unbalanced equation. Error 2 — wrong denominator: 16/85 measures against the product. Error 3 — treating O₂ as monatomic.
Answer: percentage loss of weight = 15.84 % (16 g of O₂ lost from 101 g of KNO₃).
3Lithium exists in nature in the form of two isotopes, Li-6 and Li-7 with atomic masses 6.015 u and 7.0160 u and the percentage 8.24 and 91.76 respectively. Calculate average atomic mass.Average atomic mass (Li)Average atomic mass of lithium from its two isotopes🟡 Similar—
Concept Lithium's tabulated 6.94 is not the mass of any real atom. Every natural lithium atom is either Li-6 (6.015 u) or Li-7 (7.0160 u), and 6.94 is their weighted average: 8.24 % are light and 91.76 % heavy, so the average is dragged toward the heavy end. The weighting is essential — a plain mean assumes equal abundance, which is false here. The isotopes differ only in neutron count, so their chemistry is identical.
Formula Aavg = (m₁ × %₁ + m₂ × %₂) ÷ 100, m in u. Each isotope contributes its mass in proportion to how many atoms of it there are; the ÷100 turns percentages back into fractions, so this is just Aavg = m₁f₁ + m₂f₂. First confirm %₁ + %₂ = 100. Data: Li-6 = 6.015 u at 8.24 %; Li-7 = 7.0160 u at 91.76 %.
Solution
1. Tabulate: m₁ = 6.015 u with %₁ = 8.24; m₂ = 7.0160 u with %₂ = 91.76.Pair each mass with its own abundance — swapping is silent.
2. Validate: 8.24 + 91.76 = 100.00 ✔.Weights must cover the whole sample.
3. Predict: 91.76 % are Li-7, so the answer must hug 7.016.Anything near the middle is wrong by inspection.
4. Aavg = (6.015 × 8.24 + 7.0160 × 91.76) ÷ 100.
5. Contributions: 6.015 × 8.24 = 49.56; 7.0160 × 91.76 = 643.79.
6. Add and divide: (49.56 + 643.79) ÷ 100 = 6.9335 u ≈ 6.94 u.Exactly the periodic-table value for Li.
7. Against step 3: 6.9335 sits very close to 7.016 ✔.
  LEDGER (per 100 atoms of natural lithium)
     8.24 atoms x 6.015  =  49.56
    91.76 atoms x 7.0160 = 643.79
                   total = 693.35 u
    average = 693.35/100 = 6.9335 u ~= 6.94 u
  6.015 |------------------------X--| 7.0160
  plain mean = 6.5155 <- WRONG (needs 50:50)
⚡ JEE Trick Read it as "per 100 atoms": 8.24 weigh 6.015 each, 91.76 weigh 7.0160 each, total and divide by 100. Error 1 — the plain mean: (6.015 + 7.016)/2 = 6.52 assumes 50:50, the commonest wrong answer. Error 2 — swapping mass with abundance → ~6.10, still inside the window, so only the "hug the abundant end" check catches it. Error 3 — dropping the ÷100, leaving 693.35 u. Error 4 — rounding 7.0160 to 7.0 → 6.92.
Answer: average atomic mass of lithium = 6.9335 u ≈ 6.94 u.

2. (a) n × 100 — no division, unit mol, unbounded: FAIL.The ×100 is stolen from mass %, legal only after a ratio.
3. (b) n ÷ 100 g — a mass below, mol g⁻¹: FAIL.
4. (d) n ÷ V(solvent, L) — a volume below, mol L⁻¹, temperature-dependent: FAIL.Not even molarity, which uses litres of solution.
5. (c) XA = nA ÷ ntotal — ratio ✔, total moles ✔, unitless ✔.

Concept Neutralisation is equivalent-for-equivalent: one equivalent of H⁺ cancels exactly one of OH⁻. That is why titration runs in normality — it already counts the reactive units, so N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ needs no equation. The bridge is N = M × n-factor: basicity for an acid, acidity for a base. This question is one fact: H₂SO₄ is dibasic, so 0.1 M is 0.2 N — while NaOH is monoacidic, so its N and M coincide.
Formula From Q3: M(NaOH) = 1.193 M. The bridge: N = M × (n-factor). It converts molecules per litre into reactive H⁺ or OH⁻ per litre — what meets at the endpoint. The law: N₁V₁ = N₂V₂, worked as meq = N × V(mL). Both sides are equivalents, so the unit cancels and V may stay in mL.

Concept One basis, four verdicts: take 1 L of the 3 M solution, and the density converts it to mass, from which weight %, mole fraction, and ionic molalities all follow. The extra twist is dissociation: Na₂S₂O₃ → 2 Na⁺ + S₂O₃²⁻, so ionic molality = formula-unit molality × the stoichiometric count. Every option must be checked on its own — this is a multi-correct where, unusually, everything printed survives.
Formula Mw(Na₂S₂O₃) = 46 + 64 + 48 = 158; wt% = w₂/w(soln) × 100; χ = n₂/(n₂ + n₁); m(ion) = ν × n₂ ÷ W₁(kg) — ν = 2 for Na⁺, 1 for S₂O₃²⁻; molality scales linearly with the ion count.
Solution
1. Basis 1 L: n₂ = 3 mol → w₂ = 3 × 158 = 474 g; solution = 1000 × 1.25 = 1250 g; water = 1250 − 474 = 776 g.
