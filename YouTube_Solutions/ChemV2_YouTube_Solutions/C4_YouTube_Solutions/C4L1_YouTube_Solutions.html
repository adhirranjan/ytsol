1According to Lavoisier's classification, elements that have a tendency to gain electrons were placed in which category?Lavoisier classificationMetals, non-metals and metalloids — classification of elements🟡 SimilarbNon-metals
Concept Lavoisier's was the first and crudest classification: he split all known elements into just two families, metals and non-metals, using their chemical behaviour towards electrons. The dividing test is the direction of electron transfer — a metal is electron-poor in the sense that it holds its valence electrons loosely and gives them away, while a non-metal has a nearly complete octet and pulls electrons in. So the phrase "tendency to gain electrons" is a direct definition of a non-metal.
Key relation Metal: M → Mn+ + n e⁻ (loses electrons, forms cations). e.g. Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and K → K⁺ + e⁻ — both are group-1 metals with one loose valence electron. Non-metal: X + n e⁻ → Xn− (gains electrons, forms anions). e.g. F + e⁻ → F⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ — both are halogens one electron short of an octet.
Solution
1. Read what the question actually specifies: the elements gain electrons.This fixes the direction of transfer, which is the only criterion Lavoisier used.
2. Apply Lavoisier's definitions: losing electrons ⇒ metal; gaining electrons ⇒ non-metal.
3. Eliminate the distractors:
  • (a) Metals — wrong direction; metals lose electrons.
  • (c) Metalloids — a later category; Lavoisier's scheme had no place for them (that is precisely its stated limitation).
  • (d) Transition elements — a modern block-based term, unknown to Lavoisier.

                      K  → K⁺  + e⁻
 gains e⁻ (anion)     F + e⁻ → F⁻          NON-METAL   <-- answer (b)
                      Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
 both / neither       B, Si, As            (metalloid — no place in Lavoisier)

Trap: options (c) and (d) name real categories, but both were introduced after Lavoisier — a historical question must be answered with the knowledge available at that time.

2In a Dobereiner triad, the atomic weight of the middle element is approximately equal to:Dobereiner's triad ruleDobereiner's Triads and Newlands's Law of Octaves🟡 SimilarbAverage atomic weight of the first and third elements
Concept Dobereiner (1817) noticed that certain sets of three chemically similar elements, when written in order of increasing atomic weight, show a striking arithmetic regularity: the middle element sits almost exactly halfway between the outer two. This was the first quantitative hint that atomic weight and chemical property are linked — the seed of periodicity. The rule is a plain arithmetic mean, nothing more.
Formula Amiddle ≈ (Afirst + Athird) ÷ 2 the mean of the two extremes; "≈" because the agreement is close but never exact. Standard triads: (Cl, Br, I), (Ca, Sr, Ba), (Li, Na, K), and also (K, Rb, Cs), (P, As, Sb), (S, Se, Te).
Solution
1. Write the rule: middle atomic weight = arithmetic mean of the first and third.
2. Test it on the halogen triad Cl (35.5), Br (80.0), I (127):x = (35.5 + 127) ÷ 2 = 162.5 ÷ 2 = 81.2, against the observed 80.0 — agreement to about 1.5%.
3. Test it on the alkaline-earth triad Ca (40), Sr (87.6), Ba (137):x = (40 + 137) ÷ 2 = 177 ÷ 2 = 88.5, against the observed 87.6 — again about 1%.
4. Test it on the alkali triad Li (7), Na (23), K (39):x = (7 + 39) ÷ 2 = 46 ÷ 2 = 23.0 — this one lands exactly on the observed value.
5. In every case the middle weight is the average, so option (b) is correct; (a) doubling, (c) a product and (d) a sum all fail these numbers immediately.
 triad          A₁      A₂(obs)   A₃      (A₁+A₃)/2   verdict
 Cl  Br  I      35.5     80.0     127      81.2       ≈ ✓
 Ca  Sr  Ba     40       87.6     137      88.5       ≈ ✓
 Li  Na  K       7       23        39      23.0       exact ✓
 ⇒ middle = arithmetic mean of the outer two          <-- answer (b)
⚡ JEE Trick To test any proposed triad in seconds, just add the two outer atomic weights and halve — if the result is within a unit or two of the middle element, it is a valid Dobereiner triad. Remember the limitation too, since it is asked as often as the rule: only a handful of elements could be arranged as triads, and the rule fails completely for d- and f-block elements. Trap: option (d) "sum" gives roughly twice the correct value — check the magnitude, not just the words.
Answer: (b) Average atomic weight of the first and third elements.
3According to Newlands' observation, the properties of Li are similar to which numbered element ahead of it?Newlands' law of octavesDobereiner's Triads and Newland's Octaves — Classification of Elements🟡 Similard8th
Concept Newlands (1865) arranged the known elements in increasing order of atomic mass and found that the eighth element repeated the properties of the first — exactly like the eighth note of a musical scale (sa re ga ma pa dha ni sa), which is why he called it the Law of Octaves. The period of repetition is 8 because, in the light elements he was working with, a row of the (then unknown) periodic table held 8 elements — and crucially the noble gases had not yet been discovered, so they were not in his count.
Key relation property(element n) ≈ property(element n + 7 positions ahead) = the 8th element counting inclusively count the starting element itself as "1", so the repeat lands on number 8 — the same convention as a musical octave.
Solution
1. Write the light elements in order of increasing atomic mass, starting from Li:Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, K, Ca … (H sits before Li; inert gases were unknown in 1865).
2. Count from Li, taking Li itself as position 1:
  Li(1) Be(2) B(3) C(4) N(5) O(6) F(7) Na(8).
3. Na is the 8th element and is chemically a twin of Li — both are soft, highly reactive alkali metals forming M⁺ ions.Repeat the count from Be and you land on Mg, again a chemical twin — the pattern is general.
4. So the repeat interval is 8, matching option (d); 5th, 6th and 7th would land on N, O and F, which are nothing like Li.
 position   1    2    3    4    5    6    7    8
 row 1      Li   Be   B    C    N    O    F    (Na)
 row 2      Na   Mg   Al   Si   P    S    Cl   (K)
            ↑                                   ↑
            same properties recur at the 8th    <-- answer (d)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the octave rule together with its breaking point, because that is the usual follow-up question: the law holds only up to calcium (Ca). Past Ca the d-block appears, so the true repeat interval jumps from 8 to 18, and the later discovery of the inert gases pushed the alkali metal to the 9th place — which is why the law was abandoned. Trap: counting exclusively (Li + 8 more = Mg) gives 9 and the wrong answer; a musical octave always counts the starting note.
Answer: (d) the 8th element.
4Because Mendeleev arranged elements based on atomic weight, which anomaly occurred?Anomalous pairsThe anomalous pairs in Mendeleev's periodic table (Te–I, Co–Ni, Ar–K)🟢 ExactcHeavier elements (Ar, Co) appeared before lighter ones (K, Ni)
Concept Mendeleev's ordering variable was atomic weight, but the property that actually governs chemistry is atomic number. For most elements the two run in the same order, so nothing goes wrong. For a few pairs they disagree — because the heavier element happens to have abundant heavy isotopes — and Mendeleev had to break his own rule, placing a heavier element before a lighter one so that each landed in the chemically correct group. These are the famous anomalous pairs, and they are the clearest evidence that atomic weight is the wrong ordering parameter.
Key relation order by atomic weight ≠ order by atomic number (for a few pairs) atomic weight is an isotope-weighted average of mass number, while atomic number is the proton count — an element with unusually heavy isotopes can outweigh its successor. The classic pairs are Ar–K, Co–Ni, Te–I and Th–Pa.
Solution
1. List the offending pairs with their atomic weights, as printed in the theory:
  • Ar (39.9) placed before K (39.1) — heavier first.
  • Co (58.9) placed before Ni (58.6) — heavier first.
  • Te (127.5) placed before I (127) — heavier first.
  • Th (232) placed before Pa (231) — heavier first.
2. In every case the placement is chemically right but weight-wise "backwards", so the anomaly is exactly that stated in option (c).K must sit with the alkali metals and I with the halogens; obeying atomic weight strictly would have put them in the wrong groups.
3. Kill the other options: (a) is false — isotopes had no clear position, that was another defect; (b) is false — Cu, Ag, Au were forced in with Na and K despite differing properties; (d) is false — hydrogen's placement was ambiguous (it resembles both group IA and VIIA), not "unique" in a good sense.
4. Moseley later resolved all of it: order by atomic number and every pair falls into place (Ar 18 < K 19; Co 27 < Ni 28; Te 52 < I 53).
 pair     at.wt      at.no.     Mendeleev's order   modern order
 Ar / K   39.9/39.1  18 / 19    Ar then K  (wt ✗)   Ar then K  ✓
 Co / Ni  58.9/58.6  27 / 28    Co then Ni (wt ✗)   Co then Ni ✓
 Te / I   127.5/127  52 / 53    Te then I  (wt ✗)   Te then I  ✓
 Th / Pa  232 /231   90 / 91    Th then Pa (wt ✗)   Th then Pa ✓
 ⇒ heavier element placed first                     <-- answer (c)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the four anomalous pairs as one string — Ar–K, Co–Ni, Te–I, Th–Pa — they are asked directly, and they are also the standard "reason" half of Assertion–Reason items about why atomic number replaced atomic weight. Trap: options that praise Mendeleev's table (isotopes fitted, similar elements always together) are describing the modern table, not his.
Answer: (c) Heavier elements (Ar, Co) appeared before lighter ones (K, Ni).
5An element has valence shell configuration ns² np⁴. Identify its period and group.Group & period from configurationHow to find group, period and block of any element (spdf trick)🟡 SimilarbPeriod = n, Group = 16
Concept The position of an element in the long-form table is read straight off its valence-shell configuration — no periodic table needed. Two independent readings are involved: the principal quantum number n of the outermost shell fixes the period, and the count of valence electrons (with a block-dependent rule) fixes the group. Here the last electron enters a p-orbital, so the element is a p-block element and the p-block group formula applies.
Formula period = n (of the valence shell). Block rules for the group number: s-block → group = number of valence electrons; p-block → group = 10 + number of valence electrons; d-block → group = (n−1)d electrons + ns electrons. The +10 for the p-block exists because the ten d-block groups (3–12) sit physically between group 2 and group 13 in the long form.
Solution
1. Identify the block: the last electron enters np, so this is a p-block element.
2. Read the period: the valence shell carries principal quantum number n, so period = n.Period number is always just the outermost shell number — this part needs no arithmetic.
3. Count the valence electrons: ns² np⁴ gives 2 + 4 = 6 electrons in the outermost shell.
4. Apply the p-block rule: group = 10 + 6 = 16.
5. Sanity-check with a real element: for n = 2 this is 2s²2p⁴ = oxygen, which is indeed period 2, group 16 (the oxygen family/chalcogens); for n = 3 it is 3s²3p⁴ = sulphur, period 3, group 16.
 config       ns² np⁴
 block        last e⁻ in p  →  p-block
 period       = n
 valence e⁻   = 2 + 4 = 6
 group        = 10 + 6 = 16          <-- answer (b)
 check        n=2 → O (P2,G16);  n=3 → S (P3,G16)
⚡ JEE Trick Burn the three group formulas in: s-block = valence e⁻, p-block = 10 + valence e⁻, d-block = (n−1)d + ns. Even faster for the p-block: ns²npx ⇒ group = 12 + x, so np⁴ → 16 on sight. Trap: forgetting the +10 gives "group 6" (option c's flavour) — that is a d-block answer, and this element has no d-electrons at all.
Answer: (b) Period = n, Group = 16.

1State Mendeleev's periodic law.Mendeleev's periodic lawPeriodic Table 01 — Historical Development / Periodic Classification🟡 Similar—
Concept A periodic law is a single sentence that names (i) the ordering variable and (ii) the claim that properties repeat. Mendeleev's version, published in 1869, chose atomic weight as the ordering variable — the only atomic constant measurable at the time — and asserted that both physical and chemical properties are a periodic function of it. Every merit and every defect of his table follows from that one choice.
Statement "The physical and chemical properties of elements are the periodic function of their atomic weight." "Periodic function" means: arrange the elements in increasing atomic weight and elements with similar properties reappear after regular intervals — they do not vary smoothly and endlessly in one direction.
Solution
1. State the law exactly as above — the examiner is checking for the words "atomic weight" and "periodic function".
2. Explain "periodic function": on arranging elements in increasing atomic weight, elements of similar properties recur after a regular interval.This is what allows similar elements to be stacked into vertical columns, i.e. groups.
3. Give the supporting evidence Mendeleev used — Lothar Meyer's atomic-volume curve, whose maxima are always occupied by alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs).
4. Note the consequence: it produced a table of 7 periods (horizontal rows) and 8 groups (vertical columns) for the 63 elements then known.
5. Contrast with the modern law, since board questions usually ask both together: Moseley replaced atomic weight by atomic number.
 law            ordering variable   statement
 Mendeleev 1869 atomic WEIGHT       properties = periodic function of atomic weight
 Modern (Moseley) atomic NUMBER     properties = periodic function of atomic number
 evidence       Lothar Meyer's atomic-volume curve (maxima = alkali metals)
⚡ JEE Trick The two periodic laws differ by exactly one word — weight vs number. Write the sentence once and swap that word; you have both answers. Trap: writing "atomic mass" for Mendeleev is accepted, but writing "atomic number" turns it into the modern law and loses the mark outright.
Key point: Mendeleev's periodic law — the physical and chemical properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weight.
2What are the salient features of Mendeleev's periodic table?Features of Mendeleev's tableMerits and demerits of Mendeleev's periodic table🟡 Similar—
Concept Mendeleev's table was the first systematic two-dimensional arrangement of the elements: one axis (rows) tracks increasing atomic weight, the other (columns) collects chemically similar elements. Describing its "salient features" means describing that geometry — how many rows, how many columns, how the columns are subdivided, and what the special group VIII is — plus the fact that it deliberately contained gaps.
Key relation horizontal rows = periods (7) and vertical columns = groups (8), ordered by increasing atomic weight. Elements in one column share a valency and hence similar formulas of oxides and hydrides — that is what "similar properties" meant to Mendeleev.
Solution
1. Basis: the table is built on atomic weight, and 63 elements were known; the noble gases had not yet been discovered.
2. First systematic classification: Mendeleev was the first to organise elements into horizontal rows and vertical columns together.
3. Periods: the horizontal rows are called periods, and there are 7 of them.
4. Groups: the vertical columns are called groups, and there are 8 of them (I to VIII).
5. Sub-groups: each group up to VII is split into an A and a B sub-group; 'A' sub-group elements are the normal (representative) elements and 'B' sub-group elements are the transition elements.
6. Group VIII: a special wide column containing 9 elements arranged in three rows (the transition-metal triads Fe-Co-Ni, Ru-Rh-Pd, Os-Ir-Pt).
7. Similar properties: elements of the same group show similar chemical behaviour — and blank spaces were left for elements not yet discovered.
 feature            Mendeleev's table
 basis              atomic weight
 elements known     63 (no noble gases)
 periods (rows)     7
 groups (columns)   8   (I … VIII)
 sub-groups         A = normal/representative, B = transition (groups I–VII)
 group VIII         9 elements in 3 rows (Fe Co Ni | Ru Rh Pd | Os Ir Pt)
 gaps               left blank for undiscovered elements (eka-Al, eka-Si, …)
⚡ JEE Trick Answer this by counting: 63 elements, 7 periods, 8 groups, A/B sub-groups, 9 elements in group VIII. Numbers are what earn the marks and what MCQs test. Trap: quoting "18 groups and 7 periods" — that is the long form; Mendeleev's had 8 groups.
Key point: based on atomic weight; 63 known elements; 7 periods (rows) and 8 groups (columns); groups I–VII split into A (normal/representative) and B (transition) sub-groups; group VIII holds 9 elements in 3 rows; gaps left for undiscovered elements.
3State the Modern periodic law.Modern periodic law / MoseleyPeriodic Classification — full chapter (modern periodic law & Moseley)🟡 Similar—
Concept Moseley bombarded different metal targets with high-speed electrons and measured the frequency of the X-rays they emitted. He found that the square root of the frequency varies linearly with a whole number characteristic of the element — the atomic number Z, i.e. the nuclear charge. Since Z, not weight, is the fundamental property of an atom, the periodic law had to be restated in terms of Z. Doing so instantly repaired every anomalous pair in Mendeleev's table.
Statement & relation Moseley's law: √ν ∝ Z ν = frequency of the characteristic X-rays emitted, Z = atomic number; the straight-line fit proved Z is a real, integral physical quantity. Modern periodic law: "The physical and chemical properties of elements are the periodic function of their atomic number."
Solution
1. Describe the experiment: high-speed electrons were fired at various metal surfaces, producing characteristic X-rays.
2. State the finding: √ν ∝ Z — a clean straight line, whereas plotting against atomic weight gave a scattered curve.A whole-number, scatter-free relationship identifies Z as the true fundamental property of an atom.
3. Conclude the law: properties of elements are a periodic function of atomic number — arrange elements in increasing Z and similar properties recur at regular intervals.
4. Point to the pay-off: the anomalous pairs vanish, because K (19) genuinely follows Ar (18) and Ni (28) genuinely follows Co (27) on the Z scale.
5. Add the second pay-off: all isotopes of an element share one Z, so they automatically occupy one and the same position.
 Moseley's experiment : e⁻ beam → metal target → characteristic X-rays
 finding              : √ν ∝ Z      (linear, whole-number)
 MODERN PERIODIC LAW  : properties = periodic function of ATOMIC NUMBER
 repairs              : Ar(18)<K(19), Co(27)<Ni(28), Te(52)<I(53) — no anomaly
                        isotopes share one Z → one position
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question mentions X-rays + metal target + √frequency, the answer is Moseley and the atomic number. Write the relation as √ν ∝ Z (not ν ∝ Z) — the square root is the detail examiners check. Trap: crediting the modern law to Mendeleev; Mendeleev's table was modified using Moseley's result, but the law itself is Moseley's.
Key point: Modern periodic law — the physical and chemical properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic number (Moseley, from √ν ∝ Z).
4State the position and explain the electronic configuration of s, p, d and f blocks.s, p, d, f blocksWrite the general electronic configuration of s, p, d and f block elements🟢 Exact—
Concept The long-form table is carved into four blocks by a single criterion: the type of orbital the last electron enters. Because an s-subshell holds 2 electrons, a p-subshell 6, a d-subshell 10 and an f-subshell 14, the widths of the four blocks are fixed at 2, 6, 10 and 14 columns respectively. That is why the table has exactly 2 + 6 + 10 = 18 groups in its main body, with the 14-wide f-block pulled out below.
Key relations s-block: ns1–2; p-block: ns² np1–6; d-block: (n−1)d1–10 ns0–2; f-block: (n−2)f1–14 (n−1)d0–1 ns². Note the shells involved: s and p fill the outermost shell (n), d fills the penultimate shell (n−1), and f fills the anti-penultimate shell (n−2) — which is exactly why d elements are "transition" and f elements "inner transition".
Solution
1. s-block — position: extreme left of the table, groups 1 and 2 (alkali and alkaline-earth metals), 2 columns.An s-subshell has one orbital holding at most 2 electrons, so only 2 groups can exist. Configuration ns¹⁻²; these are soft metals with low melting and boiling points.
2. p-block — position: extreme right, groups 13 to 18, 6 columns.A p-subshell has 3 orbitals holding 6 electrons ⇒ 6 groups. Configuration ns²np¹⁻⁶; mostly non-metals, but metals and metalloids also occur here.
3. d-block (transition) — position: the middle of the table, groups 3 to 12, 10 columns.A d-subshell has 5 orbitals holding 10 electrons ⇒ 10 groups. Configuration (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns⁰⁻²; all are metals with high melting/boiling points, most form coloured ions and show variable valency. Their properties are midway between s- and p-block, hence "transition".
4. f-block (inner transition) — position: two separate rows at the bottom of the table, 14 columns.An f-subshell has 7 orbitals holding 14 electrons ⇒ 14 elements per series. 4f series = lanthanoids, Ce(58) to Lu(71); 5f series = actinoids, Th(90) to Lr(103). All are metals; lanthanoids are the "rare earths".
5. Outer configurations of the two f-series: 4f1–145d0–16s² (lanthanoids) and 5f1–146d0–17s² (actinoids).

3Who plotted a curve of atomic volume vs atomic mass?Lothar Meyer's curveLothar Meyer's arrangement — periodic table, Class 11🟡 SimilarcMeyer
Concept Lothar Meyer (1869) took a purely physical route to periodicity: instead of comparing chemical reactions he plotted a measurable bulk property, atomic volume, against atomic weight. The resulting graph is not a smooth line but a repeating pattern of sharp peaks and troughs — visual, undeniable proof that physical properties are a periodic function of atomic weight. This curve became the experimental foundation on which Mendeleev's table rested.
Key relation atomic volume = gram atomic mass ÷ density, plotted on the y-axis against atomic weight on the x-axis. Concept The four-block division is defined by a single criterion, stated verbatim in the theory: "depending upon the nature of atomic orbitals in which the last electron enters, the elements can be classified into four blocks." Nothing else — not neutron count, not shell count, not mass — enters the definition. The names of the blocks are literally the names of those orbitals.
Key relation last e⁻ in ns → s-block · np → p-block · (n−1)d → d-block · (n−2)f → f-block. Because s, p, d and f sub-shells hold 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons, the blocks are 2, 6, 10 and 14 columns wide — the block widths are a direct consequence of the same criterion.
Solution
1. Recall the definition of a block: it is set by the type of orbital receiving the last electron — option (b).
2. Verify with one example from each block:

  • Cl = [Ne]3s²3p⁵ — last electron in p ⇒ p-block.
  • Sc = [Ar]3d¹4s² — last electron in 3d, the penultimate shell ⇒ d-block.
  • Ce = [Xe]4f¹5d¹6s² — last electron in 4f, the anti-penultimate shell ⇒ f-block.
3. Reject the alternatives, each of which fails a simple test:
  • (a) Neutrons — isotopes of one element have different neutron counts but occupy the same position, so neutrons cannot determine it.
  • (c) Total number of filled shells — that gives the period, not the block; Na and Cl share three shells but lie in different blocks.
  • (d) Mass number — that is protons + neutrons, and the anomalous pairs (Ar–K, Te–I) prove mass ordering is unreliable.

6Why are there exactly 14 lanthanoids and 14 actinoids in the periodic table?f-block capacityLanthanides and actinides — f-block elements🟡 SimilaraBecause f-orbitals can hold a maximum of 14 electrons
Concept The length of any block equals the electron capacity of the sub-shell being filled, because each added electron produces one new element. An f-sub-shell has 7 orbitals (the magnetic quantum number m runs −3 … 0 … +3), and each orbital holds 2 electrons, so the capacity is 7 × 2 = 14. Filling 4f therefore generates exactly 14 lanthanoids, and filling 5f exactly 14 actinoids.
Formula For azimuthal quantum number l: number of orbitals = 2l + 1 and maximum electrons = 2(2l + 1). For f, l = 3 ⇒ orbitals = 2(3) + 1 = 7 ⇒ electrons = 14. The same formula gives s (l = 0) → 2, p (l = 1) → 6, d (l = 2) → 10.
Solution
1. For an f-sub-shell, l = 3, so the number of orbitals = 2l + 1 = 7.
2. By the Pauli exclusion principle each orbital holds at most 2 electrons ⇒ capacity = 7 × 2 = 14 electrons.
3. Each electron added creates one new element, so an f-series must contain exactly 14 elements — option (a).
4. Verify against the actual series: lanthanoids run from Ce (58) to Lu (71) — that is 71 − 58 + 1 = 14; actinoids run from Th (90) to Lr (103) — 103 − 90 + 1 = 14. Both check out.
5. Reject the rest: (b) is false — many more than 14 radioactive elements exist (all actinoids and all transuranics are radioactive); (c) is false and irrelevant to counting; (d) inverts the geometry — the f-block is 14 columns wide across 2 series, not "7 groups and 2 periods".

Concept "Main group" means the s- and p-block elements. In Mendeleev's numbering (groups I to VII plus the zero group), the group number was meant to equal the count of outermost electrons — and for the s-block it always does (group I → 1 electron, group II → 2). The rule breaks in the p-block, most glaringly for the noble gases: they were placed in the zero group yet carry 8 outermost electrons. Zero versus eight is the clearest mismatch in the whole table.
Key relation s-block: group number = valence electrons ✓ always. p-block: group number ≠ valence electrons for the zero group (0 vs 8), and in modern numbering the whole p-block needs group = 10 + valence electrons. Mendeleev had no place for the noble gases when he built the table — they were discovered later and appended as a separate "zero" group, breaking the correspondence.
Solution
1. Check the s-block against Mendeleev's numbering: group I holds Li, Na, K with 1 valence electron each; group II holds Be, Mg, Ca with 2. The numbers match exactly.
2. Check the p-block. Groups III to VII still match (III → 3 electrons, VII → 7), but the zero group / group VIII-type noble gases are numbered 0 while carrying 8 outermost electrons (Ne 2s²2p⁶, Ar 3s²3p⁶).Helium is worse still — group 0 with only 2 electrons — and the theory notes group 0/18 is where "elements have generally ZERO valency".
3. Since noble gases are p-block elements, it is the p-block whose group number departs from the outermost-electron count — option (b).
4. Reinforce with modern numbering: in the 18-group table every p-block element needs the correction group = 10 + valence electrons (O has 6 valence electrons but sits in group 16), whereas s-block elements need no correction at all.
5. Reject the rest: (a) s-block always matches; (c) and (d) are not main-group elements — the question restricts itself to main groups, so d- and f-block options are out of scope by definition.
