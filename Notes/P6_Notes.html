The Three Laws & Conventions

LawStatement
First (inertia)If Fnet = 0, a body stays at rest or moves with constant velocity (a = 0). Frame where this holds = inertial.
SecondFnet = m·a = dp/dt. Acceleration ∝ net force, along the net force.
ThirdAction & reaction are equal, opposite, on different bodies — they never cancel.
working relationsFnet = m·a · Weight W = m·g · Tension pulls, Normal pushes (⊥ surface)
★ Sign conventionFix a positive direction, resolve every force, then apply ΣF = ma per body. The book takes g = 10 m·s⁻² in most numericals.
💡 Solve-it recipe(1) isolate each body, (2) draw its FBD (weight, normal, tension, spring, applied, friction), (3) write ΣF = ma along & ⊥ to motion, (4) add constraint relations to close the system.
L-1

Newton's First Law, Inertia & the FBD

  • First law / law of inertia: motion changes only when an unbalanced external force acts. Fnet = 0 ⇒ a = 0 (rest or uniform velocity).
  • Inertia = tendency to resist change of motion; it depends only on mass (more mass → more inertia).
  • Inertial frame: first law is valid (non-accelerating). Non-inertial frame: accelerating/rotating — first law fails.

Internal vs external forces

  • Internal (conservative): gravity, spring, electric, magnetic — change the form of mechanical energy, not its total.
  • External (non-conservative): applied, normal, tension, friction, air drag — change the total mechanical energy.
  • A force is internal/external relative to the chosen system; e.g. the normal pair between two stacked blocks is internal to the pair.
Rules for a clean FBD
Draw the body freed from its surroundings, then add only external forces on it: weight from the centre of mass, a normal ⊥ to every contact surface, tension (always a pull) along each string, spring pull/thrust, friction along the surface opposing relative motion, and any applied force. Never draw both members of an action–reaction pair on the same body. Then decide: balanced (Fnet = 0 → first law) or accelerating (Fnet ≠ 0 → second law).
⚠ TrapThe normal force is not always equal to mg — it equals whatever the ΣF equation gives (inclines, lifts, extra pushes all change it).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-1
Two blocks of 4 kg and 2 kg are placed side by side on a smooth horizontal surface. A horizontal 20 N force is applied on the 4 kg block. Find the normal (contact) reaction between the two blocks.
Treat both as one system: a = F/(m₁+m₂) = 20/(4+2) = 10/3 m·s⁻².
The contact force on the 2 kg block equals its mass × a: N = m₂·a = 2 × 10/3.
N = 20/3 N ≈ 6.67 N
Book Example · L-1
A block m₁ rests on a smooth incline (angle θ) and is joined over a pulley to a hanging block m₂; the system is in equilibrium. Draw all the forces acting on it.
On m₁: normal N ⊥ to the incline, tension T up along the incline, weight m₁g vertically down.
On m₂: tension T up, weight m₂g down (equilibrium ⇒ T = m₂g and T = m₁g sinθ).
Forces are N, T, m₁g (on the incline block) and T, m₂g (on the hanging block)
L-2

Problems Based on Newton's Laws

concurrent equilibriumΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 (Lami: F₁/sinα = F₂/sinβ = F₃/sinγ)
blocks in a row (force F, total M)a = Fnet/Mtotal · contact force on a block = (its mass) × a
💡 MethodFor blocks pushed in contact: find the common a from the whole system, then isolate one block to get any internal contact/normal force.
Why the contact force differs from the applied force
When F is applied to the first block of a train, each block only needs enough force to give itself the common acceleration a. So the force transmitted across a junction = (mass on the far side of that junction) × a — it is generally less than F, and depends on which block you isolate.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-2
A 40 kg object hangs from a junction held by three cords; one cord makes 37° and another 53° with the horizontal, the third is vertical. Find the three tensions (g = 10).
Vertical cord: T₃ = mg = 40 × 10 = 400 N.
At the junction: T₁cos37° = T₂cos53° and T₁sin37° + T₂sin53° = 400. Solving with cos37° = 0.8, cos53° = 0.6.
T₁ = 240 N, T₂ = 320 N, T₃ = 400 N
Book Example · L-2
Three blocks 3 kg, 2 kg, 1 kg are placed in contact on a smooth surface; a 12 N force is applied to the 3 kg block. Find the net force on the 2 kg block.
a = 12/(3+2+1) = 2 m·s⁻². Net force on the 2 kg block = m·a = 2 × 2.
4 N
Book Example · L-2
Blocks 1 kg, 4 kg, 2 kg lie in contact; 120 N pushes from the left and 50 N opposes from the right. Find (a) the acceleration and (b) the net force on the 2 kg block; also (c) the normal reaction between the 1 kg and 4 kg blocks.
a = (120 − 50)/(1+4+2) = 70/7 = 10 m·s⁻². Net force on 2 kg = 2 × 10 = 20 N.
Isolate the 1 kg block: 120 − N = 1 × 10 ⇒ N = 110 N.
a = 10 m·s⁻²; net force on 2 kg = 20 N; contact N(1–4 kg) = 110 N
Book Example · L-2
Two blocks 3 kg and 4 kg are in contact on a smooth floor; a 5 N horizontal force pushes the 3 kg block. Find the acceleration and the contact force between them.
a = 5/(3+4) = 5/7 m·s⁻². Contact force = m₂·a = 4 × 5/7.
a = 5/7 m·s⁻²; contact force = 20/7 N ≈ 2.86 N
L-3

Constraint Motion (String & Pulley Constraints)

  • An inextensible string / rigid link forces a fixed relation between the bodies' displacements, hence between their velocities and accelerations.
  • Method: write total string length in terms of the position coordinates, then differentiate twice — the length is constant so its derivatives are zero.
movable-pulley constrainty₁ + 2y₂ = constant ⇒ v₁ = −2v₂, a₁ = −2a₂
Standard movable-pulley result (block m₁ on the free end, block m₂ on the movable pulley)
With a₁ = 2a₂ the FBDs give m₁g − T = m₁(2a) and 2T − m₂g = m₂a, leading to a = (2m₁ − m₂)g /(4m₁ + m₂) and T = 3m₁m₂g /(4m₁ + m₂). If instead y₁ + 3y₂ = const (three supporting segments), then a₁ = 3a₂ and, with masses m and M, a = (3m − M)g /(9m + M), T = 4mMg /(9m + M).
★ Speed/accel ratioThe body on the movable pulley moves at half the speed (and half the acceleration) of the body on the free string end — the factor equals the number of string segments supporting the pulley.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
In a multi-pulley arrangement a plank has acceleration a₁ and a hanging block has acceleration a₄, linked through intermediate movable pulleys. Using the string constraints, find a₄ in terms of a₁.
Length constraints give a₁ + a₂ = 0, a₁ + a₃ − 2a₂ = 0, a₁ − 2a₃ + a₄ = 0.
From the first, a₂ = −a₁ ⇒ a₃ = −3a₁ ⇒ a₄ = 2a₃ − a₁ = −7a₁.
a₄ = −7a₁ (7× the magnitude, opposite sense)
L-4

String, Pulley & Wedge Constraint Problems

wedge (contact) constraintaalong common normal matches ⇒ aA = aB sinθ
  • For a block staying in contact with a moving wedge, equate the components of the two accelerations along the surface normal.
  • Movable-pulley links again halve the acceleration; write one ΣF equation per body plus the constraint.
Wedge-on-floor family of results
A rod pushed vertically by a wedge of mass M gives wedge acceleration a₂ = mg cosα sinα /(m sin²α + M) and rod acceleration a₁ = mg cosα sin²α /(m sin²α + M). A block of mass m on a free wedge of the same mass accelerates with a = 2g sinθ /(1 + sin²θ). For a wedge-to-rod mass ratio η, the wedge acceleration is aB = g tanθ /(η + tan²θ).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-4
A system of m₁ = 1 kg, m₂ = 2 kg and m₃ = 3 kg with a movable pulley is released from rest (T₁ = 2T on the 1 kg block). Find the tensions and the acceleration of each mass (g = 10).
Equations: 2T − 10 = a; T − 20 = 2(ar − a); 30 − T = 3(ar + a). Solving gives a = 6.55, ar = 0.7, T = 8.3 N.
Accelerations: m₁ = 6.55 m·s⁻², m₂ = 5.85 m·s⁻² up, m₃ = 7.25 m·s⁻² down.
T₂ = 8.3 N, T₁ = 16.6 N; a₁ = 6.55, a₂ = 5.85↑, a₃ = 7.25↓ (m·s⁻²)
Book Example · L-4
A 2 kg block on a smooth table is connected through a movable pulley to a 1 kg hanging block (tension on the 2 kg block is 2T). Find the acceleration of the 1 kg block and the tension (g = 10).
Constraint: a(2 kg) = a/2 with a = a(1 kg). Equations: 10 − T = 1·a and 2T = 2·(a/2) = a ⇒ T = a/2.
So 10 − a/2 = a ⇒ a = 20/3 m·s⁻², T = 10/3 N.
a = 20/3 m·s⁻² ≈ 6.67 m·s⁻²; T = 10/3 N
Book Example · L-4
A block of mass M rests on a smooth 30° incline and is joined through a movable pulley to a hanging block 2M. Find the acceleration of M.
With a(2M) = a/2 and tensions T (on M) and 2T (on 2M): Mg − T = M(a/2)-form gives T − Mg sin30° = Ma and Mg − T = M(a/2).
Adding: Mg − Mg/2 = 3Ma/2 ⇒ Mg/2 = 3Ma/2 ⇒ a = g/3.
a = g/3 ≈ 3.33 m·s⁻²
Book Example · L-4
A block A of mass m rests on a smooth wedge B of the same mass m (incline angle θ); B is free to slide on a smooth floor. Find the acceleration of the block relative to the ground.
Wedge constraint aA = aB sinθ combined with the two ΣF equations gives the block acceleration a = 2g sinθ /(1 + sin²θ).
Displacement of A in the first second: s = ½·a·(1)² = g sin²θ /(1 + sin²θ).
a = 2g sinθ /(1 + sin²θ); s(1 s) = g sin²θ /(1 + sin²θ)
L-5

Spring Force & the Spring Balance

Hooke's lawF = −k·x (restoring) ⇒ a = −(k/m)·x · elongation x = mg/k (static hang)
  • A spring pulls when stretched and pushes when compressed; magnitude = k × (deformation from natural length).
  • A light spring balance reads the tension passing through it — the same on both hooks; its own weight does not change that reading.
  • Cutting a spring of stiffness k into parts: k ∝ 1/length ⇒ shorter piece is stiffer (kₚ = k·L/Lₚ).
⚠ TrapA spring balance does not read the weight — it reads the tension. Only in static single-mass hanging is that tension equal to mg.
Reading of a balance in an accelerating chain
Put the balance between two masses over pulleys. If the two masses are equal the system is static and the reading = mg. If they are unequal the whole system accelerates at a = (m₂ − m₁)g /(m₁ + m₂), and the balance reads the string tension T = m₁(g + a) — different from either weight.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-5
A light spring balance connects two 20 kg blocks that hang over frictionless pulleys on either side (g = 10). (a) What does the balance read? (b) Does the reading change if the balance is heavy? (c) What if the two masses are unequal?
(a) Equal masses ⇒ system static ⇒ tension = 20g = 200 N.
(b) No — the balance reads the tension in the string, independent of its own weight. (c) Unequal masses accelerate the system, so the tension (and the reading) changes.
(a) 200 N (b) unchanged (c) reading changes (system accelerates)
Book Example · L-5
A 10 kg block is suspended in series through two light spring balances, one below the other. What does each balance read (g = 10)?
Both balances carry the same tension supporting the 10 kg block: T = T′ = 10 × 10 = 100 N.
Both read 10 kg (100 N each)
Book Example · L-5
A spring balance is placed between a 5 kg and a 10 kg block hanging over two pulleys (g = 10). Find its reading.
a = (m₂ − m₁)g /(m₁ + m₂) = (100 − 50)/15 = 10/3 m·s⁻².
Reading = tension = m₁(g + a) = 5(10 + 10/3) = 200/3 N.
200/3 N ≈ 66.7 N
L-6

Spring Combinations & Sudden Cutting

combinationsSeries: 1/keq = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂ (softer) · Parallel: keq = k₁ + k₂ (stiffer)
★ Spring can't change instantlyAt the instant a support is cut, a spring's force stays the same (its length can't jump), whereas a string's tension can vanish instantly. This is the key to "just after cutting" problems.
Just-after-cutting logic
Immediately after a cut, keep every spring force at its pre-cut value (deformation is continuous) but set any cut string tension to zero. Then re-apply ΣF = ma to each body to get its instantaneous acceleration. A freely released body (nothing holding it) simply has a = g downward.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-6
A 2 kg block hangs from a spring of k = 100 N·m⁻¹ (g = 10). A further 1 kg is added. Find the extra elongation produced.
Initial x = mg/k = (2×10)/100 = 0.2 m. New load 3 kg ⇒ x′ = (3×10)/100 = 0.3 m.
Extra elongation = 0.3 − 0.2.
0.1 m
Book Example · L-6
A block 2m hangs from a spring (k) and a block m hangs from 2m by a string, all in equilibrium. The string is cut. Find the acceleration of 2m just after cutting (g).
Before cut: string tension T = mg, so spring force kx = T + 2mg = 3mg (unchanged just after cutting).
For 2m after cut: kx − 2mg = 2m·a ⇒ 3mg − 2mg = 2m·a ⇒ a = g/2 (upward). The lower block m then falls freely at g.
2m: a = g/2 upward; m: a = g downward
Book Example · L-6
A 10 kg block hangs from the ceiling by spring A; a 5 kg block hangs from the 10 kg block by spring B (g = 10, so TA = 150 N, TB = 50 N). Find the instantaneous acceleration when (i) spring B is cut, (ii) spring A is cut.
(i) B cut: spring A force (150 N) is unchanged. For the 10 kg block: a = (TA − 10g)/10 = (150 − 100)/10 = 5 m·s⁻² up.
(ii) A cut: spring B force (50 N) unchanged. 5 kg block: (TB − 5g)/5 = (50 − 50)/5 = 0. 10 kg block: (TB + 10g)/10 = (50 + 100)/10 = 15 m·s⁻² down.
(i) 10 kg: 5 m·s⁻² up. (ii) 5 kg: 0; 10 kg: 15 m·s⁻² down
L-7

Pseudo Force & Non-Inertial Frames

non-inertial frame (accel a₀)a = (F − m·a₀)/m ⇒ add pseudo force Fpseudo = −m·a₀ on every body
  • To use Newton's laws inside an accelerating frame, add a pseudo force −m·a₀ (opposite to the frame's acceleration) to each body, then solve as if the frame were inertial (d'Alembert).
  • Centrifugal force is a pseudo force. A frame with constant velocity is inertial ⇒ pseudo force = 0.
💡 Lift shortcutInside a lift accelerating with a₀, gravity is effectively replaced by geff = g ± a₀ (+ when the lift accelerates up). All incline/pendulum results follow by swapping g → geff.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-7
A pendulum hangs from the ceiling of a car that accelerates at a₀ relative to the road. Find the angle the string makes with the vertical.
In the car frame the bob is at rest under T, mg and the pseudo force ma₀. Horizontal: T sinθ = ma₀; vertical: T cosθ = mg.
Dividing: tanθ = a₀/g.
θ = tan⁻¹(a₀/g)
Book Example · L-7
A particle slides down a smooth incline of angle θ fixed inside an elevator that accelerates upward at a₀. The incline base has length L. Find the time to reach the bottom.
Pseudo force ma₀ acts downward, so geff = g + a₀ and aalong incline = (g + a₀)sinθ.
Distance along incline = L/cosθ = ½·(g + a₀)sinθ·t².
t = √[ 2L / ((g + a₀) sinθ cosθ) ]
Book Example · L-7
A block slides down a frictionless incline of angle θ and length ℓ inside an elevator moving upward with uniform velocity v. Find the time to slide down.
Uniform velocity ⇒ frame is inertial ⇒ pseudo force = 0, so a = g sinθ (as on the ground).
ℓ = ½·g sinθ·t².
t = √( 2ℓ / (g sinθ) )
