L-1

Work Done by a Constant Force

  • Work is done when a force produces a displacement. For a constant force it is the dot product of force and displacement:
work by a constant forceW = F⃗·S⃗ = F S cosθ   (θ = angle between force and displacement)
  • Scalar quantity. SI unit joule (J) = N·m; dimensions [ML²T⁻²]. 1 J = work by 1 N over 1 m along the force.
  • In rectangular components: W = FxSx + FySy + FzSz.

Sign of work — the three cases

  • θ = 0° (force ∥ displacement): W = +FS — maximum positive work (e.g. gravity on a falling body).
  • θ = 90° (force ⊥ displacement): W = 0 — zero work (e.g. normal reaction, weight of a body moving horizontally, centripetal force).
  • θ = 180° (force opposite): W = −FS — negative work (e.g. friction, air resistance opposing motion).
💡 Zero-work trapsNo displacement ⇒ no work (holding a weight still). Displacement ⊥ force ⇒ no work (carrying a bag horizontally; a satellite in circular orbit).
⚠ TrapWork is done against gravity when you lift a body: the lifting force does +mgh, gravity does −mgh. Don't confuse "work by the force" with "work by gravity" — they carry opposite signs here.

Book Example · L-1
A body moves from A(2, 4, −6) to B(6, −4, 2) m under a constant force F⃗ = 2î + 3ĵ − k̂ (N). Find the work done.

L-2

Work Done by a Variable Force

  • When the force changes with position, split the path into tiny steps ds, add up F·ds. In the limit this is an integral:
variable forceW = ∫F⃗·ds⃗  ·  in 1-D: W = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx
graphical meaningW = area under the F–x graph (area above the axis is +ve work, below is −ve)

Work done by a spring

spring force & spring workF = −kx  ·  Wspring = −½k(x₂² − x₁²)  ·  work by an external agent = +½k(x₂² − x₁²)
Why the spring integral
The spring pulls back with F = −kx. Work done by the spring as the free end goes from x₁ to x₂ is ∫x₁x₂(−kx)dx = −½k(x₂² − x₁²). Stretching or compressing (|x₂| > |x₁|) gives negative spring work, so the agent must supply the same amount positively — this stored energy is the elastic PE ½kx².
💡 Read the graphFor a straight-line (linearly varying) force between the limits, the area is a trapezium: W = ½(F₁ + F₂)·(x₂ − x₁). Triangles below the axis subtract.

L-3

Work by Gravity & Friction · Kinetic Energy

Work done by gravity and friction

gravityWgravity = −mgh (rising) = +mgh (falling)  — depends only on vertical drop h, not on the path or launch angle
friction (on a level surface)Wfriction = −f·l = −μN·l  (always opposes motion)
  • Gravity is a conservative force — its work is path-independent and depends only on the change in height.
  • Friction is non-conservative — its work is always negative and depends on the actual distance travelled.

Kinetic energy and its link to momentum

kinetic energyKE = ½mv² = p²/2m  ·  p = mv = √(2m·KE)
Deriving KE = ½mv²
Starting from rest, a constant force does W = ∫F ds = ∫m(dv/dt)ds = ∫mv dv = ½mv². This stored energy of motion is the kinetic energy. Writing v = p/m gives the momentum form KE = p²/2m, handy when momentum is the known quantity.
💡 Percentage linksSince KE ∝ p²: if p changes by small x%, KE changes by ≈ 2x%; if KE changes by x%, p changes by ≈ x/2 %.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
A uniform rod of mass M and length L, lying flat, is raised to stand vertically. Find the work done against gravity.
The centre of mass rises by L/2. Work against gravity = Mg·(L/2).
W = MgL/2
Book Example · L-3
A 1 kg block on a rough incline (μ = 0.25, θ = 30°) slides down a length l = 5 m. Find the work done by friction. (g = 10)
Driving component mg sinθ = 10(0.5) = 5 N; friction f = μmg cosθ = 0.25(10)(√3/2) = 2.165 N.
Since mg sinθ > f the block slides, friction acts up the slope: Wf = −f·l = −(2.165)(5).
Wfriction = −10.825 J
Book Example · L-3
The momentum of a particle is increased by 50%. By what percentage does its kinetic energy increase?
KE ∝ p². p′ = 1.5p ⇒ KE′ = (1.5)²KE = 2.25 KE.
KE increases by 125%
Book Example · L-3
The kinetic energy of a particle is increased by 1%. By what percentage does its momentum increase?
p = √(2m·KE) ⇒ p ∝ √KE. p′ = √1.01 · p ≈ 1.005 p.
Momentum increases by ≈ 0.5%
Book Example · L-3
A 2 kg ball is dropped from a height of 5 m. Find its kinetic energy and momentum just before hitting the ground. (g = 10)
Energy conservation: KE = mgh = 2(10)(5) = 100 J.
p = √(2m·KE) = √(2·2·100) = √400.
KE = 100 J, p = 20 kg·m s⁻¹
Book Example · L-3
The kinetic energy of a particle is decreased by 2%. By what percentage does its momentum decrease?
Δp/p = ½·(ΔKE/KE) = ½ × 2% = 1%.
Momentum decreases by 1%
L-4

The Work–Energy Theorem

work–energy principleWnet = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu²
  • The total work done by all forces on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy. Derived from v² = u² + 2as: ½mv² − ½mu² = ma·s = F·s = W.
  • It holds for a variable force too: Kf − Ki = ∫xixf F dx (since dK/dt = F·v ⇒ dK = F dx).
inertial frameΣWexternal + ΣWinternal = ΔKE
non-inertial frameWext + Wint + Wpseudo + Wother = ΔKE  ·  if body at rest in the frame: Wreal = −Wpseudo
★ Why it is powerfulThe theorem hides all the messy time-details: you only need start and end speeds and the total work. Normal reaction and any force ⊥ to the motion drop out because they do no work.
⚠ TrapWnet is the work by every force (applied + gravity + friction + spring). A common error is to equate only the applied force's work to ΔKE and forget friction or gravity.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-4
A 5 kg object falls from rest through 20 m and reaches 10 m s⁻¹. How much work is done by air resistance? (g = 10)
Wair + Wgravity = ΔKE ⇒ Wair + mgh = ½mv² − 0.
Wair + (5)(10)(20) = ½(5)(10²) ⇒ Wair + 1000 = 250.
Wair = −750 J
Book Example · L-4
The displacement of a 2 kg particle varies as s = (2t² − 2t + 10) m. Find the total work done on it from t = 0 to t = 2 s.
v = ds/dt = 4t − 2. At t = 0: u = −2 m s⁻¹; at t = 2 s: v = 6 m s⁻¹.
Wnet = ½m(v² − u²) = ½(2)(36 − 4) = 32 J.
Wnet = 32 J
Book Example · L-4
A block of mass m starts with speed u toward a spring (constant k) a distance l₀ away on a rough floor (coefficient μ), striking and compressing it to rest. Find the maximum compression x.
Wnet = ΔKE: −μmg(l₀ + x) − ½kx² = 0 − ½mu².
⇒ kx² + 2μmg·x + (2μmg·l₀ − mu²) = 0 (quadratic in x).
x = [ −2μmg + √(4μ²m²g² − 4k(2μmg·l₀ − mu²)) ] / (2k)
Book Example · L-4
A block slides along a frictionless track through a valley to a higher level; the top level is rough (μ = 0.6) and a height h = 1.1 m above the start. Initial speed v₀ = 6 m s⁻¹. How far d does it travel on the rough top before stopping? (g = 10)
Wfriction + Wgravity = ΔKE: −μmg·d − mgh = 0 − ½mv₀².
d = (v₀² − 2gh)/(2μg) = (36 − 2·10·1.1)/(2·0.6·10) = (36 − 22)/12 = 14/12.
d = 1.167 m
L-5

Conservative Forces · F = −dU/dx · Equilibrium

Conservative vs non-conservative forces

  • Conservative: work depends only on start and end points, not the path (round-trip work = 0). Examples: gravity, spring restoring force, electrostatic, buoyancy. Every conservative force has an associated potential energy.
  • Non-conservative (dissipative): work depends on the path; energy is lost as heat/sound. Examples: friction, air/water drag, viscosity. A round trip against friction over length l does −2μmgl (not zero).
2-D conservative testforce is conservative if  ∂Fx/∂y = ∂Fy/∂x

Force from potential energy

force = −gradient of UΔU = −∫F⃗·dr⃗ = −W  ·  Fx = −∂U/∂x, Fy = −∂U/∂y, Fz = −∂U/∂z

Types of equilibrium

TypeConditionBehaviour when displaced
StabledU/dr = 0, d²U/dr² > 0 (U minimum)net restoring force pushes it back
UnstabledU/dr = 0, d²U/dr² < 0 (U maximum)net force drives it further away
NeutraldU/dr = 0, d²U/dr² = 0 (U constant)stays in equilibrium at the new spot
💡 Picture the U-curveA ball in a valley (min U) is stable; balanced on a hilltop (max U) is unstable; on a flat table (constant U) is neutral. The slope −dU/dx is the force pointing "downhill".

L-6

Potential Energy — Gravitational & Spring

  • Potential energy is stored energy by virtue of a body's position or configuration; released, it becomes kinetic energy. Only differences in PE matter — the zero level can be chosen freely.
gravitational PEU = mgh  (h = height of the centre of mass above the chosen reference; U is negative below it)
elastic (spring) PEU = ½kx²  (= −Wspring, the work an agent stores in stretching/compressing by x)
★ Key equalitiesDrop of height h releases mgh; a spring compressed/stretched by x stores ½kx². In an energy balance, the whole PE of one kind converts into KE, other PE, or friction heat.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-6

L-7

Mechanical Energy & Its Conservation

  • Mechanical energy = kinetic + potential. If only conservative forces act (no friction, no external push), the total mechanical energy stays constant: PE lost = KE gained.
conservation of mechanical energyUi + KEi = Uf + KEf  ⇒  ½mv² change = −(Uf − Ui)
oscillating spring–block½m(v₂² − v₁²) = ½k(x₁² − x₂²)  — KE lost by the block = PE gained by the spring
⚠ Use it only when valid"No external force" means no friction and no engine/hand pushing; the body's own weight is not an external force. The moment friction enters, switch to the work–energy theorem with the friction term.

A heavy particle on a string of length ℓ is given a horizontal speed v₀ at the lowest point. The string goes slack at some angle and the particle then flies as a projectile through the point of suspension. Find v₀.
At the slack point (angle θ above horizontal from centre): tension 0 ⇒ mg cosθ = mv²/ℓ ⇒ v² = gℓ cosθ.
Energy: ½mv₀² = ½mv² + mgℓ(1 + cosθ) ⇒ v₀² = gℓ(2 + 3cosθ).
Requiring the parabola to pass through the centre gives tanθ = √2 ⇒ cosθ = 1/√3, so v₀² = gℓ(2 + 3/√3) = gℓ(2 + √3).
v₀ = √(gℓ(2 + √3))
Book Example · L-7
A block (mass m) on a frictionless table is pushed against a wall-fixed spring (constant k, natural length L₀), compressing it to L₀/2, then released. Find the block's speed as a function of its distance x from the wall. (Note: this problem is printed twice in the module — as Example-4 and Example-6.)
Free end at natural length is the origin. x₁ = −L₀/2, x₂ = −(L₀ − x), v₁ = 0, v₂ = v.
½m(v² − 0) = ½k(x₁² − x₂²) = ½k[(L₀/2)² − (L₀ − x)²].
v = √( (k/m)[ (L₀/2)² − (L₀ − x)² ] ). Once the spring reaches its natural length (x = L₀), v = √(k/m)·(L₀/2).
v = √( (k/m)[ (L₀/2)² − (L₀ − x)² ] ); at x = L₀, v = (L₀/2)√(k/m)
Book Example · L-7
A 100 g block is pressed against a horizontal spring (k = 100 N m⁻¹), compressing it 5.0 cm. Released, it leaves the spring and flies off a table 2 m high. Where does it land? (g = 10)
Speed leaving spring: ½mv² = ½kx² ⇒ v = √(kx²/m) = √(100·0.05²/0.1) = 5/√10 ≈ 1.58 m s⁻¹.
Fall time: t = √(2h/g) = √(4/10) = 2/√10 s. Range R = v·t = (5/√10)(2/√10) = 10/10.

L-8

Power

average & instantaneous powerPavg = W/t = F·d/t = F·v  ·  Pinst = dW/dt = F⃗·v⃗
  • Power is the rate of doing work. SI unit watt (W) = J s⁻¹; 1 hp = 746 W. Holding a weight still does no work, so delivers no power.
  • Constant work-rate ⇒ constant power. For a constant force F on a body of mass m from rest, v = (F/m)t so P = (F²/m)t — power grows linearly with time.

Power of a water-lifting pump

pump delivering water at rate dm/dt from depth h with exit speed vP = (dm/dt)(gh + v²/2)  ·  just lifting (v = 0): P = (dm/dt)gh
💡 EfficiencyFor an engine of efficiency η, input power Pin = Pout/η. The useful (output) power is the mgh (and ½mv²) actually given to the water.
