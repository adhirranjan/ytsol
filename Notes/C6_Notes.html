L-1

System, Surroundings & State Functions

  • Thermodynamics (Greek: thermo = heat, dynamics = motion) studies the different forms of energy and the quantitative relations between them.
  • Scope: feasibility/spontaneity of a process, its extent (how far before equilibrium) and its efficiency.
  • System = specified portion of matter under study, separated from the rest of the universe by a bounding surface. Surroundings = rest of the universe able to exchange energy/matter. Boundary = real or imaginary surface separating them (fixed or movable).
  • Universe = system + surroundings.

Types of system

TypeExchanges with surroundingsExample
OpenMatter and energyOpen cup of coffee; human body; food cooked in an open vessel

IsolatedNeither matter nor energyCoffee in a closed, well-insulated thermos flask

State, state variables & state functions

  • State variables: measurable macroscopic properties that fix the state — mass, T, V, P etc.
  • State function: depends only on the initial & final state, not on the path/route taken. e.g. ΔU = Uf − Ui, ΔT = Tf − Ti. Common state functions: internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, free energy, P, T, V.
  • Path functions: heat (q) and work (w) — depend on how the change is carried out.
  • Macroscopic properties: apply to a system of a large number of particles (P, V, T, composition, density).
  • Extensive property depends on the amount of substance (mass, volume, energy, no. of moles, heat capacity, enthalpy, entropy, free energy). Intensive property is independent of amount (T, P, density, concentration, viscosity, surface tension, refractive index, molar volume, molarity, molality, mole fraction, molar enthalpy, specific heat, dielectric constant).
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium: macroscopic properties do not change with time (requires thermal + mechanical + chemical equilibrium simultaneously).

⚠ TrapHeat capacity is extensive but specific heat (per gram) and molar heat capacity are intensive. Likewise entropy (S) is extensive; molar entropy is intensive.
💡 TipA property is intensive if its value stays the same when you cut the system in half. Density, T and P survive the cut; mass, V and total energy do not.

Thermodynamic Processes · Internal Energy · First Law

A process is the operation by which a system changes from one state to another (always accompanied by a change in energy).

Internal energy (U or E)

  • Every system has a definite amount of energy stored in it — the internal energy: U = Etranslational + Erotational + Evibrational + Ebonding + Eelectronic + …
  • It is an extensive state property; depends on quantity of substance, chemical nature, T, P and V.
  • Absolute U cannot be measured; only ΔU = Uf − Ui matters (positive if Uf > Ui).

Heat & work

  • Heat (q): energy that flows because of a temperature difference — from hot to cold. Energy "on the move"; a path function.
  • Work (w): force × displacement; mechanical work of expansion w = F·dx = P·dV. Gravitational, electrical and mechanical work occur. Work appears only at the boundary during a change of state, and is a path function.
mechanical (P–V) workw = F × dx = −Pext dV (sign so that work done on the gas is positive)

Zeroth & first laws

  • Zeroth law (Fowler, 1931): if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Basis of the concept of temperature.
  • First law (Mayer & Helmholtz) = law of conservation of energy: energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only converted. Total energy of the universe (and of an isolated system) is constant; a perpetual-motion machine is impossible.
first law of thermodynamicsΔU = q + w (q = heat added to system, w = work done on system)
⚠ Sign trapThis module uses the IUPAC form ΔU = q + w, with w = work done ON the gas. Some books write ΔU = q − w (w = work done BY the gas). Check which convention a question uses before substituting.

First Law — Mathematical Form & Pressure–Volume Work

  • Mathematical statement: if a system going from state A→B absorbs heat q and has work w done on it, ΔU = UB − UA = q + w.
  • If work is done on the system (compression), w is positive ⇒ ΔU = q + w. If work is done by the system (expansion), that same quantity is negative, so ΔU = q − wby.
QuantityPositive when…Negative when…
Heat qabsorbed by systemevolved by system
Work wdone on system (compression)done by system (expansion)
Units of heat & work
1 cal = quantity of heat that raises 1 g of water by 1 °C. 1 J = 0.2390 cal; 1 cal = 4.184 J; 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ; 1 L·atm = 101.3 J = 24.2 cal.

Work of expansion (P–V work)

  • For a gas pushing a piston of area A through dl: dw = F·dl = P·A·dl = P dV. Over V1→V2, w = PextΔV.
  • Expansion (gas does work) ⇒ w = −PextΔV (negative). Compression (work done on gas) ⇒ positive.
irreversible P–V workw = −Pext(V2 − V1) = −PextΔV

Reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas

At each infinitesimal step Pext = Pgas − dP; dw = −(Pext − dP)dV ≈ −P dV. Integrating with P = nRT/V (ideal gas, T constant):

reversible isothermal workw = −∫V₁V₂ P dV = −nRT ln(V2/V1) = −2.303 nRT log(V2/V1) = −2.303 nRT log(P1/P2)
Free expansion & constant-volume heat
Free expansion = expansion against zero pressure (into vacuum): w = PextΔV = 0×ΔV = 0. Then ΔU = q + w = q. At constant volume, ΔV = 0 ⇒ w = 0 and ΔU = qv (heat change at constant volume).
⚠ TrapReversible work is the maximum obtainable. Against a fixed Pext (irreversible), use w = −PextΔV — not the log formula; the log formula is only for a reversible path.

Enthalpy · ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT · Exo/Endothermic

  • Enthalpy H = U + PV ("heat content"). An extensive state function; absolute H is unmeasurable, only ΔH = HB − HA = Hproducts − Hreactants.
  • At constant P: ΔH = ΔU + PΔV = ΔU + w. Combined with q = ΔU + w ⇒ ΔH = qp (heat change at constant pressure). At constant V (ΔV = 0): ΔU = qv.
enthalpy definitionsH = U + PV · ΔH = ΔU + PΔV · qp = ΔH, qv = ΔU

Relation between ΔH and ΔU for gaseous reactions

With PVA = nrRT and PVB = npRT, PΔV = (np − nr)RT = ΔngRT:

key thermochemical relationΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT · qp = qv + ΔngRT (Δng = moles gaseous products − reactants)
CaseExampleResult
Δng = 0H₂(g)+Cl₂(g)→2HCl(g); C(s)+O₂(g)→CO₂(g)ΔH = ΔU
Δng > 0PCl₅(g)⇌PCl₃(g)+Cl₂(g), Δn=+1; 2NH₃→N₂+3H₂, Δn=+2ΔH > ΔU
Δng < 02SO₂+O₂⇌2SO₃, Δn=−1; CO+½O₂⇌CO₂, Δn=−½ΔH < ΔU

Also ΔH = ΔU when the reaction runs in a closed vessel (ΔV = 0) or involves only solids/liquids (negligible volume change).

Standard enthalpy change

Standard state = 25 °C (298 K) and 1 atm. ΔH when all reactants and products are in their standard states is the standard enthalpy change ΔH° (or ΔH298).

Work by Graphical Methods · Adiabatic & Cyclic Processes

  • Irreversible isothermal change: q = −w = Pext(Vfinal − Vinitial) — work against a fixed external pressure.
  • Adiabatic process: q = 0 ⇒ ΔU = wadiabatic; temperature changes because all work comes from (or goes into) internal energy.

P–V diagrams & work as area

  • On a P–V plot the four basic processes fan out from a point: isochoric is vertical (V const), isobaric horizontal (P const); adiabatic is steeper than isothermal.
  • Work = area under the P–V curve. Single-step compression against Pext: w = Pext(Vf − Vi) > 0 (since Vf < Vi). Reversible path (many steps) traces the smooth curve — the shaded area under it.
  • Expansion: wexp = −Pext(V2 − V1) = negative work (area under curve, energy leaves the gas).

Cyclic process — work is the enclosed area

  • For a complete cycle ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0. Net work = area enclosed by the loop. For a triangular ABCA loop, work = ½ (V2 − V1)(P2 − P1).
  • Clockwise cycle ⇒ net work done by the gas (w negative). Anti-clockwise ⇒ net work done on the gas (w positive).
  • The three different A→B paths enclosing different areas prove that work is a path function, not a state function.
cyclic / adiabatic workAdiabatic: ΔU = w, q = 0 · Cyclic: ΔU = 0, wnet = −(enclosed area) · ABCA triangle: |w| = ½(V2−V1)(P2−P1)
💡 TipSteeper curve on a P–V plot = adiabatic (temperature drops on expansion, so pressure falls faster than for the isothermal at the same T).

Heat Capacity · Cp − Cv = R · Calorimetry · Kirchhoff

  • Heat capacity (thermal capacity) = heat to raise the system's temperature by 1° : C = dq/dT.
  • Specific heat capacity s = ΔQ/(mΔT) — heat to raise unit mass by 1°. Thermal capacity = m × s.
  • Molar heat capacity C = ΔQ/(nΔT) — heat per mole per 1° rise. At constant V: Cv = (∂U/∂T)V; at constant P: Cp = (∂H/∂T)P.
molar heat capacitiesCv = (ΔU/ΔT)V · Cp = (ΔH/ΔT)P

Mayer's relation Cp − Cv = R

For 1 mole ideal gas PV = RT. Heating by 1° at constant P: P(V+ΔV) = R(T+1) ⇒ PΔV = R. The extra heat Cp supplies goes into this expansion work, so:

γ is used to determine the atomicity of a gas.

Experimental heat of reaction — calorimetry
Heat given out = heat taken up: Q = (W + m)·s·(T2 − T1), W = water equivalent of the calorimeter. The bomb calorimeter (sealed steel bomb, sample + O₂ at ~20 atm in insulated water) runs at constant volume, so it measures ΔU; ΔH follows from ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT.

Kirchhoff's law (variation of ΔH with T)

Kirchhoff's equation(ΔH2 − ΔH1)/(T2 − T1) = ΔCp · (ΔU2 − ΔU1)/(T2 − T1) = ΔCv
⚠ TrapA bomb calorimeter gives the constant-volume heat (ΔU = qv), NOT ΔH. Convert with ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT before quoting an enthalpy of combustion.

Thermochemistry · Heat of Reaction & Types of Enthalpy

Thermochemical equations

  • A balanced equation that also states the heat change. Physical states (s, l, g, aq) and allotropic forms must be written.
  • Modern convention writes ΔH: ΔH negative = exothermic, positive = endothermic. Reversing the equation reverses the sign of ΔH.
  • Multiplying/dividing coefficients multiplies/divides ΔH: H₂+½O₂→H₂O(g), ΔH=−241.8 kJ; doubling ⇒ 2H₂+O₂→2H₂O(g), ΔH=−483.6 kJ.

Heat (enthalpy) of reaction

enthalpy of reactionΔH = ΣHP − ΣHR (heat evolved/absorbed when the equation's amounts react completely)

Factors affecting ΔH: (i) physical state (H₂+½O₂→H₂O(g) ΔH=−241.8; →H₂O(l) ΔH=−285.8 kJ, extra heat from condensation); (ii) allotropic form (Cdiamond+O₂ ΔH=−94.3 kcal vs Camorphous −97.6 kcal; heat of transition = 3.3 kcal); (iii) reaction in solution vs dry; (iv) temperature (Kirchhoff); (v) constant P vs V (ΔH = ΔU + ΔngRT).

Types of enthalpy

  • Enthalpy of formation ΔfH: heat change when 1 mole of a compound forms from its elements. For 2 mol product, divide by 2 (e.g. H₂+Cl₂→2HCl, ΔfH = −44/2 = −22 kcal per mol HCl).
  • Standard enthalpy of formation ΔfH°: elements in their standard states (25 °C, 1 atm) taken as zero. Positive ΔfH° = endothermic (less stable) compound; negative = exothermic (more stable).
  • Enthalpy of combustion ΔcH: heat evolved when 1 mole is completely oxidised (usually negative; a few, e.g. N₂+½O₂→N₂O, are endothermic).
reaction enthalpy from formation dataΔH° = ΣΔfH°(products) − ΣΔfH°(reactants) (elements' ΔfH° = 0)
Calorific value & standard ΔfH° values
Calorific value = heat per gram of fuel = |ΔcH|/molar mass. Methane: 890.3/16 = 55.6 kJ/g. Some ΔfH° (kJ mol⁻¹): H₂O(l) −285.83, CO₂(g) −393.51, NH₃(g) −46.11, HCl(g) −92.31, CH₄(g) −74.81, NaCl(s) −411.15, NO(g) +90.25, NO₂(g) +33.18.
⚠ TrapEnthalpy of formation is per mole of the compound. If the equation makes 2 mol (e.g. 2HCl), divide the reaction ΔH by 2 to get ΔfH.

Hess's Law · Born–Haber Cycle · Bond Enthalpy

Hess's law (constant heat summation, 1840)

  • If a reaction occurs in one step or several, the total enthalpy change is the same — it depends only on initial and final states.
  • Verified for C→CO₂: direct C+O₂→CO₂ ΔH = −393.5 kJ equals the two-step C+½O₂→CO (−110.5) then CO+½O₂→CO₂ (−283.0), sum −393.5 kJ.
  • Thermochemical equations can be added, subtracted or multiplied like algebraic equations.
Hess's lawΔHdirect = ΔH1 + ΔH2 + ΔH3 + … (for a cycle, ΔHsteps + ΔHreverse = 0)

Uses: enthalpies of formation of compounds that can't be made directly, of very slow reactions, of allotropic transitions, plus bond energies, resonance energy and lattice enthalpy.

Born–Haber cycle (lattice enthalpy)

For Na(s) + ½Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s), the formation enthalpy is broken into steps (sublimation S, dissociation D, ionisation IE, electron-gain ΔegH, and lattice enthalpy U₀):

Born–Haber for NaClΔfH° = S + ½D + IE + ΔegH + U₀ ⇒ U₀ = ΔfH° − S − ½D − IE − ΔegH
NaCl worked values
S = +108.5, D = +243.0 (½D = 121.5), IE = +495.8, ΔegH = −348.6, ΔfH° = −411.2 kJ mol⁻¹.
U₀ = (−411.2) − 108.5 − ½(243.0) − 495.8 − (−348.6) = −788.4 kJ mol⁻¹. Lattice enthalpy of NaCl = −788.4 kJ mol⁻¹. (Positive sign = energy absorbed; negative = released.)

Bond enthalpy

bond enthalpy methodΔH = Σ B.E.(bonds broken in reactants) − Σ B.E.(bonds formed in products)

Enthalpies of Solution, Neutralization & Phase Change · Bond Enthalpy

Enthalpy of solution

  • Heat change when 1 mole dissolves in excess solvent (further dilution gives no more heat). ΔHsolution = ΔHionisation + ΔHhydration.
  • ΔHi (separating ions) is +ve (endothermic); ΔHh (hydration) is −ve (exothermic). If ΔHi > |ΔHh| ⇒ endothermic (KNO₃, NH₄Cl cool the water); if ΔHi < |ΔHh| ⇒ exothermic (NaOH, H₂SO₄ warm it).

Enthalpy of neutralization

  • Heat released when 1 g-equivalent of acid is neutralised by 1 g-equivalent of base in dilute solution. It is really the heat of formation of water: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O.
  • For any strong acid + strong base it is constant: −13.7 kcal = −57.1 kJ.
  • Weak acid/base gives a smaller magnitude (part of the heat ionises the weak component); HF gives a larger value (~16.4 kcal) due to the heavily hydrated small F⁻ ion.
key enthalpy valuesΔHneut(strong–strong) = −57.1 kJ = −13.7 kcal · ΔHsolution = ΔHi + ΔHh

Enthalpies of phase change

TypeProcessSign
Vaporisationliquid → vapour at b.p. (H₂O ΔH=+40.6 kJ)+ (endo)
Sublimationsolid → vapour (I₂ ΔH=+62.4 kJ); ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap+ (endo)

Bond enthalpy / bond dissociation enthalpy

  • Energy to break 1 mole of a given bond in the gaseous state. Bond formation releases energy (−ve); bond dissociation absorbs energy (+ve) — equal magnitude, opposite sign (H–H = ±435.14 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • Polyatomic molecules: identical bonds may need different energies (O–H in water: 501.87 then 423.38 kJ) ⇒ use average bond energy = total dissociation energy ÷ number of bonds (O–H avg = (501.87+423.38)/2 = 462.3 kJ).
  • Higher bond dissociation energy ⇒ stronger bond. Resonance energy = (ΔfH)exp − (ΔfH)calc from bond data.
⚠ TrapHeat of reaction from bond data = Σ B.E.(reactants) − Σ B.E.(products) — reactants minus products (bonds broken minus bonds formed). Reversing this flips the sign.

L-10

Spontaneity · Entropy · Second Law of Thermodynamics

Limitations of the first law & spontaneity

  • The first law conserves energy but says nothing about direction or why natural processes are irreversible — the second law fills this gap.
  • Spontaneous process: has a natural tendency to occur, on its own (water flowing downhill, gas diffusing) or after initiation (burning coal). It proceeds until equilibrium. Non-spontaneous: needs continuous external energy (heat from cold→hot, electrolysis of water).

Two driving forces

  • Tendency to minimum energy: most spontaneous reactions are exothermic (ΔH −ve), but this alone fails — endothermic processes (ice melting ΔH=+6.0 kJ; CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂ ΔH=+178.3 kJ at high T) are also spontaneous, and reversible reactions and zero-ΔH reactions (esterification) occur.
  • Tendency to maximum randomness: nature moves from order to disorder (two inert gases mixing, sugar dissolving). Enthalpy alone cannot decide spontaneity — a second factor, entropy, is needed.

Entropy (S)

  • A state function measuring disorder/randomness; extensive. ΔS = Sfinal − Sinitial = Sproducts − Sreactants.
  • Order of entropy: gas > liquid > solid; raising T raises S. Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (or cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).
entropy — thermodynamic definitionΔS = qrev/T (heat absorbed ⇒ +ΔS; heat evolved ⇒ −ΔS)
entropy of an ideal gasT,V vary: ΔS = 2.303nCvlog(T₂/T₁) + 2.303nR log(V₂/V₁) · isothermal: ΔS = 2.303nR log(V₂/V₁) = 2.303nR log(P₁/P₂)
mixing of ideal gasesΔS = −2.303R[n₁ log x₁ + n₂ log x₂] (x = mole fraction; always +ve)

Second law — spontaneity in terms of entropy

second law criterionΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings · spontaneous: ΔStotal > 0 · equilibrium: ΔStotal = 0 · ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys/T
  • Reversible process: ΔStotal = q/T − q/T = 0 (ΔSsys = −ΔSsurr). Irreversible: heat q flows from T₁→T₂ (T₁>T₂), ΔSprocess = q(T₁−T₂)/(T₁T₂) > 0.
  • Statements of the second law — Clausius: heat cannot flow from cold to hot without external work. Kelvin–Planck: no engine can convert heat wholly into work; complete heat→work conversion is impossible without leaving an effect elsewhere.
phase-transition entropyΔSfus = ΔHfus/Tm · ΔSvap = ΔHvap/Tb · ΔSsub = ΔHsub/T (ice: 6000/273 = 21.98; water→steam: 40626/373 = 108.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
★ Key ideaNeither minimum energy (ΔH) nor maximum randomness (ΔS) alone decides spontaneity. The combined criterion is ΔStotal > 0 — equivalently ΔG < 0 (next lecture).

Gibbs Free Energy & Spontaneity

  • Gibbs free energy G = H − TS — the portion of energy available to do useful work. Extensive state function.
  • Since H = U + PV, G = U + PV − TS. At constant T, P: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS (Gibbs–Helmholtz equation).
  • Because ΔGsystem = −TΔStotal and spontaneous ⇒ ΔStotal > 0, a spontaneous process has ΔG < 0. The decrease in G equals the maximum non-expansion work obtainable.

Coupled reactions
A non-spontaneous reaction (ΔG > 0) can be driven by coupling it to one with a large −ve ΔG. e.g. 2Fe₂O₃→4Fe+3O₂ (ΔG°=+1487 kJ) coupled with 6CO+3O₂→6CO₂ (ΔG°=−1543 kJ) gives 2Fe₂O₃+6CO→4Fe+6CO₂, ΔG°=−52.2 kJ (spontaneous).
★ Temperature controlTemperature is a third lever: an exothermic reaction with unfavourable ΔS is favoured at low T; an endothermic reaction with favourable ΔS is favoured at high T. The crossover is at T = ΔH/ΔS (where ΔG = 0).

Standard Free Energy, ΔG° = −RT ln K & the Third Law

Standard free energy change ΔG°

  • ΔG° = free energy change at 298 K, 1 atm with all species in standard states. ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°.
  • From formation data: ΔG° = ΣΔfG°(products) − ΣΔfG°(reactants). The ΔfG° of an element in its standard state = 0.
standard free energyΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = ΣΔfG°(products) − ΣΔfG°(reactants)

ΔG, equilibrium & the equilibrium constant

  • As a reaction proceeds, G falls to a minimum where ΔG = 0 and Q = K — the equilibrium state (U-shaped G vs extent-of-reaction curve). Minimum near products ⇒ K ≫ 1; near reactants ⇒ K ≪ 1.
  • General relation: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q. At equilibrium ΔG = 0, Q = K ⇒ ΔG° = −RT ln K.
free energy & equilibrium constantΔG = ΔG° + 2.303RT log Q · ΔG° = −RT ln K = −2.303RT log K

Physical significance & electrical work

  • −ΔG = wnon-expansion = maximum useful (non-P–V) work obtainable. Derived from q = ΔH + wnon-exp and TΔS = qrev.
  • In an electrochemical cell: ΔG = −nFE (ΔG° = −nFE°), F = 96500 C, n = moles of electrons, E = cell emf.
free energy & cell emf−ΔG = wuseful · ΔG = −nFE · ΔG° = −nFE°

Third law of thermodynamics

  • (Nernst, Planck) The entropy of a perfectly crystalline solid is zero at absolute zero: limT→0 S = 0. It is impossible to reach absolute zero in a finite number of steps.
  • Enables absolute entropies: ST = ∫₀T Cp dT/T (area under a Cp/T vs T plot). ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants). A tephigraph (entropy vs T) shows S rising with T and jumping at each phase change (fusion, vaporisation).
★ Sign checkK > 1 ⇒ ΔG° < 0 (products favoured); K < 1 ⇒ ΔG° > 0 (reactants favoured); K = 1 ⇒ ΔG° = 0. Always cross-check the sign of ΔG° against whether K is bigger or smaller than 1.
