1Which interaction exists among polar molecules such as HCl?Dipole–dipoleA-DIPOLE🟡 SimilarbDipole–dipole
Concept A molecule like HCl is polar: chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so it pulls the shared electrons toward itself and the molecule carries a permanent separation of charge — a slightly negative Cl end (δ−) and a slightly positive H end (δ+). When many such permanent dipoles sit near one another, the positive end of one molecule is electrostatically attracted to the negative end of the next. That specific attraction between two permanent dipoles is called a dipole–dipole interaction.
Key relation permanent dipole + permanent dipole ⇒ dipole–dipole force, whose strength grows with the molecular dipole moment μ. Both partners already have fixed δ+/δ− ends, so no induction is needed — the alignment δ+···δ− is automatic. Contrast: ion–dipole needs a full ion; induced-dipole needs a non-polar partner to be polarized.
Solution
1. Classify HCl: unequal electronegativities (H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0) give an unsymmetrical charge distribution, so HCl is a permanent dipole.A polar bond in a molecule with no cancelling symmetry leaves a net dipole moment.
2. Place two HCl molecules together: the δ+ H of one lines up with the δ− Cl of the other.
3. This mutual attraction between two permanent dipoles is by definition a dipole–dipole interaction — option (b).
4. Eliminate the rest: ion–dipole needs a free ion (none here); London (induced-dipole) is the weak background force present in all molecules but is not the characteristic force of polar species; H-bonding needs H attached to F, N or O (Cl does not qualify strongly).
⚡ JEE Trick "Polar molecules interacting" ⇒ think dipole–dipole first. Quick tell: no ions in the list ⇒ rule out ion–dipole; H not on F/N/O ⇒ rule out true H-bonding. Trap: HCl does have weak London forces too, but the question asks the defining force of polar molecules, which is dipole–dipole.
Answer: (b) Dipole–dipole.
2Which of the following is not a van der Waals force?vdW classificationA-VDW🟡 SimilardIon–dipole
Concept van der Waals forces is the umbrella term for the weak attractions between neutral molecules. It covers exactly three members: dipole–dipole (Keesom), dipole–induced dipole (Debye), and induced dipole–induced dipole / London dispersion. What they share is that no full charge (ion) is involved — only partial charges on neutral species. An ion–dipole force involves a real, fully charged ion, so it falls outside the van der Waals family and is the odd one out.
Key relation van der Waals = {dipole–dipole, dipole–induced dipole, London dispersion}; all act between neutral molecules. A van der Waals force never needs a complete charge — it lives on δ+/δ− partial charges. The presence of a full ionic charge disqualifies it.
Solution
1. List the accepted van der Waals members: dipole–dipole, dipole–induced dipole, London (dispersion) — all between neutral molecules.
2. Examine each option against "are both partners neutral?".
3. Ion–dipole pairs a full ion (e.g. Na⁺) with a polar molecule — one partner carries a complete charge, so it is not a van der Waals force.Ion–dipole is typically far stronger than any van der Waals force precisely because a full charge, not a partial one, is involved.
4. Therefore the exception is ion–dipole — option (d).

 dipole–induced dipole     δ± + induced (neutral)   yes
 London dispersion         transient (neutral)      yes
 ion–dipole                FULL ion + δ±             NO  <-- odd one out
⚡ JEE Trick van der Waals = the three neutral-only forces. The instant an option names an ion (ion–dipole, ion–induced dipole), it is the non-van-der-Waals answer. Trap: London forces feel "too weak to count," but they ARE van der Waals; it is the ion term that is excluded.
Answer: (d) Ion–dipole.
3Hydrogen-halide b.p. HF/HCl/HBr/HI — which statement explains the trend?H-bonding vs masswatch?v=18H9nHNEHDU🟢 ExactdAll four compounds have identical intermolecular forces. ⚠
Concept The boiling points of the hydrogen halides do NOT rise smoothly down the group: the measured order is HCl < HBr < HI < HF. Two different forces are competing. From HCl to HI the molecules get bigger and heavier, so London dispersion forces strengthen and b.p. climbs steadily. HF, though the lightest, tops the list because H on the tiny, highly electronegative F gives strong hydrogen bonds — a force the other three cannot form.
Key relation b.p. ∝ strength of intermolecular attraction: H-bond (HF only) ≫ dipole–dipole ≈ London (grows with molar mass). H-bonding needs H on F, N or O; Cl, Br, I are too large / insufficiently electronegative, so HCl–HI rely on ordinary van der Waals forces. Data: b.p. HF ≈ +19.5 °C, HCl ≈ −85 °C, HBr ≈ −67 °C, HI ≈ −35 °C.
Solution
1. Write the experimental order from the data: HCl (−85) < HBr (−67) < HI (−35) < HF (+19.5 °C).
2. Explain HCl → HBr → HI: molar mass 36.5 → 81 → 128 g/mol, so electron count and polarizability rise ⇒ London forces strengthen ⇒ b.p. rises with mass.Dipole moment actually falls HCl→HI, so dispersion, not polarity, controls this leg.
3. Explain HF: H–F satisfies the H-bond condition (H on F), so molecules link into H-bonded chains; breaking these needs far more energy ⇒ abnormally high b.p. despite the smallest mass.
4. Test the statements: "identical forces in all four" is false — HF alone hydrogen-bonds; "b.p. rises with mass throughout" fails for HF. Only HF anomalous by H-bonding, the rest rising with molar mass fits — option (b).

 HCl         36.5     dipole + London     -85
 HBr         81       London (larger)     -67
 HI         128       London (largest)    -35

⚡ JEE Trick Split any hydride series into TWO stories: first member (H-bonded spike) + the rest (London rise with mass). Trap: "same forces in all" and "mass decides everything" each explain only half the graph — the right option must cover both.

4HCl and HF both polar, but HF has much higher b.p. — why?Hydrogen bondingwatch?v=17o2rapHPLk🟢 ExactaHF molecules form strong hydrogen bonds
Concept HCl and HF are both polar diatomic molecules with dipole–dipole attractions, yet HF boils at +19.5 °C while HCl boils at −85 °C — a gap of over 100 °C that mere polarity cannot produce. The difference is a category of force, not a matter of degree: in HF, hydrogen is attached directly to fluorine — the smallest, most electronegative atom — so the H carries a large δ+ and grips a lone pair of F on a neighbouring molecule: a hydrogen bond (F–H···F), ~10× stronger than ordinary dipole–dipole contact. Chlorine is too big and insufficiently electronegative for HCl to match this.
Key relation H-bond condition: H bonded to F, N or O (small + highly electronegative + lone pair). Only these three atoms make the H sufficiently positive and offer a compact lone pair close enough for a strong directional bridge; Cl (EN 3.0, large radius) gives at best marginal H-bonding. Typical strengths: H-bond ≈ 10–40 kJ/mol vs dipole–dipole ≈ 2–8 kJ/mol.
Solution
1. Note both molecules are polar (ΔEN: H–F = 1.9, H–Cl = 0.9), so both have dipole–dipole and London forces — polarity alone cannot explain a 100 °C gap.
2. Apply the H-bond checklist to HF: H on F ✓, F tiny ✓, F has 3 lone pairs ✓ ⇒ liquid HF forms strong F–H···F hydrogen-bonded zig-zag chains.
3. Apply it to HCl: H on Cl ✗ (Cl too large, EN too low) ⇒ no effective hydrogen bonding; HCl sticks together only by ordinary van der Waals forces.
4. Boiling must rupture intermolecular links: breaking H-bond chains in HF needs far more thermal energy than separating weakly held HCl molecules ⇒ b.p.(HF) ≫ b.p.(HCl).
5. Hence the reason is that HF molecules form strong hydrogen bonds — option (a).
 property        HF               HCl
 polar?          yes (ΔEN 1.9)    yes (ΔEN 0.9)
 H on F/N/O?     YES ⇒ H-bonds    no ⇒ vdW only
 b.p.            +19.5 °C         -85 °C
 cause: F–H···F chains in HF      <-- answer (a)
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the trio F, N, O — H bonded to anything else (even Cl) does not count as classic H-bonding. Trap: "HCl is heavier, so more London force" is true but tiny compared with HF's H-bonds; never let a molar-mass argument override a hydrogen-bonding one when H sits on F/N/O.
Answer: (a) HF molecules form strong hydrogen bonds.
5H2O and NH3 both H-bond, yet H2O has higher b.p. — why?H-bond networkwatch?v=MmMZl6VZdF8🟢 ExactaEach H₂O molecule can form four hydrogen bonds (two donor + two acceptor) whereas NH₃ forms fewer
Concept Both H₂O (b.p. 100 °C) and NH₃ (b.p. −33 °C) satisfy the H-bond condition (H on O, H on N), so the question is not "who hydrogen-bonds" but "who hydrogen-bonds MORE per molecule". Counting matters: a hydrogen bond needs one donor (an X–H) and one acceptor (a lone pair). Water has 2 O–H bonds and 2 lone pairs — a perfectly balanced 2 + 2 — so every molecule can anchor four H-bonds into a 3-D network. Ammonia has 3 N–H donors but only 1 lone pair, so each NH₃ averages far fewer bonds.
Key relation H-bonds per molecule = matched pairs of (X–H donors, lone-pair acceptors): H₂O = 2+2 ⇒ 4; NH₃ = 3+1 ⇒ limited by 1 acceptor. In bulk liquid the scarce partner caps the count — NH₃'s single lone pair is the bottleneck, so on average only ~1–2 H-bonds form per NH₃. Also O (EN 3.5) > N (EN 3.0) makes each O–H···O bond individually stronger.
Solution
1. Confirm both liquids H-bond: H on O in water, H on N in ammonia ⇒ mere presence of H-bonding cannot separate them.
2. Count for H₂O: donors = 2 (two O–H), acceptors = 2 (two lone pairs) ⇒ up to 4 H-bonds per molecule, giving an extensive tetrahedral 3-D network.
3. Count for NH₃: donors = 3 (three N–H) but acceptors = 1 lone pair ⇒ the acceptor shortage limits each molecule to roughly one effective H-bond on average.Unmatched N–H hydrogens find no lone pair to grip.
4. Add bond quality: ΔEN(O–H) > ΔEN(N–H), so each water H-bond is also individually stronger than an ammonia one.
5. More bonds × stronger bonds ⇒ far more energy to free a water molecule ⇒ b.p. H₂O (100 °C) ≫ NH₃ (−33 °C) — option (a).
 molecule   X–H donors   lone pairs   H-bonds/molecule   b.p.
 H₂O            2            2           4  (2+2)        100 °C  <-- answer (a)
 NH₃            3            1          ~1  (capped)     -33 °C
⚡ JEE Trick When two H-bonding liquids are compared, count donor/acceptor pairs — the smaller of (X–H count, lone-pair count) caps the bonds; water's 2 + 2 balance is unbeatable. Trap: "N–H is weaker than O–H" is true but secondary — the count (4 vs fewer) is the flagged reason.
Answer: (a) Each H₂O molecule can form four hydrogen bonds (two donor + two acceptor) whereas NH₃ forms fewer.

1What are the types of intermolecular forces?Types of IMFA-IMF🟡 Similar—
Concept Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are the attractions that act between separate molecules (not the strong covalent bonds within a molecule). They are much weaker than chemical bonds but decide the physical properties — melting point, boiling point, viscosity, solubility, state at room temperature. The task is to name and classify the recognised types in order of increasing strength.
Key relation IMF = van der Waals forces (dipole–dipole, dipole–induced dipole, London dispersion) + ion–dipole/ion–induced dipole + hydrogen bonding. The three van der Waals members act between neutral molecules; ion–dipole needs a full ion; hydrogen bonding is a specially strong dipole–dipole case when H is bonded to F, N or O.
Solution
1. Separate IMFs from intramolecular bonds: IMFs act between molecules and are weak; covalent/ionic bonds act within and are strong.
2. List the van der Waals sub-types (neutral molecules only): (a) dipole–dipole — between two permanent dipoles; (b) dipole–induced dipole — a permanent dipole polarizes a non-polar molecule; (c) London dispersion (induced–induced) — transient dipoles in any molecule.
3. Add the ion-based attractions: ion–dipole and ion–induced dipole, which involve a full ionic charge and are stronger than van der Waals.
4. Add the special case: hydrogen bonding, the strongest IMF, when H is attached to F, N or O.
 type                       partners                strength (rough)
 London dispersion          any molecules            weakest
 dipole–induced dipole      polar + non-polar        weak
 dipole–dipole              two polar molecules      moderate
 hydrogen bonding           X–H···Y (X,Y = F,N,O)    strong
 ion–dipole                 ion + polar molecule     strongest (of these)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the family tree: van der Waals = the three neutral-molecule forces (London, dipole–induced, dipole–dipole); then hydrogen bond (special, strong) and ion–dipole (needs an ion). Trap: London dispersion is present in every substance, even noble gases — never say a molecule "has no intermolecular force."
Key point: intermolecular forces = van der Waals forces (London dispersion, dipole–induced dipole, dipole–dipole), ion–dipole / ion–induced dipole, and hydrogen bonding — all weak attractions between molecules that govern physical properties.
2Explain dipole–dipole interaction with a suitable exampleDipole–dipoleA-DIPOLE🟡 Similar—
Concept A dipole–dipole interaction is the electrostatic attraction between two molecules that each possess a permanent dipole moment — i.e. two polar molecules. Because each molecule has a fixed positive end (δ+) and negative end (δ−), neighbouring molecules orient so that opposite partial charges face each other, giving a net attractive force. It is one of the van der Waals forces and is responsible for the higher boiling points of polar substances compared with non-polar ones of similar mass.
Key relation strength ∝ μ² (and falls off with distance), where μ is the permanent dipole moment. A larger dipole moment means larger partial charges, hence stronger δ+···δ− attraction. Requires both partners to be permanent dipoles.
Solution
1. Take a polar example — HCl: Cl is more electronegative than H, so the molecule has δ− on Cl and δ+ on H (a permanent dipole).The unequal sharing of the bonding electrons creates fixed partial charges.
2. Bring two HCl molecules close: they orient head-to-tail so the δ+ H of one points at the δ− Cl of the other.
3. The opposite partial charges attract — this alignment attraction is the dipole–dipole interaction.
4. Consequence: polar HCl (M ≈ 36.5) boils higher than a non-polar molecule of similar mass because dipole–dipole adds to the weak London forces.
 molecule   polar?   dipole   example of dipole–dipole
 HCl        yes      δ+H–Clδ−  δ+H···Clδ−  (attraction)
 (repeat across many molecules → net cohesive force)
⚡ JEE Trick Dipole–dipole needs both molecules polar; strength scales as μ², so doubling the dipole moment quadruples the force. Trap: a molecule can have polar bonds yet be non-polar overall if the dipoles cancel by symmetry (e.g. CO₂) — such molecules show no dipole–dipole force.
Key point: dipole–dipole interaction = electrostatic attraction between the permanent dipoles of two polar molecules (e.g. HCl···HCl), a van der Waals force whose strength rises with dipole moment (∝ μ²).
3Explain ion–dipole interaction with a suitable exampleIon–dipoleA-IONDIP🟡 Similar—
Concept An ion–dipole interaction is the electrostatic attraction between a full ion (cation or anion) and a nearby polar molecule (permanent dipole). Because one partner carries a complete charge rather than a partial one, this force is markedly stronger than the van der Waals forces. It is the force that drives the dissolution of ionic salts in water — the classic and expected example.
Key relation strength ∝ (ionic charge × dipole moment) ÷ (distance)². A higher ionic charge or larger dipole moment, and a shorter separation, all increase the attraction — hence small, highly-charged ions are strongly hydrated. One partner MUST be a real ion; this is why it is not classed as a van der Waals force.
Solution
1. Take NaCl dissolving in water. Water is polar: O is δ−, the two H are δ+.
2. Around a Na⁺ cation, water molecules point their δ− oxygen ends inward — cation–dipole attraction.Opposite charges attract, so the negative end of the dipole faces the positive ion.
3. Around a Cl⁻ anion, water molecules point their δ+ hydrogen ends inward — anion–dipole attraction.
4. These ion–dipole attractions (hydration) release energy and pull the ions out of the crystal lattice, dissolving the salt.
 ion    water orientation      interaction
 Na⁺    O(δ−) faces the ion     cation–dipole  (attraction)
 Cl⁻    H(δ+) faces the ion     anion–dipole   (attraction)
 ⇒ salt dissolves (hydration of ions)
⚡ JEE Trick Ion–dipole = "ion + polar solvent" ⇒ think salt dissolving in water. It is stronger than all van der Waals forces because a full charge is involved, and it scales as charge/distance² — so small, highly-charged ions (Al³⁺, Mg²⁺) hydrate most strongly. Trap: it is NOT a van der Waals force (those need neutral molecules only).
Key point: ion–dipole interaction = attraction between a full ion and a polar molecule (e.g. Na⁺/Cl⁻ with water during dissolution); stronger than van der Waals forces and the basis of hydration.
4Explain dipole–induced dipole interactionDipole–induced dipoleA-INDDIP🟡 Similar—
Concept A dipole–induced dipole interaction (the Debye force) occurs between a permanent dipole (a polar molecule) and a non-polar molecule. The permanent dipole's electric field distorts the electron cloud of the non-polar molecule, inducing a temporary dipole in it; the original dipole and this newly induced dipole then attract. It explains, for example, how non-polar O₂ or a noble gas can dissolve slightly in polar water.
Key relation strength ∝ μ² × α, where μ = dipole moment of the polar molecule and α = polarizability of the non-polar molecule. A stronger permanent dipole distorts the neighbour more, and a more polarizable (larger, softer) electron cloud is distorted more easily — both raise the induced attraction.
Solution
1. Start with a permanent dipole, e.g. HCl (δ+H–Clδ−), and a non-polar partner, e.g. an Ar atom or O₂ molecule.
2. The dipole's field pushes/pulls the electrons of the non-polar molecule, shifting its electron cloud slightly — this induces a temporary dipole.A non-polar molecule has no fixed dipole, but its electrons can be displaced by an external field.
3. The permanent dipole and the freshly induced dipole are oriented to attract each other.
4. The force strengthens with the polar molecule's μ and with the non-polar molecule's polarizability α (bigger, softer atoms are more polarizable).
 partner 1 (permanent)   partner 2 (non-polar)   result
 HCl  δ+···δ−             Ar  (cloud distorted)    induced dipole in Ar
 ⇒ dipole–induced dipole attraction  (∝ μ²·α)
⚡ JEE Trick Pattern = "one polar + one non-polar" ⇒ dipole–induced dipole (Debye). Strength ∝ μ²·α, so it grows with the non-polar partner's size/polarizability. Trap: don't confuse it with London dispersion (induced–induced, needs no permanent dipole) or dipole–dipole (needs two permanent dipoles).
Key point: dipole–induced dipole interaction = a permanent dipole induces a temporary dipole in a nearby non-polar molecule and the two attract (e.g. HCl···Ar); strength ∝ μ²·α, one of the van der Waals forces.

2London dispersion forces are also known as …London forcesA-LONDON🟡 SimilarcInstantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces
Concept Even a perfectly non-polar molecule has electrons in constant motion. At any instant the electron cloud can be lop-sided, giving a fleeting instantaneous dipole. This momentary dipole then induces a matching dipole in a neighbour, and the two attract for that instant. Averaged over time these flickering attractions give a real net force — London dispersion — so the fullest name for it is the "instantaneous dipole–induced dipole" force.
Key relation London dispersion = instantaneous (temporary) dipole–induced dipole force, strength ∝ polarizability α (and ∝ 1/r⁶). Bigger, softer electron clouds fluctuate and distort more, so heavier atoms/molecules have stronger dispersion forces.
Solution
1. Recognise that all molecules — even non-polar ones — have moving electrons that create momentary uneven charge.
2. This instantaneous dipole polarizes a neighbour, inducing a dipole in it.The fleeting field of one molecule distorts the electron cloud of the next.
3. Instantaneous dipole + the induced dipole attract; summed over time this is the London/dispersion force.
4. So its alternative name is "instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces" — option (c). It is not "permanent dipole" (that is dipole–dipole) nor "ion–dipole" (needs an ion).
⚡ JEE Trick London = dispersion = instantaneous dipole–induced dipole = the ONLY force present in non-polar species (noble gases, I₂, CH₄). 3The strength of dipole–dipole interactions depends on …Dipole–dipoleA-DIPOLE🟡 SimilarbDipole moment
Concept A dipole–dipole force arises from the δ+ and δ− ends of two polar molecules attracting. The size of those partial charges — and therefore the strength of the attraction — is captured by a single molecular property, the dipole moment μ. The larger a molecule's dipole moment, the larger its partial charges, and the stronger the dipole–dipole interaction. So the correct dependence is on the dipole moment.
Key relation dipole–dipole strength ∝ μ² (and decreases with separation, ∝ 1/r³ to 1/r⁶ depending on whether dipoles are fixed or rotating). Force depends on the product of the two interacting charges; since both scale with μ, the force scales with μ².
Solution
1. Identify what sets the partial charges: the molecular dipole moment μ.
2. Bigger μ ⇒ bigger δ+/δ− ends ⇒ stronger head-to-tail attraction between neighbours.
3. Quantitatively the interaction energy scales as μ², so dipole moment is the controlling factor — option (b).Both partners contribute a factor of μ, so the product goes as μ².
4. Rule out distractors: molar mass governs London/dispersion, not dipole–dipole; the number of atoms alone does not fix polarity.
⚡ JEE Trick Dipole–dipole ⇒ think μ (and μ²). If one molecule's μ is double another's, its dipole–dipole force is 4× larger. Trap: mass/size affects dispersion forces; for the dipole–dipole part specifically, dipole moment is the answer.
Answer: (b) Dipole moment.
4Hydrogen bonding occurs when hydrogen is bonded to …H-bond condition (F,N,O)A-HBOND🟡 SimilarbF, N, O
Concept A hydrogen bond is a specially strong dipole–dipole attraction that only forms when a hydrogen atom is covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom — and in practice only fluorine, oxygen, or nitrogen qualify. Solution
1. State the requirement: H must be bonded to a small, strongly electronegative atom for the bond to be "hydrogen bonding."

3. Cl has similar electronegativity to N but is much larger, so HCl forms only weak, ordinary dipole–dipole forces, not true hydrogen bonds.
4. Hence hydrogen bonding requires H bonded to F, N, or O — option (b).

5Which molecule shows both hydrogen bonding and dipole–dipole interaction?H-bondingA-HBOND🟡 SimilarcH₂O
Concept The molecule must satisfy two things at once: it must be polar (to have dipole–dipole forces) and it must have H bonded to F, O or N (to hydrogen-bond).
