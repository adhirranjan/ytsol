1What does the area under a velocity-time graph represent? (a) Speed (b) Displacement (c) Acceleration (d) Distance-timeArea under v-t graphShow that area under v-t graph gives displacement – class 11 physics🟢 ExactbDisplacement
Concept On a velocity–time graph the vertical axis is v and the horizontal axis is t, so a thin vertical strip of width dt and height v has area v·dt — which is exactly the small displacement covered in that instant. Adding every strip (i.e. the total area under the curve) gives the net displacement. The classic trap is to swap this with the slope of the v–t graph, which is acceleration. Area ↔ displacement, slope ↔ acceleration — keep the two apart.
Formula s = ∫ v dt = area under the v–t graph. Dimensions settle it: (m s⁻¹)(s) = m, a length — so the area is a displacement, never a speed or an acceleration.
Solution
1. Take one strip of the graph, width Δt, at velocity v. Its area = v·Δt = distance advanced in that slice.This is just "distance = speed × time" applied to a single thin slice.
2. Sum every strip from t₁ to t₂: total area = Σ v·Δt = ∫v dt = displacement over that interval.
3. Screen the options by dimension: (a) Speed = area ÷ time, not area; (c) Acceleration = slope = rise ÷ run, not area; (d) "Distance–time" is not a physical quantity at all.
4. Only displacement carries the dimension velocity × time = area under the curve, so option (b) is correct.Where the graph dips below the t-axis the strip area is negative, so the area gives signed displacement; total path length needs the sum of |areas|.
 quantity        where it lives on a v-t graph
 acceleration    slope  (rise / run)
 displacement    area under the curve    <-- answer
 velocity        the y-value itself
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the pairing once and for all: on a v–t graph slope = acceleration, area = displacement. The seductive wrong answer is "distance" — the area is displacement (signed); it equals distance only when v never turns negative. Any patch below the axis subtracts.
Answer: (b) Displacement.
2Starting from rest, a body travels with acceleration α for some time, then deceleration β, finally coming to rest. Total time of journey = t. Find (i) maximum velocity (ii) net displacement (iii) average velocityTwo-phase accelerate/decelerate motionA car accelerates from rest at a constant rate alpha for some time, after which it decelerates at a constant rate beta to come to rest🟢 Exact(i) vmax = αβt/(α+β) · (ii) s = αβt²/2(α+β) · (iii) vavg = αβt/2(α+β)
Concept The motion is a triangle on the v–t graph: a straight rise from rest at slope α, then a straight fall at slope −β back to rest. The single peak is vmax. Two facts pin everything down — the two phases share the same vmax, and their durations add to t. The trap is trying to find t₁ and t₂ separately; you never need them individually, only their sum t.
Formula phase 1: vmax = α t₁; phase 2: vmax = β t₂; t₁ + t₂ = t; displacement = area of the triangle s = ½ · t · vmax. Rest → rest means the peak reached going up is the same peak lost coming down.
Solution
1. From the two slopes, t₁ = vmax/α and t₂ = vmax/β.
2. Add and set equal to t: vmax/α + vmax/β = t ⇒ vmax(α+β)/(αβ) = t.
3. (i) Solve: vmax = αβt/(α+β).Symmetric in α,β as it must be — swapping the two phases can't change the peak.
4. (ii) Net displacement = triangle area = ½ · base · height = ½ · t · vmax = ½ t · αβt/(α+β) = αβt²/[2(α+β)].
5. (iii) Average velocity = s/t = αβt²/[2(α+β)] ÷ t = αβt/[2(α+β)] — exactly vmax/2.A triangular v–t profile always averages to half its peak, so this is a built-in check.

 s    = abt^2 / [2(a+b)]          <-- (ii)

⚡ JEE Trick Don't chase t₁ and t₂ — the equal-peak condition vmax = αt₁ = βt₂ plus t₁+t₂ = t collapses straight to vmax = αβt/(α+β). The trap is writing s = ½αt₁² + ½βt₂² and grinding; the triangle area ½·t·vmax gives (ii) in one line. And vavg = vmax/2 is automatic for any rest-to-rest straight-line profile.
Answer: (i) vmax = αβt/(α+β); (ii) s = αβt²/[2(α+β)]; (iii) vavg = αβt/[2(α+β)].

Concept The v–t graph is a trapezium: a rising ramp (accelerate from rest), a flat top at the greatest speed V, and a falling ramp (retard to rest). Average speed = total distance ÷ total time = area of the trapezium ÷ its base. Because only the ratio of the three times is given, pick a convenient time unit; the answer is independent of the actual scale.
Formula vavg = total area ÷ total time; ramp area = ½·V·(ramp time); flat area = V·(flat time). Times in ratio 1:8:1 ⇒ take them as k, 8k, k so total = 10k; the k cancels at the end.
Solution
1. Let t₁:t₂:t₃ = 1:8:1 = k:8k:k, greatest speed V = 60 km h⁻¹.
2. Accelerating leg (rest → V): distance = ½·V·k = ½·60·k = 30k.
3. Constant leg: distance = V·8k = 60·8k = 480k.
4. Retarding leg (V → rest): distance = ½·V·k = 30k.Same shape as the accelerating triangle, so same area.
5. Total distance = 30k + 480k + 30k = 540k; total time = k + 8k + k = 10k.
6. vavg = 540k ÷ 10k = 54 km h⁻¹ — the k cancels, so the numeric ratio was all that mattered.

⚡ JEE Trick Never solve for the actual times — a ratio problem lets you set them to 1,8,1 and cancel the unit at the end. Shortcut: vavg/V = (½+8+½)/(1+8+1) = 9/10, so vavg = 0.9·60 = 54. The trap is averaging the leg-speeds (0, 60, 0) → 40, which ignores how long each leg lasts.

(a) Find maximum acceleration a_max and when it occurs (b) distance travelled between t=0.75 h and t=1.00 h by integrating v(t) (c) maximum jerk j(t)=da/dt and where junctions are excludedPiecewise v(t): max acceleration/jerk, integration#1 Kinematics: Introduction to Jerk | Rate of Change of Acceleration | AP Physics 1 · Calculus: Distance, Displacement, Piecewise Function🟡 Similar(a) 0.01852 m/s² (b) 34.77 km (c) 2.54 × 10⁻⁵ m/s³
Concept This is kinematics done with calculus instead of graph areas. Velocity is handed to us as an explicit piecewise formula v(t), so acceleration is its derivative a = dv/dt, jerk is the next derivative j = da/dt, and distance is its integral ∫v dt. Because v is glued together from four different formulas, every derivative must be taken piece by piece, and the junctions t = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 h must be examined separately: v is continuous there, but a jumps, so j does not exist at those instants. Units are the other trap — v is in km h⁻¹ with t in h, so raw derivatives come out in km h⁻² and must be converted to m s⁻².
Formula a(t) = dv/dt [km h⁻² → m s⁻²], j(t) = da/dt [km h⁻³ → m s⁻³], s = ∫ v dt [km, valid as distance only where v ≥ 0]. Unit bridges: 1 km h⁻² = 1000 m ÷ (3600 s)² = 7.716×10⁻⁵ m s⁻² and 1 km h⁻³ = 1000 m ÷ (3600 s)³ = 2.143×10⁻⁸ m s⁻³. Each "per hour" costs one factor of 3600 s; the km costs one factor of 1000 m.
Solution
1. First check the pieces actually join up (v must be continuous or the model is nonsense):t=0.5: 240(0.5) = 120 vs 120 + 30 sin 0 = 120 ✓. t=1.0: 120 + 30 sin(π) = 120 vs 120 − 60(0) = 120 ✓. t=1.5: 120 − 60(0.5) = 90 vs 90[1 − 0] = 90 ✓. t=2.0: 90[1 − (0.5²/0.5²)] = 90[1−1] = 0 ✓ — matches the printed note v(2) = 0.
2. (a) Differentiate each piece to get a(t) in km h⁻²:
  • 0 ≤ t ≤ 0.5: v = 240t ⇒ a = 240 km h⁻² (constant).
  • 0.5 < t ≤ 1.0: v = 120 + 30 sin[2π(t−0.5)] ⇒ a = 30·2π·cos[2π(t−0.5)] = 60π cos[2π(t−0.5)], so |a| ≤ 60π = 188.5 km h⁻².Chain rule: d/dt sin(2π(t−0.5)) = 2π cos(2π(t−0.5)).
  • 1.0 < t ≤ 1.5: v = 120 − 60(t−1) ⇒ a = −60 km h⁻² (constant).
  • 1.5 < t ≤ 2.0: v = 90[1 − (t−1.5)²/0.25] = 90 − 360(t−1.5)² ⇒ a = −720(t−1.5) km h⁻².0.5² = 0.25, and 90 ÷ 0.25 = 360; then d/dt of −360(t−1.5)² is −720(t−1.5).
3. Scan for the largest magnitude: the last piece grows linearly, peaking at the endpoint t = 2.0 h: |a| = 720 × (2.0 − 1.5) = 720 × 0.5 = 360 km h⁻². That beats 240 and 188.5.
4. Convert: a_max = 360 km h⁻² × 7.716×10⁻⁵ m s⁻² per km h⁻² = 0.0278 m s⁻², occurring at t = 2.0 h (the braking is hardest as the train stops).Long-hand: 360 × 1000 m ÷ 12 960 000 s² = 0.02778 m s⁻².
5. (b) Integrate piece 2 over 0.75 h → 1.00 h. Substitute u = t − 0.5, so u runs 0.25 → 0.50 and dt = du:
  • ∫₀.₂₅^₀.₅ 120 du = 120 × 0.25 = 30 km
  • ∫₀.₂₅^₀.₅ 30 sin(2πu) du = [−30 cos(2πu) ÷ 2π]₀.₂₅^₀.₅ = (−30/2π)[cos π − cos(π/2)] = (−30/2π)[−1 − 0] = 15/π = 4.77 km
  • Distance = 30 + 4.77 = 34.8 km.v = 120 + 30 sin(...) stays between 90 and 150 km h⁻¹ > 0 on this interval, so distance = displacement — no sign-splitting needed.
6. (c) Differentiate a(t) again, interior of each piece only:
 piece            a(t) [km/h²]        j = da/dt [km/h³]      max |j| on piece
 0 ≤ t ≤ 0.5      240                 0                      0
 0.5 < t ≤ 1.0    60π cos[2π(t-.5)]   -120π² sin[2π(t-.5)]   120π² = 1184   at t = 0.75
 1.0 < t ≤ 1.5    -60                 0                      0
 1.5 < t ≤ 2.0    -720(t-1.5)         -720                   720
7. Largest is |j| = 120π² = 1184 km h⁻³ at t = 0.75 h (where sin[2π(t−0.5)] = sin(π/2) = 1).
8. Convert: 1184 km h⁻³ × 2.143×10⁻⁸ m s⁻³ per km h⁻³ = 2.54×10⁻⁵ m s⁻³.
9. Why junctions are excluded: at t = 0.5 the acceleration jumps 240 → 60π (≈188.5); at t = 1.0 it jumps −60π (≈−188.5) → −60; at t = 1.5 it jumps −60 → 0. A jump in a means v is not twice differentiable there, so j = da/dt is undefined (an idealised infinite spike), not merely large.Physically a real train cannot change its acceleration discontinuously — the model is only piecewise-smooth.
⚡ JEE Trick For piecewise v(t): never differentiate across a junction — differentiate inside each piece, then compare interior extrema against the piece endpoints. Here the winner (360 km h⁻²) sits at an endpoint, which a "set da/dt = 0" search would miss entirely. Second trap: derivatives inherit the given units — v in km h⁻¹ with t in h forces a in km h⁻², so convert only at the very end (÷3600 once per "per hour", ×1000 for km). Sanity check: 0.0278 m s⁻² is a gentle train deceleration; if you got tens of m s⁻², you skipped the 3600².
Answer: (a) a_max ≈ 0.0278 m s⁻² (360 km h⁻²) at t = 2.0 h; (b) 34.8 km (= 30 + 15/π km); (c) |j|_max ≈ 2.54×10⁻⁵ m s⁻³ (120π² ≈ 1184 km h⁻³) at t = 0.75 h, junctions t = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 h excluded because a is discontinuous there.

Concept A velocity–time graph turns a three-stage journey into three simple areas. The key fact is that the area between the v–t curve and the time axis equals the displacement, because v = ds/dt means s = ∫v dt, and an integral is an area. Since the car never reverses (v ≥ 0 throughout), displacement and distance are equal here. Drawing the graph converts the whole problem into geometry: a triangle (speeding up), a rectangle (cruising), and another triangle (stopping) — no equations of motion needed.
Formula s = area under v–t graph, with v in m s⁻¹ and t in s so the area comes out in m. Triangle: s = ½ × base(t, s) × height(v, m s⁻¹). Rectangle: s = v × t. Unit conversion: 1 km h⁻¹ = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 5/18 m s⁻¹. Mixing km h⁻¹ with seconds is the single biggest source of wrong answers — convert first, always.
Solution
1. Convert the peak speed to SI: v = 72 km h⁻¹ × (5/18) = 20 m s⁻¹.72 × 5 = 360, and 360 ÷ 18 = 20. Now every quantity is in m and s, so the area will be in metres.
2. Lay out the three phases on the time axis (they run back to back):Phase 1: 0 → 20 s, v climbs 0 → 20 m s⁻¹. Phase 2: 20 → 45 s, v held at 20 m s⁻¹ for 25 s. Phase 3: 45 → 55 s, v falls 20 → 0 m s⁻¹ in 10 s. Total journey = 55 s.

1What does a straight line on a distance-time graph indicate? (a) Object at rest (b) Uniform speed (c) Acceleration (d) Variable speedDistance-time graph interpretationDistance vs Time Graph (What the slope says about speed)🟡 SimilarbObject moving with uniform speed
Concept On a distance–time (s–t) graph the slope is the speed, because speed = distance ÷ time = rise ÷ run. So the shape of the s–t curve tells you how the speed behaves: a straight line has one fixed slope everywhere, which means the speed never changes — uniform speed. A curve has a slope that changes from point to point, which means the speed is changing, i.e. the body is accelerating. Note this is distance vs time, not velocity vs time, so a straight line here means constant speed, not constant acceleration.
Formula speed = slope of s–t graph = Δs/Δt, with Δs in m and Δt in s, giving m s⁻¹. For a straight line the ratio Δs/Δt is the same no matter which two points you pick — that constancy is exactly the definition of uniform speed.
Solution
1. Read the printed figure: it is a straight line through the origin passing through the marked points (1 s, 5 m), (2 s, 10 m), (3 s, 15 m) and (4 s, 20 m).
2. Compute the slope between the first pair of points:Δs = 10 m − 5 m = 5 m; Δt = 2 s − 1 s = 1 s; speed = 5 m ÷ 1 s = 5 m s⁻¹.
3. Compute it again over a different, wider pair to test constancy:Δs = 20 m − 5 m = 15 m; Δt = 4 s − 1 s = 3 s; speed = 15 m ÷ 3 s = 5 m s⁻¹ — identical.
4. The slope is 5 m s⁻¹ everywhere ⇒ the speed never changes ⇒ the object moves with uniform speed, which is option (b).
  t (s)   s (m)   Δs (m)   Δt (s)   slope = Δs/Δt
   1       5       -        -        -
   2      10       5        1        5 m/s
   3      15       5        1        5 m/s
   4      20       5        1        5 m/s   <-- constant => uniform speed
5. Eliminate the distractors by the same slope rule:(a) "at rest" would need a horizontal line (slope = 0), but this line rises. (c) "acceleration" would need a curve (changing slope), but this is straight. (d) "variable speed" would likewise need a changing slope. Only (b) survives.
