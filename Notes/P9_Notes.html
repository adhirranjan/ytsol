L-1

Impulse, Impulsive Force & Conservation of Momentum

  • Impulse is the change in momentum produced when a force acts for a time interval — the product of force and the time it acts.
  • An impulsive force acts for a very short time but produces a large change in momentum (hammer on a nail, a kicked football, a car hitting a wall).
impulse (constant force)J⃗ = F⃗t  ·  Ft = mv − mu  ⇒  J = Δp⃗ = p⃗f − p⃗i

Instantaneous impulse

  • When a force acts for so short a time that the effect is instantaneous (a bat striking a ball), F and t individually are unknown but their product is found from the momentum change.
general impulseJ⃗ = ∫F⃗ dt = Δp⃗ = p⃗f − p⃗i  ·  (mass constant) pf − pi = m(vf − vi)
  • Case 1 — constant force: impulse = force × time interval.
  • Case 2 — force a function of time: impulse = ∫F dt over the interval.
  • Case 3 — F–t graph given: impulse = area under the F–t curve = change in momentum.
💡 Force-time curveThe area under an F–t graph is the impulse, i.e. the change in linear momentum pf − pi. Instantaneously, F⃗ = dp⃗/dt.

Conservation of linear momentum (recoil, explosion)

from Newton's 2nd lawF⃗ = dp⃗/dt  ·  if Fext = 0 ⇒ dp⃗/dt = 0 ⇒ p⃗ = constant
  • With no external force the total momentum of a system stays constant. For a body at rest that explodes into fragments, 0 = m₁v⃗₁ + m₂v⃗₂ ⇒ the fragments carry equal and opposite momenta.
  • Useful spin-off: p = √(2mK) (K = kinetic energy), so for equal-momentum fragments the lighter one carries more KE.
Why gun–bullet / explosion problems work
The forces driving an explosion (or the gases pushing a bullet) are internal to the system, so they cannot change its total momentum. Starting from rest, the total stays zero: the gun recoils with exactly the momentum the bullet carries forward, mgunvgun = mbulletvbullet. The lighter body always ends up faster.
⚠ TrapImpulse and momentum are vectors. For a ball that bounces straight back, Δp = m(vafter − vbefore) uses opposite signs — the change is m(v + u), not m(v − u).

A block of mass m and a pan of equal mass are connected by a string over a smooth light pulley. A particle of mass m falls on the pan with speed v and sticks. Find the speed of the system just after the collision.
On the particle: ∫N dt = mv − mV. On the pan: ∫(N − T)dt = mV. On the block: ∫T dt = mV.
Adding the last two: ∫N dt = 2mV. Equating with the first: mv − mV = 2mV ⇒ v = 3V.

Two billiard balls each of mass 0.05 kg moving in opposite directions with speed 6 m/s collide and rebound with the same speed. What is the impulse imparted to each ball by the other?

L-2

Collisions: Elastic & Inelastic · One-Dimensional Elastic Collision

  • A collision is any strong, short interaction between bodies — they need not physically touch. During it each body feels an impulse, but the total momentum of the system is always conserved.
  • Elastic collision: the bodies regain their original shape and size; both kinetic energy and momentum are conserved.
  • Inelastic collision: the bodies do not fully regain their shape; part of the mechanical energy becomes deformation/heat energy. Momentum is still conserved, kinetic energy is not.
The spring picture of a collision
Imagine a spring on the rear block m₂ with the faster block m₁ (v₁ > v₂) catching up. The spring compresses, slowing m₁ and speeding up m₂, until at maximum compression both move with the same velocity. The spring then relaxes, pushing them apart, and finally the front block leaves faster (v₂' > v₁'). If all the stored energy is returned the collision is elastic.

One-dimensional elastic collision

conservation lawsm₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'  ·  ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² = ½m₁v₁'² + ½m₂v₂'²
solving the two togetherv₁' = ((m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂))v₁ + (2m₂/(m₁+m₂))v₂
v₂' = ((m₂−m₁)/(m₁+m₂))v₂ + (2m₁/(m₁+m₂))v₁

Special cases

  • m₁ = m₂: the velocities are simply exchanged — v₁' = v₂, v₂' = v₁.
  • m₁ ≫ m₂, v₁ = 0: v₁' = 0, v₂' = −v₂ (light body bounces straight back off the heavy one).
  • m₂ ≫ m₁, v₁ = 0: v₁' = 2v₂, v₂' = v₂ (heavy body ploughs on; light body is knocked ahead at twice the heavy body's speed).
★ RememberEqual-mass elastic collision on a line = a clean swap of velocities. This is why a moving billiard ball stops dead and the struck ball goes off with the incoming speed.

Book Example · L-2
Two balls of masses m and 2m moving in opposite directions collide head-on elastically with velocities v and 2v. Find their velocities after collision.
Take the m-ball as body 1 moving in −x: v₁ = −v, m₁ = m; the 2m-ball: v₂ = +2v, m₂ = 2m.
v₁' = ((m−2m)/(3m))(−v) + ((2·2m)/(3m))(2v) = (−1/3)(−v) + (4/3)(2v) = v/3 + 8v/3 = 3v.
v₂' = ((2m−m)/(3m))(2v) + ((2m)/(3m))(−v) = (1/3)(2v) − (2/3)(v) = 2v/3 − 2v/3 = 0.
v₁' = 3v,   v₂' = 0
Book Example · L-2
Two pendulum bobs of masses m and 2m collide head-on elastically at the lowest point of their motion. Both are released from a height H above the lowest point. To what heights do they rise for the first time after the collision?
Speeds at the bottom: each √(2gH). Body 1 (m): v₁ = −√(2gH); body 2 (2m): v₂ = +√(2gH).
v₁' = ((m−2m)/3m)(−√(2gH)) + (4m/3m)(√(2gH)) = (1/3 + 4/3)√(2gH) = (5/3)√(2gH).
v₂' = ((2m−m)/3m)(√(2gH)) + (2m/3m)(−√(2gH)) = (1/3 − 2/3)√(2gH) = −(1/3)√(2gH).
h₁ = v₁'²/2g = (25/9 · 2gH)/2g = 25H/9;   h₂ = v₂'²/2g = (1/9 · 2gH)/2g = H/9.
h₁ = 25H/9 (mass m),   h₂ = H/9 (mass 2m)
L-3

Inelastic Collision · Coefficient of Restitution · 1-D Inelastic Collision

  • In an inelastic collision the bodies do not regain their shape and some kinetic energy is lost; momentum is still conserved.
  • Partially inelastic: the bodies stick briefly then separate — some KE lost, not all.
  • Perfectly inelastic: the bodies stick together and move as one — the maximum KE (consistent with momentum) is lost.

Coefficient of restitution e

law of restitutione = (separation speed)/(approach speed) = (v₂' − v₁')/(v₁ − v₂)
limits0 ≤ e ≤ 1  ·  e = 0 (perfectly inelastic, stick)  ·  e = 1 (elastic, separation speed = approach speed)
  • For a given pair of bodies e is a constant that measures how "bouncy" the collision is.

General 1-D collision (momentum + restitution)

the two working equationsm₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'  ·  v₁' − v₂' = e(v₁ − v₂)
solved for the final velocitiesv₁' = ((m₁−em₂)/(m₁+m₂))v₁ + ((m₂+em₂)/(m₁+m₂))v₂
v₂' = ((m₂−em₁)/(m₁+m₂))v₂ + ((m₁+em₁)/(m₁+m₂))v₁
  • e = 1 reproduces the elastic formulas of L-2.
  • e = 0 gives the common velocity v₁' = v₂' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂).
  • m₁ = m₂, v₁ = 0 (struck ball at rest): v₁' = ((1+e)/2)v₂,   v₂' = ((1−e)/2)v₂.
⚠ TrapThe restitution equation carries a sign convention: separation speed and approach speed are magnitudes along the line of impact. Set up v₁ − v₂ (approach) and v₂' − v₁' (separation) consistently, or you will get the wrong sign for e.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
A ball of mass m moving at speed v makes a head-on inelastic collision with an identical ball at rest. The kinetic energy after collision is ¾ of the original. Find the coefficient of restitution.
Equal masses, target at rest: v₁' = ((1+e)/2)v, v₂' = ((1−e)/2)v.
Kf = ¾Ki: ((1+e)/2)² + ((1−e)/2)² = ¾ ⇒ (1+e)² + (1−e)² = 3 ⇒ 2 + 2e² = 3.
e² = ½.
e = 1/√2 ≈ 0.71
Book Example · L-3
A ball is moving with velocity 2 m/s towards a heavy wall moving towards the ball with speed 1 m/s. Assuming the collision is elastic, find the velocity of the ball immediately after the collision.
Elastic ⇒ e = 1, so separation speed = approach speed. Approach speed = 2 + 1 = 3 m/s (they move toward each other).
The heavy wall keeps its speed (1 m/s, unchanged). If the ball rebounds at v, then v − 1 = 2 + 1.
v = 4 m/s (rebounds, opposite to its original direction)
Book Example · L-3
A cart A of mass 50 kg moving at 20 km/h hits a lighter cart B of mass 20 kg moving towards it at 10 km/h. The carts cling together. Find the speed of the combined mass after the collision.
Perfectly inelastic: v' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂) with v₂ = −10 km/h.
v' = (50·20 − 20·10)/70 = (1000 − 200)/70 = 800/70.
v' = 80/7 ≈ 11.4 km/h (in A's original direction)
Book Example · L-3
An object of mass 10 kg moving with speed 10 m/s hits another object of mass 5 kg moving with velocity v. They stick and the combined mass moves at 25/3 m/s. Find v.
v' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂): 25/3 = (10·10 + 5·v)/15 ⇒ 125 = 100 + 5v.
v = 5 m/s
Book Example · L-3
A block of mass m moving at velocity v collides head-on with another block of mass 2m at rest. If the coefficient of restitution is ½, find the velocities of the blocks after the collision.
Momentum: mv = mv₁' + 2mv₂' ⇒ v = v₁' + 2v₂'.
Restitution: (v₂' − v₁')/v = ½ ⇒ v₂' − v₁' = v/2.
Adding suitably: 3v₂' = 3v/2 ⇒ v₂' = v/2, then v₁' = 0.
v₁' = 0,   v₂' = v/2
L-4

Loss of Kinetic Energy in Inelastic & Perfectly Inelastic Collisions

  • When a collision is inelastic, some kinetic energy is converted into heat, sound or deformation energy, so Kf < Ki. Momentum is still conserved.
general loss (using e-form velocities)Ki − Kf = ½m₂(v₂' − v₂)(v₁ + v₁' − v₂ − v₂')

Perfectly inelastic collision (compact result)

common velocity & energy lostv' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂)  ⇒  Ki − Kf = m₁m₂(v₁ − v₂)² / [2(m₁+m₂)]
Where the compact formula comes from
Writing Ki = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² and Kf = ½(m₁+m₂)v'² with v' = (m₁v₁+m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂), the algebra collapses to ΔK = m₁m₂(v₁−v₂)²/[2(m₁+m₂)]. It depends only on the relative velocity (v₁ − v₂): if the two bodies approach with the same relative speed, the same energy is lost regardless of the frame.
★ Reduced mass shortcutΔK = ½·μ·(v₁ − v₂)² with the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). Maximum possible loss in a collision is exactly this — reached when the bodies stick (e = 0).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-4
A ball of mass m moving with speed 2v collides with a ball of mass 2m moving with speed v (same line). If the coefficient of restitution is ¼, find the loss in kinetic energy due to the collision.
Label as the module does: m₁ = 2m (at v), m₂ = m (at 2v), e = ¼.
v₁' = ((2m − m/4)/3m)v + ((m + m/4)/3m)(2v) = (7/12)v + (10/12)v = 17v/12.
v₂' = ((m − m/2)/3m)(2v) + ((2m + m/2)/3m)(v) = (1/6)(2v) + (5/6)(v) = 7v/6.
Direct check: Ki = ½(2m)v² + ½(m)(2v)² = mv² + 2mv² = 3mv².
Kf = ½(2m)(17v/12)² + ½(m)(7v/6)² = 289mv²/144 + 98mv²/144 = 387mv²/144.
ΔK = 3mv² − 387mv²/144 = (432 − 387)mv²/144 = 45mv²/144 = 5mv²/16.
Loss = 5mv²/16 ⚠ (see note)
⚠ Book misprintThe module prints the loss for this example as 5mv²/8. That drops the leading ½ in the loss formula. Both the direct Ki − Kf calculation above (3mv² − 387mv²/144) and ½m₂(v₂'−v₂)(v₁+v₁'−v₂−v₂') = ½m(−5v/6)(−3v/4) give 5mv²/16, not 5mv²/8. Use 5mv²/16.
Book Example · L-4
In the previous problem, if the coefficient of restitution is ¾, what will be the loss in kinetic energy?
m₁ = 2m (at v), m₂ = m (at 2v), e = ¾.
v₁' = ((2m − 3m/4)/3m)v + ((m + 3m/4)/3m)(2v) = (5/12)v + (14/12)v = 19v/12.
v₂' = ((m − 3m/2)/3m)(2v) + ((2m + 3m/2)/3m)(v) = (−1/6)(2v) + (7/6)(v) = 5v/6.
ΔK = ½m₂(v₂'−v₂)(v₁+v₁'−v₂−v₂') = ½m(5v/6 − 2v)(v + 19v/12 − 2v − 5v/6) = ½m(−7v/6)(−v/4).
Loss = 7mv²/48
Book Example · L-4
A 60 kg man skating at 10 m/s collides with a 40 kg skater at rest and they cling together. Find the loss of kinetic energy during the collision.
Perfectly inelastic: ΔK = m₁m₂(v₁−v₂)²/[2(m₁+m₂)].
= (60)(40)(10 − 0)²/[2(60 + 40)] = 240000/200.
Loss = 1200 J
Book Example · L-4
A block of mass 2.0 kg moving at 2.0 m/s collides head-on with another block of equal mass at rest. Find the maximum possible loss in kinetic energy due to the collision.
Maximum loss ⇒ perfectly inelastic: ΔK = m₁m₂(v₁−v₂)²/[2(m₁+m₂)].
= (2)(2)(2 − 0)²/[2(2 + 2)] = 16/8.

L-5 & L-6

Collisions in Two Dimensions (Oblique Collisions)

  • When bodies move off in different directions after impact (billiard balls, air molecules), the vector nature of momentum matters. Resolve everything into two perpendicular directions (x and y) and conserve momentum in each separately. 2-D collisions come in the same three flavours: elastic, partially elastic and perfectly inelastic.

Perfectly inelastic collision in 2-D

  • Two objects collide and stick, moving off as one body with common velocity v' at angle θ₂. Momentum is conserved in both x and y; kinetic energy is not.
m₁ along x, m₂ at angle θ₁; combined at θ₂x: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂cosθ₁ = (m₁+m₂)v'cosθ₂
y: m₂v₂sinθ₁ = (m₁+m₂)v'sinθ₂

Partially inelastic (non-sticking) 2-D collision

  • Both bodies scatter along different directions. Conserve momentum in x and y, and apply restitution along the line of impact (the line joining the centres), since the contact force acts along that line.
momentum (u = before, v = after)x: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂cosθ = m₁v₁cosθ₁ + m₂v₂cosθ₂
y: m₂u₂sinθ = m₁v₁sinθ₁ − m₂v₂sinθ₂
restitution along the line of contacte = [v₂cosα₂ − v₁cos(θ₁+α₁)] / [u₁cosα₁ − u₂cos(θ+θ₂+α₂)]
★ Equal-mass elastic cornerWhen one body strikes an equal-mass body at rest and the collision is elastic and oblique, the two fly off at 90° to each other — the classic billiards result.

Perfectly inelastic 2-D collision: m₁ = m along x with v₁ = v, m₂ = 2m with v₂ = 2v at θ₁ = 45°. Find the common speed v' and angle θ₂.
x: mv + (2m)(2v)cos45° = (m+2m)v'cosθ₂ ⇒ (1 + 2√2)v = 3v'cosθ₂.

Dividing: tanθ₂ = 2√2/(1 + 2√2) ⇒ θ₂ = tan⁻¹(2√2/(1+2√2)).
v' = (1/3)√((1+2√2)² + (2√2)²) v = (1/3)√(17 + 4√2) v.
v' = (√(17 + 4√2)/3) v,   θ₂ = tan⁻¹(2√2/(1+2√2))

Same as above but with θ₁ = 30°. Find v' and θ₂.

Book Example · L-5 & L-6
Partially inelastic 2-D collision: m₁ = 1 kg, m₂ = 2 kg, u₁ = 2 m/s, u₂ = 1 m/s, θ = 45°, θ₁ = 30°, θ₂ = 60°. Find v₁ and v₂.
x: 1·2 + 2·1·cos45° = 1·v₁cos30° + 2·v₂cos60° ⇒ (×2)   4 + 2√2 = √3 v₁ + 2v₂ … (i)
y: 2·1·sin45° = v₁sin30° − 2v₂sin60° ⇒ (×2)   2√2 = v₁ − 2√3 v₂ … (ii)
Multiply (ii) by √3: 2√6 = √3 v₁ − 6v₂ … (iii). Subtract (iii) from (i): 4 + 2√2 − 2√6 = 8v₂.
v₂ = (4 + 2√2 − 2√6)/8; back-substituting in (i): v₁ = (6 + 3√2 + √6)/(2√3).
v₂ = (4 + 2√2 − 2√6)/8 m/s,   v₁ = (6 + 3√2 + √6)/(2√3) m/s
Book Example · L-5 & L-6

★ STRONG BOX

Previous-Year Questions (fully worked)

  • Momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion — that single law solves most of these. Reach for restitution only when the collision is not perfectly elastic or inelastic.
  • For an exploding body at rest, fragments carry equal and opposite total momentum; combine perpendicular momenta by Pythagoras.
  • For a bullet-in-block that then rises, chain two ideas: momentum through the (inelastic) hit, then energy for the rise.
Worked examples from the module
Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Main 2024)
A simple pendulum of length 1 m has a wooden bob of mass 1 kg. It is struck by a bullet of mass 10⁻² kg moving at 2×10² m/s. The bullet embeds. Find the height to which the bob rises. (g = 10)
Inelastic hit: v = (0.01×200)/(1.01) = 2/1.01 ≈ 1.98 m/s.
Rise: h = v²/2g = (1.98)²/20 ≈ 3.92/20 ≈ 0.196 m.
h ≈ 0.20 m  (option b)
Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Main 2024)
A stationary particle breaks into two parts of masses mA, mB moving with velocities vA, vB. Find the ratio of their kinetic energies KB : KA.
Momentum: mAvA = mBvB = p. KE = p²/2m, so KB/KA = mA/mB.
Since mA/mB = vB/vA, the ratio equals vB : vA.
KB : KA = vB : vA  (option a)

A spherical body of mass 100 g is dropped from 10 m. After hitting the ground it rebounds to 5 m. Find the impulse imparted by the ground. (g = 9.8)
Down: v₁ = √(2·9.8·10) = 14 m/s. Up: v₂ = √(2·9.8·5) = √98 ≈ 9.9 m/s.
Impulse = m(v₂ + v₁) = 0.1(9.9 + 14) = 0.1 × 23.9.
≈ 2.39 kg·m/s  (option d)

An artillery piece of mass M₁ fires a shell of mass M₂ horizontally. Just after firing, find the ratio of KE of the artillery to that of the shell.
Equal and opposite momenta: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ = p. KE = p²/2M, so KEart/KEshell = M₂/M₁.

A ball of mass 200 g rests on a vertical post of height 20 m. A bullet of mass 10 g hits its centre horizontally; afterward both travel independently. The ball lands 30 m and the bullet 120 m from the foot. Find the bullet's initial velocity. (g = 10)
Fall time t = √(2·20/10) = 2 s. So vball = 30/2 = 15, vbullet,after = 120/2 = 60 m/s.
Momentum: 0.01u = 0.01·60 + 0.2·15 = 0.6 + 3 = 3.6 ⇒ u = 360.

Each ball: Δp = 2mv. Total over 100 balls = 200mv in time t.

Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Main 2022)
A body of mass M at rest explodes into three pieces in the mass ratio 1:1:2. The two smaller pieces fly off perpendicular to each other at 30 m/s and 40 m/s. Find the velocity of the third piece.
Smaller momenta: p₁ = 1·30 = 30, p₂ = 1·40 = 40, at 90° ⇒ resultant √(30²+40²) = 50.
Third piece (mass 2) carries 50 in the opposite direction: v = 50/2.
25 m/s  (option c)

A batsman hits back a ball of mass 0.4 kg straight towards the bowler without changing its speed of 15 m/s. Find the impulse imparted to the ball.
Direction reverses: Δp = m(v − (−v)) = 0.4 × (15 + 15) = 0.4 × 30.
Impulse = 12 N·s

Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Advanced 2010)
Three objects A, B, C of masses m, 2m, m lie in a line. A moves at 9 m/s and collides elastically with B; then B collides completely inelastically with C. Find the final speed of C.
A(m,9) elastic with B(2m,0): vB' = (2m/3m)·9 = 6 m/s.
B(2m,6) sticks to C(m,0): v = (2m·6)/(3m) = 4 m/s.
vC = 4 m/s  (option b)
Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Advanced 2023, multiple correct)
A frictionless slide (horizontal at its lower end) sits on a terrace of height 3h. A ball of mass m is released from height h above the terrace top, leaves the slide with u₀ horizontal, falls a distance d at angle θ, bounces with velocity v to height h₁; e = 1/√3. Which statements are correct?
Leaving the slide: u₀ = √(2gh) x̂ ⇒ (a) correct.
At the ground vx = √(2gh), vy = √(2g·3h) = √(6gh) ⇒ tanθ = √(6gh)/√(2gh) = √3 ⇒ θ = 60° ⇒ (c) correct.
After bounce vy' = e√(6gh) = √(2gh) (upward, +ẑ), vx unchanged = √(2gh); so v = √(2gh)(x̂ + ẑ) — option (b) writes (x̂ − ẑ), wrong sign ⇒ (b) incorrect.
h₁ = (vy')²/2g = h; d = u₀√(6h/g) = √(2gh)·√(6h/g) = 2√3 h ⇒ d/h₁ = 2√3 ⇒ (d) correct.
Correct: (a), (c), (d)
Strong Box · PYQ (JEE Advanced 2010, multiple correct)
A point mass of 1 kg collides elastically with a stationary 5 kg mass. After collision the 1 kg reverses and moves at 2 m/s. Which statements are correct?
Elastic: v₁' = ((1−5)/6)u = −2u/3 = −2 ⇒ u = 3 m/s; v₂' = (2·1/6)u = u/3 = 1 m/s.
(a) Total momentum = 1×3 = 3 kg·m/s ✓. (b) 5 kg momentum = 5×1 = 5, not 4 ✗.
(c) Vcm = 3/6 = 0.5 ⇒ KEcm = ½·6·0.5² = 0.75 J ✓. (d) Total KE = ½·1·3² = 4.5 J (elastic), not 4 J ✗.
Correct: (a) and (c)
