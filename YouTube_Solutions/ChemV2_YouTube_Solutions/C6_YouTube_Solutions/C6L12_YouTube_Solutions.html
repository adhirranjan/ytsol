1Match the reactions in Column-I with relations of Column-II:
(A) ΔSsystem > 0 (Isolated system)   (B) ΔG < 0   (C) ΔTotalS = 0   (D) (ΔG)T,P > 0
(p) Spontaneous   (q) Non-spontaneous photochemical reaction   (r) Equilibrium   (s) Non-spontaneous		Matching spontaneity criteriaEasiest Trick to identify Spontaneous & non spontaneous reaction NEET/JEE🟡 Similar(A)→(p), (B)→(p), (C)→(r), (D)→(q),(s)⚠
Concept Four different-looking conditions all describe the same three states of a reaction: running forward, sitting at equilibrium, or blocked. The isolated-system entropy test and the free-energy test are equivalent, so both point at "spontaneous"; the zero and positive cases give equilibrium and non-spontaneity.
Key relation ΔStotal > 0 ⇔ ΔG < 0 → spontaneous; ΔStotal = 0 ⇔ ΔG = 0 → equilibrium; ΔG > 0 → non-spontaneous The bridge ΔG = −TΔStotal makes the entropy and free-energy statements interchangeable.
Solution
1. (A) ΔSsystem > 0 in an isolated system. No heat crosses the boundary, so ΔSsurr = 0 and ΔStotal = ΔSsystem > 0. The second law makes this spontaneous → (p).
2. (B) ΔG < 0. This is the direct free-energy criterion for a change at constant T and P: the reaction releases free energy and runs on its own. Spontaneous → (p).
3. (C) ΔTotalS = 0. No entropy is produced, so the change is reversible and the system has no net drive in either direction — equilibrium → (r).
4. (D) (ΔG)T,P > 0. Work must be supplied from outside, so the forward change cannot occur unaided — non-spontaneous → (s).
5. That leaves (q), "non-spontaneous photochemical reaction". A photochemical process such as photosynthesis has ΔG > 0 and runs only because light supplies the free energy, so (q) is a special case of (D), not of (B).
6. Hence (A)→(p), (B)→(p), (C)→(r), (D)→(q),(s). The key's placement of (q) under (B) contradicts (B)'s own definition.

 (B) dG < 0                -> spontaneous     (p)
 (C) dS_total = 0          -> equilibrium     (r)
 (D) (dG)_T,P > 0          -> non-spont.  (q),(s)

 photochemical reaction: dG > 0, driven by light

1For isothermal expansion in case of an ideal gas:
(a) ΔG = ΔS   (b) ΔG = ΔH   (c) ΔG = −T·ΔS   (d) none of these		ΔG when ΔH = 0Numericals & More on Gibbs' Free Energy (Thermodynamics #14)🟡 Similarc
Concept For an ideal gas the enthalpy depends only on temperature, so an isothermal change has ΔH = 0. The Gibbs equation then loses its first term entirely and free energy is governed by the entropy term alone.
Key relation ΔH = 0 for an isothermal ideal gas ⇒ ΔG = −TΔS H = U + PV = U + nRT for an ideal gas, and both U and nRT are fixed once T is fixed.
Solution
1. For an ideal gas U depends only on T, and PV = nRT, so H = U + nRT also depends only on T.
2. In an isothermal expansion T does not change, therefore ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0.
3. Substitute into ΔG = ΔH − TΔS: the first term vanishes, leaving ΔG = −TΔS.
4. That is option (c). Option (a) ΔG = ΔS is dimensionally impossible — an energy cannot equal an entropy.
5. Option (b) ΔG = ΔH would require TΔS = 0, which is false here because the gas expands and ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) > 0.
6. Check the sign: expansion gives ΔS > 0, so ΔG = −TΔS is negative — correctly predicting that a gas expands spontaneously into a larger volume.
7. Worked value: one mole doubling its volume at 298 K has ΔS = 8.314 ln 2 = 5.76 J K−1, so ΔG = −298 × 5.76 = −1717 J mol−1.
8. Note that this is the same −TΔS that appears as wrev = −nRT ln(V2/V1): for an isothermal ideal gas the free-energy change and the reversible work coincide.

3For a reaction at temperature T, the standard Gibbs free energy change is related to equilibrium constant K by:
(a) ΔG = −RT ln Q   (b) ΔG° = −RT ln Q   (c) ΔG° = −RT ln K   (d) ΔG = −RT ln K		ΔG° = −RT ln KClass 11 chap 6 | Thermodynamics 13 | Standard Gibb's Free Energy and Equilibrium Constant JEE/NEET🟢 Exactc
Concept Two things must both be right: the quantity must be ΔG° (standard states) rather than ΔG, and the logarithm must be of K rather than of Q. Only one option gets both right, and the other three are the three ways of getting it wrong.
Key relation ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q; at equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K, so ΔG° = −RT ln K The degree symbol goes with K; the plain symbol goes with Q.
Solution
1. Start from the general isotherm ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, valid at any composition.
2. At equilibrium the reaction quotient equals the equilibrium constant, Q = K, and the free-energy drive vanishes, ΔG = 0.
3. Substituting gives 0 = ΔG° + RT ln K, so ΔG° = −RT ln K — option (c).
4. Option (a) ΔG = −RT ln Q drops the ΔG° term entirely and is simply false.
5. Option (b) ΔG° = −RT ln Q pairs the standard quantity with a non-equilibrium quotient; ΔG° is a fixed number for a given reaction and temperature, whereas Q changes as the reaction proceeds, so they cannot be equal in general.
6. Option (d) ΔG = −RT ln K would make ΔG a constant, contradicting the fact that ΔG falls to zero as equilibrium is approached.
7. The equivalent base-10 form used in most numericals is ΔG° = −2.303 RT log10 K, which at 298 K reads ΔG° = −5706 log K in J mol−1.

Answer: (c) ΔG° = −RT ln K
4A reaction occurs spontaneously if:
(a) TΔS = ΔH and both ΔH and ΔS are positive   (b) TΔS > ΔH and both ΔH and ΔS are positive   (c) TΔS < ΔH and both ΔH and ΔS are positive   (d) TΔS > ΔH and ΔH is positive and ΔS is negative		Inequality form of ΔG < 0Spontaneity in Thermochemistry | Class 11 Chemistry | Temperature, Entropy & Gibbs Free Energy |🟡 Similarb
Concept The condition ΔG < 0 can be rewritten as an inequality between the enthalpy term and the entropy term. The question then becomes a test of whether each option's inequality is consistent with the signs it also specifies.
Key relation ΔG = ΔH − TΔS < 0 ⇔ TΔS > ΔH Simply moving TΔS to the other side of the inequality.
Solution
1. Rearrange the spontaneity condition: ΔH − TΔS < 0 gives TΔS > ΔH. That inequality is the answer in one line.
2. Option (a) TΔS = ΔH gives ΔG = 0, which is equilibrium, not spontaneity.
3. Option (c) TΔS < ΔH gives ΔG > 0, the non-spontaneous case — the exact opposite.
4. Option (d) states TΔS > ΔH but also that ΔH is positive and ΔS is negative. With ΔS < 0 the product TΔS is negative, so it can never exceed a positive ΔH. The option is self-contradictory.
5. Option (b) TΔS > ΔH with both ΔH and ΔS positive is fully consistent: the entropy term is positive and large enough to beat the positive enthalpy term.
6. This is the "spontaneous only at high temperature" family, since raising T grows TΔS until the inequality is satisfied above T = ΔH/ΔS.

 (b) T dS > dH, both +ve        SPONTANEOUS
 (c) T dS < dH      -> dG > 0   non-spont.
 (d) dS < 0 so T dS < 0 ; cannot exceed +dH
⚡ JEE Trick Check options for internal contradiction before doing any thermodynamics. Option (d) dies purely on arithmetic: a negative number cannot be greater than a positive one.
Answer: (b) TΔS > ΔH and both ΔH and ΔS are positive
5Which of the following statements is correct according to Gibbs free energy theory?
(a) At equilibrium, ΔG° becomes zero   (b) Decrease in Gibbs free energy equals useful (non-expansion) work   (c) ΔG depends only on enthalpy change   (d) Entropy of all substances is zero at 298 K		Physical meaning of −ΔGRELATIONSHIP BETWEEN CHANGE IN GIBBS FREE ENERGY AND USEFUL WORK / THERMODYNAMICS🟢 Exactb
Concept The physical meaning of Gibbs free energy is that its decrease measures the useful, non-expansion work a system can deliver at constant T and P. Three of the four statements are standard misconceptions the chapter explicitly corrects.
Key relation −ΔG = wnon-expansion = wuseful Derived on p.278 from ΔH − TΔS = wnon-expansion.
Solution
1. Statement (a) "At equilibrium, ΔG° becomes zero" is false. At equilibrium ΔG = 0, while ΔG° = −RT ln K, which vanishes only in the special case K = 1.
2. Statement (c) "ΔG depends only on enthalpy change" is false: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS depends on entropy and temperature as well. If enthalpy alone decided matters, no endothermic reaction could be spontaneous.
3. Statement (d) "Entropy of all substances is zero at 298 K" is false. The third law puts S = 0 at 0 K for a perfect crystal, not at 298 K; at 298 K every substance has a positive absolute entropy.
4. Statement (b) is the derivation of p.278: combining q = ΔH + wnon-exp with qrev = TΔS gives ΔH − TΔS = wnon-exp, i.e. −ΔG = wuseful.
5. So a reaction with ΔG = −300 kJ can deliver up to 300 kJ of useful work, for example as electrical work in a cell where ΔG = −nFE.
6. Only statement (b) survives.
 (a) at eqm dG0 = 0        FALSE (dG = 0 instead)
 (b) -dG = w_useful        TRUE
 (c) dG depends only on dH FALSE (dS, T matter)
 (d) S = 0 at 298 K        FALSE (S = 0 at 0 K)

 dG = -300 kJ -> up to 300 kJ of useful work
⚡ JEE Trick The word "useful" or "non-expansion" is the tell for ΔG; plain "total work" belongs to Helmholtz energy A. Match the adjective to the letter and these statement questions resolve at a glance.
Answer: (b) Decrease in Gibbs free energy equals useful (non-expansion) work

1How does second law of thermodynamics explain the conditions of spontaneity?Second law as the spontaneity testGibb's Energy and Spontaneity - Thermodynamics (Part 28)🟡 Similar—
Concept The first law conserves energy but says nothing about direction. The second law supplies the missing arrow: it declares that the entropy of the universe increases in every spontaneous change, and when that statement is rewritten for the system alone it becomes the familiar free-energy criterion.
Key relation ΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0  ⇔  ΔG = −TΔStotal < 0 Two faces of the same law: one counts the universe, the other counts only the system.
Solution
1. Statement. The second law says the entropy of the universe increases in every spontaneous process and stays constant only in a reversible one. Symbolically ΔStotal > 0 for spontaneous change, = 0 at equilibrium, < 0 for an impossible change.
2. Why the first law is not enough. Heat flowing from a cold body to a hot one would conserve energy perfectly, yet never happens. The first law permits it; the second forbids it.
3. Isolated system. With q = 0 the surroundings gain nothing, so ΔSsurr = 0 and the criterion reduces to ΔSsystem > 0. Free expansion of a gas into a vacuum is the standard illustration.
4. Non-isolated system. At constant T and P the heat given out is −ΔH, so ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T. Then TΔStotal = TΔSsystem − ΔH = −ΔG.
5. Consequence. Because T is always positive, ΔStotal > 0 is exactly ΔG < 0. So the second law, applied at constant T and P, is the free-energy test, and a reaction may lower the system's own entropy provided the surroundings gain more.
6. Illustration. Water freezing at −10 °C has ΔSsystem < 0, yet the latent heat released warms the surroundings enough that ΔStotal > 0 — and freezing is indeed spontaneous below 0 °C.
 spontaneous   dS_total > 0   dG < 0
 equilibrium   dS_total = 0   dG = 0
 impossible    dS_total < 0   dG > 0

 isolated:     dS_surr = 0  -> dS_sys > 0
 T and P set:  dS_surr = -dH/T -> dG = -T dS_total
⚡ JEE Trick Write the bridge ΔG = −TΔStotal at the top of the answer. Every second-law statement in the syllabus can then be translated into a free-energy statement in one line.
Answer: the second law gives ΔStotal > 0 for spontaneity, which at constant T and P becomes ΔG < 0.
2(1) Obtain the relation between ΔG and ΔS. (2) Comment on spontaneity of the reaction.ΔG = −TΔStotalClass 11 Chemistry Chapter 6 | Gibbs Free Energy & Spontaneity - Thermodynamics |Class 11 CBSE/NCERT🟡 Similar—
Concept The relation between ΔG and ΔS is obtained by expressing the entropy change of the surroundings in terms of the system's enthalpy change. Once derived, the single equation ΔG = −TΔStotal settles every question about spontaneity.
Key relation ΔGsystem = −TΔStotal  and  ΔG = ΔH − TΔS The first form links system to universe; the second is the working equation.
Solution
1. Part 1, step one. For a system that can exchange heat with its surroundings, ΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings.
2. Step two. At constant temperature and pressure the heat received by the surroundings is (qP)surr = −ΔHsystem, so ΔSsurr = −ΔHsystem/T.
3. Step three. Substituting and multiplying through by T: TΔStotal = TΔSsystem − ΔHsystem, i.e. −TΔStotal = ΔHsystem − TΔSsystem.
4. Step four. The right-hand side is exactly the Gibbs–Helmholtz expression, so ΔGsystem = −TΔStotal.
5. Part 2, spontaneity. Since T is always positive, the sign of ΔG is the opposite of the sign of ΔStotal. A spontaneous process needs ΔStotal > 0 and therefore ΔG < 0.
6. Full verdict table. ΔG < 0 spontaneous; ΔG = 0 equilibrium, the process does not occur on balance; ΔG > 0 non-spontaneous forward, though the reverse direction is spontaneous. A non-spontaneous step can still be driven by coupling it to a strongly negative-ΔG reaction.

Concept Ice at −10 °C cannot melt directly, so the entropy change is computed along a three-step reversible path: warm the ice to 0 °C, melt it there, then warm the water to +10 °C. The question then asks how many of those steps use the third-law formula S = ∫CPdT/T rather than the latent-heat formula.
Key relation Heating steps: ΔS = CP ln(T2/T1)  |  Fusion step: ΔS = ΔHfus/Tfus The CPdT/T integral is the third-law expression derived on p.278; the latent-heat step uses a different formula.
Solution
1. Step 1, ice from 263 K to 273 K: ΔS1 = 9 ln(273/263) = 9 × 0.03733 = 0.336 cal deg−1 mol−1.
2. Step 2, fusion at 273 K: ΔS2 = 1440/273 = 5.275 cal deg−1 mol−1.
3. Step 3, water from 273 K to 283 K: ΔS3 = 18 ln(283/273) = 18 × 0.03598 = 0.648 cal deg−1 mol−1.
4. Total: 0.336 + 5.275 + 0.648 = 6.258 cal deg−1 mol−1, exactly the value quoted in the question, which confirms the path is right.
5. Now count. The third-law expression ST = ∫CPdT/T is used in steps 1 and 3, the two heating stages. Step 2 uses ΔHfus/T instead, which is a latent-heat relation, not the third-law integral.
6. So the answer is 2 steps.

Answer: 2 — the CPdT/T form is used in the two heating steps (total ΔS = 6.258 cal deg−1 mol−1)
