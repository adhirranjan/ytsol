Universal Law of Gravitation

Universal Law of Gravitation

  • Formulated by Sir Isaac Newton in 1687; it describes the gravitational interaction between any two objects.
  • Statement: every particle of matter in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.
  • G is the universal gravitational constant, G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²; it fixes the strength of the gravitational force between any two objects.
  • Definition of G: the gravitational force acting between two bodies each of unit mass separated by unit distance.
  • The force acts along the line joining the centres of the two objects and attracts them towards each other.
  • Gravity is a very weak force compared with the other fundamental forces such as electromagnetism, unless a very large mass is involved or the separation is extremely small.
  • Note: in calculations the mass of each body is assumed concentrated at its geometric centre — this simplification gives accurate results in most cases.
F ∝ m₁m₂/r² ⇒ F = G m₁m₂ / r²
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²
💡 NoteTwo uniform spheres of radius R just touching are 2R apart centre to centre, not surface to surface — that one substitution carries most of the sphere problems.

Properties of Gravitational Force (12 points)

  • 1. Attractive force: it only pulls masses together, never pushes; it keeps planets, moons and stars in their orbits.
  • 2. Weakest of all known forces: weaker than electromagnetic and the strong and weak nuclear forces; significant only for large masses or vast distances.
  • 3. Non-contact force: it acts without physical contact, across empty space.
  • 4. Centripetal force: for circular or elliptical motion the gravitational pull supplies the centripetal force, directed towards the centre of the orbit.
  • 5. No medium required: it propagates through vacuum.
  • 6. Central force: it acts along the line joining the centres of the two objects.
  • 7. Inverse square law: the magnitude falls as 1/r², so the force drops rapidly as the separation grows.
  • 8. Obeys Newton's third law: the forces on the two bodies are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
  • 9. Proportional to the product of the masses: larger masses ⇒ larger attraction.
  • 10. Principle of superposition: with several sources the total force is the vector sum of the individual forces.
  • 11. Acceleration due to gravity on Earth: the surface value is about 9.8 m/s², denoted g.
  • 12. Point masses and spherical bodies: the expression is derived for point masses, but for an external point of a spherical body the whole mass may be taken at its centre of mass.
★ RememberProperties 6 and 7 together (central + inverse square) are what make gravitational orbits closed ellipses and let Kepler's laws apply later in the chapter.

Vector Form of Newton's Law of Gravitation

  • With r̂ the unit vector pointing from the first object to the second and r̄ = r̄₂ − r̄₁, the force on m₁ due to m₂ is along −r̂, i.e. towards m₂ (attraction).
  • The minus sign is what encodes attraction: the force is opposite to the unit vector drawn from the body towards the source.
  • By Newton's third law F̄₁₂ = −F̄₂₁ — equal magnitudes, opposite directions, along the same line.
  • Since r̂₁₂ = −r̂₂₁, writing F̄₁₂ = −(G m₁m₂/r²) r̂₂₁ and F̄₂₁ = −(G m₁m₂/r²) r̂₁₂ keeps the signs consistent.
  • The law is stated for point masses; for a group of point masses the total force on one of them is the vector sum of the forces exerted by all the others.
  • For four masses: F̄₁ = (G m₂m₁/r₂₁²) r̂₂₁ + (G m₃m₁/r₃₁²) r̂₃₁ + (G m₄m₁/r₄₁²) r̂₄₁.
F̄ = G m₁m₂/r² (−r̂) = −G m₁m₂/r² r̂
F̄ = −G m₁m₂ r̄ / |r̄|³
r̄ = r̄₂ − r̄₁
F̄₁₂ = −F̄₂₁
⚠ TrapThe 1/|r|³ in the vector form is not a different law — the extra power of r is used up normalising r̄ into a unit vector.

Principle of Superposition

  • The total effect at a point due to several independent sources equals the vector sum of the effects of each source acting alone.
  • For gravity: the resultant force on one mass is the vector sum of the separate pulls of all the other masses, each computed from Newton's law with its own mass and its own distance.
  • Total force on body 1: F = F₁₂ + F₁₃ + F₁₄ + F₁₅ + ...
  • Each pair interaction is unaffected by the presence of the other bodies — gravity is not screened or altered by intervening masses.
  • Standard figure: three masses m at the vertices A, B, C of an equilateral triangle acting on a mass 2m at the centroid G, F̄_R = F̄_GA + F̄_GB + F̄_GC (which vanishes by symmetry when the three masses are equal).
  • The same principle governs electric fields, magnetic fields and wave superposition, letting complex systems be split into simple components.
F̄ = F̄₁ + F̄₂ + ... + F̄ₙ = Σ (i = 1 to n) F̄ᵢ
F̄_R = F̄_GA + F̄_GB + F̄_GC
Resultant of two forces: F_net = √(F₁² + F₂² + 2F₁F₂ cos θ)
💡 NoteUse symmetry first: resolve every force along the symmetry axis because the perpendicular components cancel in pairs. The vector sum then collapses into one scalar addition.

Weight, Gravitation and Gravity

  • Assume the Earth is a uniform non-rotating sphere of mass M and radius R.
  • The force on a particle of mass m at distance r from the Earth's centre (r outside the Earth) is F = GMm/r².
  • The intensity of the gravitational field is the gravitational force per unit mass: E_g = F/m = GM/r².
  • The same force gives every particle an acceleration towards the Earth's centre, the gravitational acceleration a_g = F/m = GM/r²; numerically it equals the field intensity.
  • At the surface (r = R) this is the acceleration due to gravity, g = GM/R² ≈ 9.8 m/s².
  • The weight of a body at the surface is the force of gravity on it, F = mg — weight is directly proportional to mass.
F = GMm/r²
E_g = F/m = GM/r²
a_g = F/m = GM/r²
g = GM/R² (r = R) ≈ 9.8 m/s²
W = mg
★ RememberField intensity E_g and acceleration due to gravity a_g are numerically the same quantity (N/kg = m/s²); only the point of view differs — source field versus test-mass acceleration.

Units and Dimensions of G, Radius of Earth

  • Rearranging Newton's law gives G = Fr²/(m₁m₂), from which the unit follows directly.
  • Unit of G = (newton)(metre²)/(kilogram)² = N·m²/kg².
  • Writing N = kg·m/s²: [G] = (kg·m/s²)(m²)/(kg)² = m³/(kg·s²).
  • Dimensional formula: [G] = [M⁻¹L³T⁻²].
  • Value: G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² in SI (MKS); in CGS the figure quoted in the drill set is 6.67 × 10⁻⁸ (dyne·cm²/g²).
  • Standard data used in this chapter: mass of Moon ≈ 7.4 × 10²² kg, Earth–Moon distance ≈ 3.78 × 10⁸ m, mass of Earth ≈ 5.98 × 10²⁴ kg, mass of Sun ≈ 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg, Earth–Sun distance ≈ 1.5 × 10¹¹ m.
G = F r² / (m₁m₂)
Unit of G = N·m²/kg² = m³/(kg·s²)
[G] = [M⁻¹L³T⁻²]
⚠ TrapThe book prints the unit of G as N·m²/kg in the Solution lines of Examples 1 and 2; the correct unit, derived in this very section, is N·m²/kg². The arithmetic of those examples is unaffected.

Opposite corner B is along the diagonal at distance √2 a: F_DB = Gm²/(√2 a)² = (1/2)(Gm²/a²) = F/2

Three particles A, B and C, each of mass m, are placed in a line with AB = BC = d. Find the gravitational force on a fourth particle P of the same mass, placed at a distance d from particle B on the perpendicular bisector of the line AC.

Acceleration Due to Gravity and its Variation with Height and Depth

Acceleration Due to Gravity at the Surface of Earth

  • Acceleration due to gravity (g) is the uniform acceleration experienced by a freely falling object under the influence of Earth's gravitational force.
  • It is denoted by 'g' and has the SAME value for all objects regardless of their mass, shape or size.
  • At the surface of the Earth g ≈ 9.8 m/s²: the speed of a freely falling body increases by 9.8 m/s every second, near the equator at sea level.
  • The general field of a planet at distance r from its centre is g = GM/r²; putting r = R gives the surface value.
  • The actual value of g varies with height, depth and latitude, because of the Earth's rotation, its oblate shape and its uneven density distribution.
g = GM/r² (at distance r from the centre, r ≥ R)
g = GM/R² (at the surface, r = R)
★ Rememberg is a property of the ATTRACTING body (M and R), never of the falling body — this is why mass cancels out.

Relation Between G and g

  • For a body of mass m near the Earth, Newton's law of gravitation gives the force F = GMm/R², where M is the Earth's mass and R its radius.
  • Newton's second law writes the same force as F = ma = mg.
  • Equating: mg = GMm/R², and m cancels from both sides, leaving g = GM/R².
  • This shows g is independent of the mass of the object and depends only on the mass and the radius of the Earth.
  • Consequence: drop a wooden ball and a solid iron ball of different masses from the same height together and both reach the ground at the same instant (ignoring air drag).
  • Standard data: mass of the earth M = 6×10²⁴ kg, radius of the earth R = 6.4×10³ km = 6.4×10⁶ m, which give g ≈ 9.8 m/s² near the equator at sea level.
F = GMm/R² and F = mg
mg = GMm/R² ⇒ g = GM/R²
M = gR²/G (rearranged — the standard way to 'weigh the Earth')
⚠ TrapG is a universal constant (6.67×10⁻¹¹ N-m²/kg², the same everywhere in the universe); g is a local field value that changes from place to place and planet to planet. Never interchange them.
💡 Noteg = GM/R² means g ∝ M/R². In any comparison question take the ratio g'/g = (M'/M)(R/R')² — G never needs to be substituted at all.

Mass and Density of Earth

  • The mass of the Earth is approximately 5.97×10²⁴ kg — the total amount of matter contained within the Earth.
  • The average density of the Earth is approximately 5.5 g/cm³ (= 5.5×10³ kg/m³); density is a measure of mass per unit volume.
  • Treating a planet as a uniform sphere, M = ρ × (4/3)πR³; substituting this into g = GM/R² eliminates M in favour of the mean density ρ.
  • Result: g = 4πGρR/3, where ρ is the average density of the planet, R is its radius, G is the gravitational constant and g is its surface gravity.
  • Reading the formula: at FIXED density g ∝ R — a bigger planet made of the same stuff has proportionally stronger surface gravity.
Density = Mass / Volume, ρ = M/[(4/3)πR³]
g = 4πGρR/3
ρ = 3g/(4πGR) (inverted, to get a planet's mean density from g and R)
★ RememberTwo master forms of g — g = GM/R² (use when MASS is given) and g = 4πGρR/3 (use when DENSITY is given). Picking the right one turns most of this chapter into one line.
💡 NoteEarth's mean density 5.5 g/cm³ is about twice that of surface rock (~2.7 g/cm³) — evidence of a dense iron core. A moon that comes out near 1.6 g/cm³ is mostly ice.

Variation of 'g' with Altitude (Height)

  • Consider a body of mass m at height h above the Earth's surface; its distance from the centre is (R + h).
  • The field there is g_h = GM/(R + h)², while at the surface g = GM/R².
  • Dividing the two: g_h/g = R²/(R + h)² = 1/(1 + h/R)², that is g_h = g(1 + h/R)⁻².
  • This exact result holds for ANY height, large or small.
  • For h ≪ R (close to the Earth's surface), expand (1 + h/R)⁻² by the binomial theorem and neglect the higher powers: (1 + h/R)⁻² ≈ 1 − 2h/R.
  • Hence the small-height working formula g_h = g(1 − 2h/R).
  • Thus acceleration due to gravity DECREASES with height, as the inverse square of the distance from the centre.
  • At the height of a geostationary satellite g_h = 0.225 m/s²; at a distance as far as the Moon from the Earth's surface g_h = 0.0027 m/s².
g_h = GM/(R + h)²
g_h = g₀(1 + h/R)⁻² (exact — use for any h)
g_h = g₀(1 − 2h/R) (approximate — only when h ≪ R)
⚠ TrapThe binomial form g₀(1 − 2h/R) is ONLY for h ≪ R. Apply it at h = R and it returns a negative g — an instant giveaway that the wrong formula was used.
💡 NoteMemorise the exact values at round heights: at h = R, g → g/4; at h = 2R, g → g/9; at h = 3R, g → g/16. The denominator is always (1 + h/R)².

Loss in Weight at Height (h ≪ R)

  • Weight at the surface of the earth is W = mg; at height h the field is g_h = g(1 − 2h/R) for h ≪ R.
  • So the weight of the body at height h is W_h = mg(1 − 2h/R).
  • Dividing by W gives the weight ratio W_h/W = 1 − 2h/R.
  • The fractional LOSS in weight is therefore 2h/R, and the actual loss is ΔW = 2mgh/R.
  • The mass m is unchanged — only the weight falls, because the field itself has weakened.
W_h = mg(1 − 2h/R)
W_h/W = 1 − 2h/R
Loss in weight ΔW = W − W_h = 2mgh/R (fractional loss = 2h/R)
💡 NoteA 1% loss of weight needs only h = R/200 ≈ 32 km. Every percentage question on loss of weight at height reduces to the single number 2h/R.
⚠ TrapMass never changes with height or depth; only weight does. A beam balance comparing masses would read the same, a spring balance would not.

Variation of 'g' with Depth

  • As we go deep into the earth the EFFECTIVE mass of the earth decreases: only the inner solid sphere of radius (R − d) pulls on the body, the outer shell contributes nothing.
  • Effective mass at depth d: M_d = (4/3)π(R − d)³ρ, where ρ is the average density of the earth.
  • Assume the earth is a homogeneous sphere. Field at depth d: g_d = GM'/(R − d)² = Gρ(4/3)π(R − d); at the surface g₀ = GM/R² = Gρ(4/3)πR.
  • Dividing the depth equation by the surface equation: g_d/g₀ = (R − d)/R, i.e. g_d = g₀(1 − d/R).
  • So g decreases LINEARLY with depth — inside a uniform sphere the field is proportional to the distance r from the centre.
  • Special case — at the centre of the earth d = R, therefore g_d = 0 (the body is pulled equally in all directions).
  • Special case — at the surface of the earth d = 0, therefore g_d = g₀ = g.
  • The book also writes the same formula with h in place of d: g_d = g(1 − h/R), where h is then the depth, not a height.
M_d = (4/3)π(R − d)³ ρ
g₀ = GM/R² = Gρ(4/3)πR ... (1)
g_d = GM'/(R − d)² = Gρ(4/3)π(R − d) ... (2)
(2) ÷ (1): g_d/g₀ = (R − d)/R ⇒ g_d = g₀(1 − d/R)
Inside a uniform sphere: g ∝ r (r measured from the centre)
★ RememberThe two laws are not symmetric. Going UP, g ∝ 1/(R + h)², falling as 2h/R to first order. Going DOWN, g falls strictly linearly as d/R — exactly HALF as fast for the same small distance.
⚠ TrapDepth carries no square. Writing g_d = g(1 − d/R)² is the single most common slip in this lecture.
⚠ TrapNotation clash on pages 174-175: the book uses 'h' for depth in g_d = g(1 − h/R) on the same spread where 'h' means height in g_h = g(1 − 2h/R). Read which quantity is meant before substituting; using the depth symbol in the height formula (or vice versa) loses the factor 2.
💡 Noteg is maximum at the SURFACE and zero both at the centre and at infinity. Sketch g versus r: a straight line rising to r = R, then a 1/r² decay — this graph is a standard MCQ.

Variation of 'g' with latitude: at the equator, at the poles

  • Model the earth as a perfect sphere of radius R and mass M spinning with angular velocity ω about the polar axis.
  • A particle on the surface at latitude λ does not go round a great circle — it rides a circle of radius r = R cos λ about the polar axis.
  • In the rotating frame it therefore feels two forces: true gravity mg towards the centre, and a centrifugal force F_c = mω²r = mω²R cos λ directed outward, perpendicular to the spin axis.
  • The angle between mg and the centrifugal force is (180° − λ), so the two must be combined with the parallelogram law, not simply subtracted.
  • Numerically Rω²/g = (6.38×10⁶/9.8)(2π/86400)² = 1/291, a small number, so its square can be dropped and the square root expanded binomially.
  • Result: the apparent gravity falls off as cos²λ — maximum at the poles, minimum at the equator.
  • Rotation changes g by at most 0.034 m/s², i.e. about 0.35%, so g at the equator ≈ 9.78 and at the poles ≈ 9.83 m/s².
r = R cos λ (radius of the circle traced by a particle at latitude λ)
F_c = mω²r = mω²R cos λ
g′ = F_net/m = √[(mg)² + (mω²R cos λ)² + 2(mg)(mω²R cos λ)cos(180° − λ)]/m
g′ = [g² + R²ω⁴cos²λ − 2gRω² cos²λ]^(1/2) = g[1 + (Rω²/g)² cos²λ − 2(Rω²/g)cos²λ]^(1/2)
Rω²/g = (6.38×10⁶/9.8)(2π/(24×60×60))² = 1/291 (small ⇒ drop its square)
g′ ≈ g[1 − 2(Rω²/g)cos²λ]^(1/2) ≈ g[1 − (Rω²/g)cos²λ] (binomial expansion)
g′ = g − ω²R cos²λ ← the working formula
Case I, poles (λ = 90°): cos λ = 0 ⇒ g′ = g (rotation has NO effect at the poles)
Case II, equator (λ = 0°): g′ = g − ω²R; g − g′ = ω²R = (2π/T)²R ≈ 0.034 m/s²
Case III, zero effective g at the equator: g − ω′²R = 0 ⇒ ω′ = √(g/R)
★ Rememberg is LEAST at the equator and GREATEST at the poles — the classic MCQ. The difference ω²R ≈ 0.034 m/s².
💡 NoteBecause g′ = g − ω²R cos²λ, the drop at latitude λ is cos²λ times the drop at the equator. E.g. at 45° the loss is exactly half of the equatorial loss, ω²R/2.
⚠ TrapNever write g′ = g − ω²R cos λ. The cosine is SQUARED: one factor from r = R cos λ and one from resolving the centrifugal force along the radius.
💡 NoteSetting g′ = 0 at the equator gives ω′ = √(g/R), i.e. T = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 84 min ≈ 1.4 h — the earth would have to spin about 17 times faster for bodies at the equator to float.

Gravitational field

  • A gravitational field is the space surrounding a mass (or a system of masses) in which any other test mass experiences a gravitational force; it is the way we describe gravity's influence over a region.
  • A field is characterised by two quantities: gravitational field strength Ē (a vector) and gravitational potential V (a scalar).
  • Field strength Ē is the force experienced by a UNIT mass placed at that point; units N/kg, which is dimensionally the same as m/s².
  • Gravitational potential V is the potential energy per unit mass at a point; units J/kg.
  • The two are linked: the field is the negative gradient of the potential — the field strength is the rate of change of potential with distance.
Ē = F̄/m (field strength)
V = U/m (potential, J/kg)
Ē = −dV/dr (field = negative gradient of potential)
💡 NoteE is a vector and adds by the parallelogram/component rule; V is a scalar and adds algebraically. That asymmetry is what makes most multi-mass problems quick when done through V and slow when done through E.

Gravitational field intensity (strength)

  • The field intensity at a point is the gravitational force per unit mass placed at that point.
  • It is a vector, always directed TOWARDS the mass producing it (gravity is only attractive) — i.e. towards the centre of the field.
  • SI unit: newton per kilogram (N/kg); equivalently m/s². The field strength at a point is numerically the acceleration a free particle would have there.
  • Field intensity tells us how strong gravity is at different points and is the basis for all superposition problems.
Ē = F̄/m
F̄ = mĒ (force recovered from the field)
|E| = √(Ex² + Ey² + Ez²)
★ RememberE = g: the 'acceleration due to gravity' at any point IS the gravitational field intensity there. Treat the two symbols as the same quantity.

Gravitational field intensity due to point masses

  • Start from the vector form of Newton's law of gravitation and divide by the test mass.
  • The minus sign and the unit vector r̂ together encode that the field points back towards the source mass.
  • For several point masses, compute each Ē separately and add as vectors (the principle of superposition).
  • Standard geometries: equilateral triangle (field on one vertex due to the other two), square (field on one vertex, and field at the centre).
F̄ = (−GMm/r²) r̂
Ē = F̄/m = (−GM/r²) r̂ where r̂ is the unit vector along r̄
Ē_net = Σ Ē_i (superposition, vector sum)
Equilateral triangle, side a, three equal masses m: force on one = √3 Gm²/a² towards the centroid
Square, side a, four equal masses m: force on one = (2√2 + 1)Gm²/(2a²) along the diagonal
Square, side a: distance from a corner to the centre is a/√2, so r² = a²/2
💡 NoteAt the CENTRE of a regular figure, equal masses at opposite corners cancel exactly — only the difference of each opposite pair survives. Use that before writing any unit vectors.
⚠ TrapFor touching spheres, the centre-to-centre separation is 2a (twice the radius), not a. Using a gives four times the correct force.

Field due to a uniform rod (on its perpendicular bisector)

  • Take an element of length dx at distance x from the centre O of a rod AB of mass M and length L; the point P lies at perpendicular distance d from O.
  • The element's mass is dm = (M/L)dx and its field at P has magnitude G dm/(d² + x²).
  • Components along the rod from symmetric elements cancel; only the components along PO survive, which is why the result carries a factor of 2 and an integral from 0 to L/2.
  • The factor d/√(d² + x²) is the cosine that projects dE onto PO.
dm = (M/L)dx
dE = G dm/(d² + x²)
E = ∫₀^(L/2) 2GMd dx/[L(d² + x²)^(3/2)]
E = 2GM/(d√(L² + 4d²)) ← field on the perpendicular bisector
Limit L ≪ d: E → 2GM/(d·2d) = GM/d² (point mass) ✓
Limit L ≫ d (infinite wire of linear density λ = M/L): E → 2Gλ/d
⚠ TrapLabelling slip in the book's figure: the surviving component is written '2 dE sinθ' but the substituted factor is d/√(d²+x²), which is the COSINE of the angle measured from the perpendicular PO. The algebra and the final formula are correct; only the name of the angle is loose.
💡 NoteCheck any field formula with a limit: push the body far away and it must reduce to GM/r².

Field due to a uniform ring on its axis

  • Ring of mass M and radius a; the point P is on the axis at distance r from the centre O, so every element of the ring is the same distance z = √(a² + r²) from P.
  • Each element contributes dE = G dm/z² along the line towards it; by symmetry only the axial components survive.
  • The cosine of the axial angle is cos α = r/z, the same for every element, so it comes outside the integral and ∫dm = M.
  • E = 0 at the centre (r = 0) — every element is cancelled by the diametrically opposite one.
  • E is maximum at r = a/√2 (differentiate the result), after which it decays like GM/r² as the ring looks more and more like a point mass.
z = √(a² + r²), cos α = r/z
dE = G dm/z², axial part dE cos α = (G dm/z²)cos α
E = ∫(G dm/z²)cos α = GM cos α/z²
E = GMr/(a² + r²)^(3/2) ← field on the axis of a ring
E(r = 0) = 0; E is maximum at r = a/√2, where E_max = 2GM/(3√3 a²)
★ RememberE_max for a ring occurs at r = a/√2 and equals 2GM/(3√3 a²). This exact pair appears repeatedly in objective questions.

Field due to a uniform disc on its axis

  • Slice the disc of mass M and radius a into concentric rings of radius x and thickness dx; the ring's area is 2πx dx against the disc's πa².
  • Each ring's field is the ring result already derived, then integrate x from 0 to a.
  • The substitution r² + x² = z² (so x dx = z dz) turns the integral into ∫dz/z².
  • In terms of the half-angle θ that the disc's rim subtends at P, the answer takes the compact form 2GM(1 − cos θ)/a².
dm = (M/πa²)(2πx dx) = 2Mx dx/a²
dE = G dm r/(r² + x²)^(3/2) = (2GMr/a²)·x dx/(r² + x²)^(3/2)
E = (2GMr/a²)∫₀^a x dx/(r² + x²)^(3/2)
Substitute r² + x² = z², 2x dx = 2z dz ⇒ ∫x dx/(r²+x²)^(3/2) = ∫dz/z² = −1/√(r²+x²)
E = (2GMr/a²)[1/r − 1/√(r² + a²)] = (2GM/a²)[1 − r/√(r² + a²)]
E = (2GM/a²)(1 − cos θ) where cos θ = r/√(r² + a²)
Infinite sheet limit (a → ∞, surface density σ = M/πa²): E → 2πGσ, independent of distance
💡 NoteThe disc result is the gateway to the infinite-sheet field 2πGσ, which is distance-independent — the gravitational analogue of the charged-plane field σ/2ε₀.

Field due to a uniform spherical shell (the shell theorem)

  • Divide the shell of mass M and radius a into thin circular strips of angular width dθ; the strip at polar angle θ has mass dm = (M/2)sin θ dθ.
  • Each strip is a ring, so its axial field at P (distance r from the centre) is dE = (G dm/z²)cos α.
  • Use the cosine rule in triangle OAP twice: z² = a² + r² − 2ar cos θ (gives sin θ dθ = z dz/ar) and a² = z² + r² − 2zr cos α (gives cos α).
  • After substituting, the integrand becomes a clean function of z alone: dE = (GM/4ar²)[1 − (a² − r²)/z²]dz.
  • Everything then hinges on the LIMITS of z. Outside, z runs from r − a to r + a; inside, z runs from a − r to a + r — and the second range makes the bracket vanish identically.
  • Conclusion: a uniform shell attracts an external body exactly as if all its mass were concentrated at its centre, and exerts NO force anywhere inside its cavity.
dm = (M/2) sin θ dθ
dE = (G dm/z²) cos α = (GM sin θ dθ cos α)/(2z²)
z² = a² + r² − 2ar cos θ ⇒ 2z dz = 2ar sin θ dθ ⇒ sin θ dθ = z dz/(ar)
a² = z² + r² − 2zr cos α ⇒ cos α = (z² + r² − a²)/(2zr)
dE = (GM/4ar²)[1 − (a² − r²)/z²] dz ⇒ ∫dE = (GM/4ar²)[z + (a² − r²)/z]
Outside (r > a), z: r − a → r + a ⇒ E = GM/r²
Inside (r < a), z: a − r → a + r ⇒ E = 0
Summary (hollow sphere): E = GM/r² for r ≥ R, E = 0 for r < R
★ RememberShell theorem — the single most used result of this lecture: outside, a shell = a point mass at its centre; inside, the field is EXACTLY zero everywhere, not just at the centre.
⚠ TrapThe shell's field is DISCONTINUOUS at r = R: it is 0 just inside and GM/R² just outside. (A solid sphere's field, by contrast, joins up smoothly.)

Field due to a uniform solid sphere

  • External point (r > a): slice the sphere into thin concentric shells; the point is outside every one of them, so each contributes G dm/r² and the sum is simply GM/r².
  • Internal point (r < a): shells of radius greater than r give zero at P, so only the mass of the inner sphere of radius r counts.
  • That enclosed mass is M r³/a³ for a uniform sphere (mass scales with volume, i.e. with r³).
  • Hence E = G(Mr³/a³)/r² = GM r/a³ — the field grows LINEARLY from zero at the centre.
  • Graph of E vs r: a straight line from the origin rising to the peak GM/a² at r = a, then a 1/r² decay — continuous, with a sharp corner at the surface.
External (r ≥ a): E = ∫G dm/r² = (G/r²)∫dm = GM/r²
Mass enclosed within radius r: M_in = [M/((4/3)πa³)]·(4/3)πr³ = Mr³/a³
Internal (r ≤ a): E = G(Mr³/a³)/r² = (GM/a³)r
At the surface r = a both give E = GM/a² (they agree, so the graph is continuous)
Summary (solid sphere): E = (GM/R³)r for r ≤ R, E = GM/r² for r > R
★ RememberInside a solid sphere E ∝ r; outside E ∝ 1/r². The peak GM/R² sits exactly on the surface — a graph you are expected to recognise on sight.
💡 NoteSame field value, two places: GM/4R² occurs inside at r = R/4 and outside at r = 2R. Pairings like this are the whole content of 'at what distance is the field equal to…' questions.
⚠ TrapGauss-style reasoning only works for symmetric bodies: at radius r, count ONLY the mass inside r. Mass in shells outside r contributes nothing at all.

Reading the standard results together

  • Every field in this lecture reduces to GM/r² once you are far enough away — always use that as the sanity check.
  • Concentric shells: at any point, only the shells whose radius is smaller than r contribute; the larger ones are invisible.
  • Outside a whole system of concentric bodies, the field is G(ΣM)/r² — the system behaves as one point mass.
  • Neutral point between two masses M₁ and M₂ a distance x apart: equate the two fields and take square roots immediately.
Rod (perpendicular bisector): E = 2GM/(d√(L² + 4d²)); infinite wire: E = 2Gλ/d
Ring (axis): E = GMr/(a² + r²)^(3/2); E_max = 2GM/(3√3 a²) at r = a/√2
Disc (axis): E = (2GM/a²)(1 − cos θ) = (2GM/a²)[1 − r/√(r² + a²)]; infinite sheet: E = 2πGσ
Shell: E = GM/r² (r ≥ R), 0 (r < R)
Solid sphere: E = GMr/R³ (r ≤ R), GM/r² (r > R)
Neutral point: GM₁/r² = GM₂/(x − r)² ⇒ r/(x − r) = √(M₁/M₂) ⇒ r = x√M₁/(√M₁ + √M₂)

Book Example 1 · L-3
Suppose the earth increases its speed of rotation. At what new time period will the weight of a body on the equator become zero? Take g = 10 m/s² and radius of earth R = 6400 km.
At the equator the effective gravity is g′ = g − ω²R (latitude λ = 0, so cos²λ = 1).
Weight zero ⇒ g′ = 0 ⇒ g − ω²R = 0 ⇒ ω = √(g/R).
Write ω in terms of the time period: ω = 2π/T ⇒ 2π/T = √(g/R) ⇒ T = 2π√(R/g).
Substitute R = 6400 km = 6.4×10⁶ m and g = 10 m/s²: R/g = 6.4×10⁶/10 = 6.4×10⁵ s².
√(6.4×10⁵) = 800 s ⇒ T = 2π(800) = 5026.5 s.
Convert to hours: T = 5026.5/3600 = 1.396 h ≈ 1.4 h.
T ≈ 1.4 h (instead of 24 h) — then a body at the equator would be weightless.
⚡JEE trick: the 'weightless earth' period is just the period of a satellite skimming the surface, T = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 84 min ≈ 1.4 h. Memorise 84 minutes.
Book Example 2 · L-3
The angular speed of rotation of the earth about its axis at which the weight of a man standing on the equator becomes half of his weight at the poles is ____ ? (R = 6.4×10⁶ m)
Weight at the pole is unaffected by rotation: g_pole = g₀ (there r = R cos90° = 0, so no centrifugal term).
At the equator the effective value is g_eq = g₀ − ω²R (full centrifugal reduction, λ = 0).
Condition 'weight at equator = half the weight at the pole' ⇒ g_eq = g₀/2 ⇒ g₀/2 = g₀ − ω²R.
Rearrange: ω²R = g₀ − g₀/2 = g₀/2 ⇒ ω = √(g₀/2R).
Substitute g₀ = 9.8 m/s², R = 6.4×10⁶ m: ω = √(9.8/(2 × 6.4×10⁶)) = √(7.656×10⁻⁷).
ω = 8.75×10⁻⁴ rad/s (about 17 times the earth's actual ω = 7.27×10⁻⁵ rad/s).
ω = 8.75×10⁻⁴ rad/s

Book Example 3 · L-3

L-4

Gravitational Potential (Rod & Ring), Potential Difference and its Relation with Field

Gravitational potential of a uniform rod at a point on its axis

  • Set-up: a uniform rod of length ℓ and linear mass density λ lies along a line; the point P is on the same line, at distance a from the nearer end of the rod.
  • Take a mass element of length dx at distance x from the near end, so its distance from P is (a + x) and its mass is dM = λ dx.
  • Potential is a SCALAR, so the contributions of all elements are added algebraically — no vector resolution is needed (this is why the rod's potential is far easier than its field).
  • The element contributes dV = −G dM/(a+x) = −Gλ dx/(a+x).
  • Integrating x from 0 to ℓ gives a logarithm: V = −Gλ[ln(a+x)] from 0 to ℓ = −Gλ(ln(a+ℓ) − ln a).
  • Total mass of the rod is M = λℓ, so λ may be replaced by M/ℓ when the mass is given instead of the density.
dM = λ dx
dV = −G dM/(a + x) = −Gλ dx/(a + x)
V = ∫₀^ℓ −Gλ dx/(a + x)
V = −Gλ ln((a + ℓ)/a)
with λ = M/ℓ: V = −(GM/ℓ) ln((a + ℓ)/a)
💡 NoteOnly the RATIO (a+ℓ)/a enters the logarithm, so the answer is dimensionless inside ln — a quick way to check you have not mixed up a and ℓ.
★ RememberRod and ring are the only two continuous-body potential derivations in this lecture's syllabus.

Gravitational potential of a uniform ring at a point on its axis

  • Ring of mass M and radius a, centre O; the field point P lies on the axis with OP = r.
  • EVERY element dm of the ring is at the SAME distance z = √(a² + r²) from P — this is the whole reason the ring integral is trivial.
  • Each element contributes dV = −G dm/z = −G dm/√(a² + r²).
  • Since √(a² + r²) is the same constant for every element, it comes out of the integral and ∫dm = M.
  • Result: V = −GM/√(a² + r²), or, in terms of the slant distance z, simply V = −GM/z.
  • At the centre (r = 0) the potential is V = −GM/a, which is the maximum magnitude on the axis even though the FIELD there is zero.
  • For r ≫ a the expression reduces to V ≈ −GM/r, i.e. the ring looks like a point mass from far away.
z = √(a² + r²)
dV = −G dm/z = −G dm/√(a² + r²)
V = ∫dV = −(G/√(a² + r²)) ∫dm = −GM/√(a² + r²)
V = −GM/z (z = distance from P to any point of the ring)
At the centre (r = 0): V = −GM/a
⚠ TrapAt the centre of the ring the field E = 0 but the potential V = −GM/a ≠ 0. Zero field never implies zero potential — it only implies the potential is locally constant.

Gravitational potential difference

  • Gravitational potential is a scalar that equals the work required per unit mass to bring a unit mass from infinity to the given point in the field.
  • If the potential at A is V_A and at B is V_B, the gravitational potential difference between A and B is ΔV = V_B − V_A.
  • ΔV is the change in potential energy of a UNIT mass in going from A to B; equivalently it is the work done per unit mass against the gravitational force in moving from one point to the other.
  • Potential difference determines the direction and magnitude of the gravitational force on an object and is the quantity used in calculations of gravitational potential energy and of the orbits of celestial bodies.
  • For a particle of mass m, the change in potential energy is ΔU = m ΔV, and the work done BY the gravitational field is W = −ΔU = −m ΔV.
ΔV = V_B − V_A
ΔU = m ΔV
W (by gravity) = −ΔU = −m(V_B − V_A)
💡 NoteUnit of gravitational potential is J/kg (not volt); unit of gravitational field intensity is N/kg = m/s².

Relation between gravitational field intensity and gravitational potential

  • In a Cartesian system a point is fixed by (x, y, z), so both E and V are in general functions of three variables; for a point mass or a spherical body the single variable r (distance from the mass / from the centre) suffices.
  • Converting V → E needs DIFFERENTIATION; for several variables this is partial differentiation, with the other coordinates held constant.
  • The field is minus the gradient of the potential: E = −∇V, whose components are the three partial derivatives.
  • For one variable, E = −dV/dx = −dV/dr: the field equals the NEGATIVE of the slope of the V–x (or V–r) graph.
  • Converting E → V needs INTEGRATION of E along the path, with respect to the position vector: dV = −E·dr.
  • Integrating between two points gives V_B − V_A = −∫(A to B) E·dr, with the standard displacement vector dr = dx î + dy ĵ + dz k̂.
  • For a single variable this reduces to ∫dV = −∫E dr, i.e. V_B − V_A = −∫(a to b) E dr.
E = −gradient V = −[(∂V/∂x) î + (∂V/∂y) ĵ + (∂V/∂z) k̂]
E = −∇V
One variable: E = −dV/dx = −dV/dr = −(slope of V–x graph) = −(slope of V–r graph)
dV = −E·dr, dr = dx î + dy ĵ + dz k̂
V_B − V_A = −∫(A to B) E·dr
One variable: ∫dV = −∫E dr ⇒ V_B − V_A = −∫(a to b) E dr
★ RememberTwo-way master pair: V → E by differentiating (E = −dV/dr), E → V by integrating (V = −∫E·dr). Almost every JEE question in this lecture is one of these two directions.
⚠ TrapThe book prints the partial derivatives in places as total derivatives dV/dx, dV/dy, and in Example-2 even writes ∂V/∂x twice; the second component must always be ∂V/∂y.
💡 NoteA flat stretch on a V–x graph means E = 0 there; a steeper slope means a stronger field, and E always points in the direction of DECREASING V.

Gravitational Potential Energy, Relation Between Potential and Potential Energy; Self-Energy of a Sphere

Gravitational potential energy — definition from the conservative force

  • Potential energy is defined only for a conservative force field; gravity is conservative, so U can be defined.
  • The change in PE of a system in a conservative field equals minus the work done by that field: dU = −F·dr.
  • For gravitation the reference point is taken at infinity with U(∞) = 0, so U at any point is the negative of the work done by gravity in bringing the body from ∞ to that point.
  • The PE of a body/system is therefore the negative of the work done by the conservative force in bringing it from infinity to its present position.
  • The PE of two particles of masses m₁ and m₂ separated by r depends only on the two masses, the separation and G.
  • The negative sign means gravity does positive work in assembling the system from infinity — bound systems have negative PE.
dU = −F·dr ⇒ ∫ᵢ^f dU = −∫_{rᵢ}^{r_f} F·dr ⇒ U_f − U_i = −∫_{rᵢ}^{r_f} F·dr
Taking rᵢ = ∞ and U_i = 0: U = −∫_∞^r F·dr = −W
Two-particle PE: U = −Gm₁m₂/r
★ RememberU is a property of the PAIR (the system), not of one particle — never say 'the PE of m₂'.

Potential energy of a system of many particles — pair-wise summation

  • For a system m₁, m₂, m₃, m₄ … the total PE is found by taking every possible PAIR of particles and adding up each pair's −Gm_im_j/r_ij.
  • For 4 particles there are 6 pairs: (4,3), (4,2), (4,1), (3,2), (3,1), (2,1).
  • For an n-particle system the number of pairs is n(n−1)/2; the PE of each pair is computed and all are added to get the total PE of the system.
  • The total is always negative, and more negative means more tightly bound.
U = −G[ m₄m₃/r₄₃ + m₄m₂/r₄₂ + m₄m₁/r₄₁ + m₃m₂/r₃₂ + m₃m₁/r₃₁ + m₂m₁/r₂₁ ]
Number of pairs for n particles = n(n−1)/2
⚠ TrapThe book writes the pair count as n((n−1)/2) — read it as n(n−1)/2. For n = 3 it gives 3 pairs, for n = 4 it gives 6, for n = 6 (hexagon) it gives 15.
💡 NoteFor identical masses on a regular polygon, group the pairs by distance (sides, short diagonals, long diagonals) instead of listing all of them — e.g. a square of side l gives 4 sides of length l and 2 diagonals of length l√2.

Gravitational potential energy at height 'h' above the surface of the earth

  • Near the surface U = mgh, with g the acceleration due to gravity and h the height measured from the chosen reference (usually the surface). This form assumes h ≪ R.
  • At the surface (h = 0) the PE is U = mgh = 0 — there is no height difference from the reference point.
  • At height h the PE is non-zero and increases with height; the difference between the two positions is mgh.
  • Exact treatment: take point B on the surface (distance R from the centre) and point A at height h (distance R + h), with M the earth's mass.
  • U_A > U_B always (U is less negative higher up), so ΔU is positive when the body is raised.
  • Carrying out the subtraction exactly gives ΔU = GMmh/[R(R+h)], which rearranges to mgh/(1 + h/R) using g = GM/R².
  • Hence mgh is the difference in PE only in the limit h ≪ R.
U_B = −GMm/R (at the surface)
U_A = −GMm/(R + h) (at height h); U_A > U_B
ΔU = U_A − U_B = −GMm/(R+h) − (−GMm/R) = GMm(1/R − 1/(R+h)) = GMmh/[R(R+h)]
ΔU = GMmh/[R²(1 + h/R)] = mgh/(1 + h/R)
For h ≪ R: ΔU = mgh
★ RememberBOX RESULT: ΔU = mgh/(1 + h/R); h ≪ R ⇒ ΔU = mgh. This single formula is the whole lecture — every numerical in L-5 is one substitution into it.
⚠ TrapUsing mgh when h is comparable to R is the classic error. At h = R it overstates ΔU by 2×, at h = 2R by 3×. Check h/R before writing mgh.

Work done in raising a body from the surface of the earth to a large height 'h'

  • Raising a body does work against gravity; the work done BY gravity is the negative of the change in PE: W_gravity = −ΔU.
  • The body is raised slowly (no change in kinetic energy), so by the work–energy theorem the external agent's work equals the whole PE change: W_ext = ΔU.
  • With ΔU = mgh (for h ≪ R), the work done by the external agent is W = mgh and is positive, since lifting raises the body's PE.
  • The work done against the gravitational force equals the work done by the external agent, and both account for the change in potential energy.
  • For large h substitute the exact ΔU: W_ext = mgh/(1 + h/R).
W_gravity = −ΔU
W_ext = ΔU = mgh (h ≪ R)
W_ext = mgh/(1 + h/R) (any h)
💡 NoteSign bookkeeping: lift the body ⇒ W_ext > 0, W_gravity < 0. Let it fall ⇒ signs reverse. If the body also gains speed, W_ext = ΔU + ΔKE.

Self-energy of a sphere (formula only)

  • The self-energy of a sphere is the energy the sphere possesses because of the gravitational interaction between its own constituent particles (its binding energy, with a minus sign).
  • Derivation sketch: take a spherical shell element of thickness dr at radius r inside a uniform sphere of mass M and radius R.
  • m₁ = the mass already enclosed inside radius r = (M/((4/3)πR³)) × (4/3)πr³ = Mr³/R³.
  • m₂ = the mass of the shell element = (M/((4/3)πR³)) × 4πr² dr = 3Mr² dr/R³.
  • The PE of this inner-mass-plus-shell pair is dU = −Gm₁m₂/r.
  • Integrating r from 0 to R builds the whole sphere shell by shell, giving the total self-energy.
  • The integral reduces to −(3GM²/R⁶)∫₀^R r⁴ dr = −(3GM²/R⁶)(R⁵/5) = −(3/5)GM²/R.
  • The magnitude (3/5)GM²/R is the energy that must be supplied to disperse the sphere completely to infinity.
m₁ = Mr³/R³ , m₂ = 3Mr² dr/R³
dU = −Gm₁m₂/r
U = −∫₀^R Gm₁m₂/r = −G∫₀^R (Mr³/R³)(3Mr²/R³)dr / r = −(3GM²/R⁶)∫₀^R r⁴ dr
U = −(3GM²/R⁶)(R⁵/5)
U_self = −(3/5)GM²/R
★ RememberU_self = −(3/5)GM²/R — memorise it; the (3/5) comes from ∫r⁴dr and is specific to a UNIFORM solid sphere. A hollow shell gives −GM²/(2R) instead.
💡 NoteEnergy needed to break a planet up completely and scatter it to infinity = +(3/5)GM²/R. JEE phrases it as 'energy to remove all the mass of the earth to infinity'.

Escape Velocity and Orbital Velocity

Escape Velocity

  • Escape velocity is the minimum velocity required for an object to escape the gravitational field of a planet or any massive object.
  • It is the launch velocity at which the object's kinetic energy exactly equals the magnitude of its gravitational potential energy at that point.
  • Binding energy of a particle of mass m at rest on the earth's surface = GMm/R; supply exactly this much as kinetic energy and the particle just reaches infinity with zero speed.
  • Derivation: ½mvₑ² = GMm/R ⇒ vₑ = √(2GM/R); using g = GM/R² this becomes vₑ = √(2gR).
  • Substituting g = 9.8 m/s² and R = 6.4×10⁶ m gives vₑ = 11.2 km/s for the earth.
  • Meaning: an object must be launched at a minimum of 11.2 km/s to escape the earth's pull and travel to infinity.
  • vₑ is independent of the mass of the projected body, of the direction of projection and of the body's shape — it depends only on M and R (i.e. on g and R) of the planet.
vₑ = √(2GM/R)
vₑ = √(2gR) [since g = GM/R²]
Binding energy at the surface = GMm/R
vₑ(earth) = 11.2 km/s, vₑ(moon) ≈ 2.38 km/s
vₑ ∝ √(M/R); in terms of density, vₑ = R√(8πGρ/3)
💡 Notevₑ ∝ √(M/R) — use the ratio form to scale from the earth's 11.2 km/s instead of plugging G, M, R in afresh.
★ RememberEscape velocity does not depend on the mass of the escaping body or on the angle of projection; only the speed matters.

A Body Thrown with a Velocity 'n' Times the Escape Velocity

  • Let a body of mass m be thrown from the surface with speed nvₑ; its speed far from the planet follows from energy conservation.
  • ½mv² = ½m(nvₑ)² − GMm/R, and since GMm/R = ½mvₑ², the GMm/R term removes exactly one vₑ² worth of kinetic energy.
  • Result: v = √(2GM(n² − 1)/R) = vₑ√(n² − 1) — the speed left over at a very large distance.
  • For n = 1 the body arrives at infinity with v = 0 (the definition of escape velocity); for n < 1 it falls back.
½mv² = −GMm/R + ½m(nvₑ)²
v = √(2GM(n² − 1)/R) = √(n² − 1)·vₑ
💡 NoteLaunch at 4vₑ ⇒ far-away speed is √15 vₑ = 43.38 km/s, not 4vₑ = 44.8 km/s.

Orbital Velocity

  • Orbital velocity is the speed at which a satellite must travel to maintain a stable circular orbit around a celestial body.
  • The gravitational force supplies exactly the centripetal force needed: mv₀²/r = GMm/r².
  • Hence v₀ = √(GM/r), i.e. v₀ ∝ 1/√r — the orbital speed DECREASES as the orbital radius increases.
  • For an orbit close to the earth's surface (r ≈ R): v₀ = √(GM/R) = √(gR) = vₑ/√2 ≈ 7.9 km/s.
  • So a low-earth-orbit satellite needs about 7.9 km/s to stay in orbit; the extra (√2 − 1)√(gR) ≈ 3.28 km/s turns that orbit into an escape.
  • The satellite's own mass never enters v₀ or vₑ.
  • Related results used in the examples: angular momentum of a circular orbit L = m√(GMr); time period T = 2πr/v₀.
mv₀²/r = GMm/r² ⇒ v₀ = √(GM/r), v₀ ∝ 1/√r
Close to the surface: v₀ = √(GM/R) = √(gR) = vₑ/√2 ≈ 7.9 km/s
vₑ = √2 · v₀ (always)
T = 2πr/v₀ = 2π√(r³/GM)
L = m v₀ r = m√(GMr)

💡 NoteAlways use r = R_planet + h for the orbit radius; using the height h alone is the commonest slip.

Satellites: Launching, Orbital Motion, Time Period, Height and Energy

Launching of Artificial Satellites — the four stages

  • 1. First stage: a powerful rocket engine plus a large fuel tank lift the vehicle off the ground; it supplies the initial boost of thrust needed to overcome Earth's gravity.
  • 2. Second stage: once the first stage burns out it is jettisoned; a smaller engine and fuel system ignite to push vehicle and satellite higher and accelerate them towards the desired orbit.
  • 3. Third stage: gives the final velocity and trajectory that place the satellite in its intended orbit; it also carries the fairing — a metal/composite shield that protects the satellite from aerodynamic forces and the harsh environment during the ascent through the atmosphere.
  • 4. Fourth stage: beyond the atmosphere the satellite is separated from the upper stage so it can manoeuvre and operate independently; its own onboard propulsion/thrusters then fine-tune the orbit, maintain stability and perform the mission. The spent upper stage either continues on its own trajectory or is discarded.
  • The exact number of stages varies with satellite type, target orbit, launch vehicle and mission, but the principle — reaching the desired orbit through multiple staged burns — stays the same.
💡 NoteStaging exists because an empty fuel tank is dead weight: dropping each spent stage raises the mass ratio of what is left, which is exactly what the rocket equation rewards.

Motion of Satellites

  • Satellites move in BOUNDED orbits — they are gravitationally bound to the Earth, so the path is a closed curve, either circular or elliptical.
  • In the satellite's rotating frame the inward gravitational pull is balanced by the outward centrifugal force; in the ground frame gravity simply supplies the centripetal force, GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • If the speed at distance r suddenly DECREASES below the critical value √(GM/r), the centrifugal force falls short of gravity, the net force drags the satellite inward, and the path becomes an ellipse with the Earth at one focus (path-I, the smaller dashed ellipse).
  • If the speed at distance r is GREATER than √(GM/r), the satellite swings out into a BIGGER ellipse — again with the Earth at one focus — whose farthest point is the apogee (path-II).
  • Nearest point of an elliptical orbit = perigee; farthest point = apogee.
  • A circular orbit is only the special case of an elliptical orbit in which the satellite-to-Earth distance stays constant; the shape and size of the orbit are fixed entirely by the speed given at a particular distance.
  • Figure (p.227): earth of mass Mₑ and radius Rₑ centred at C; satellite of mass m at distance r moving with velocity v tangentially, with F_c (centrifugal, outward) and F_g (gravitational, inward) drawn along the radius; the dashed circle is the circular orbit, path-I the smaller ellipse, path-II the bigger ellipse.
Force balance: GMm/r² = mv²/r ⇒ v₀ = √(GM/r) = √(gR²/r)
v < √(GM/r) ⇒ smaller ellipse (earth at a focus); v > √(GM/r) ⇒ bigger ellipse; v = √(GM/r) ⇒ circle
★ Remember√(GM/r) is the 'critical value' the whole lecture keeps referring to: it is just the circular-orbit speed at radius r. Anything slower falls inward, anything faster swings outward.

Time Period of Revolution of Satellites

  • T is the time taken for one complete orbit around the parent body, measured in seconds, minutes or hours.
  • It follows from Kepler's third law — the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis; for an earth satellite the semi-major axis is essentially the mean satellite-to-earth-centre distance r.
  • T is directly proportional to the square root of the cube of r, so the farther the satellite, the longer the period; T also depends on M and G, but NOT on the satellite's own mass.
  • Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites have short periods, about 90 minutes to a few hours; higher orbits and geostationary orbit run from several hours to several days.
T = 2πr/v₀ = 2πr/√(GM/r)
T = 2π√(r³/GM) or T = 2π√(r³/gR²) [boxed in the book]
T² = 4π²a³/GM ⇒ a = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3)
T ∝ r^(3/2) ; f = 1/T ∝ r^(−3/2) ; ω = 2π/T ∝ r^(−3/2)
💡 NoteSubstitute gR² for GM whenever the question gives g and R instead of G and M — it kills one step and avoids the 10⁻¹¹ × 10²⁴ arithmetic.

Height of Satellites

  • First find the orbital radius r — the distance from the CENTRE of the Earth to the orbit — then subtract the earth's radius to get the height above the surface.
  • h = height of the satellite above the surface, r = orbital radius, R = radius of the Earth.
h = r − R
⚠ TrapThe commonest slip in this chapter: feeding the height h into a formula that wants the orbital radius r = R + h. Every v₀, T and energy formula takes r, never h.

Geostationary Satellites

  • A geostationary satellite is placed in an orbit directly above the EQUATOR with an orbital period exactly matching the Earth's rotation period (≈ 24 hours), so it appears fixed in the sky and stays above the same point of the equator at all times.
  • Its height is ≈ 35,786 km above the surface (≈ 3.6 × 10⁴ km), which means an orbital radius of ≈ 4.2 × 10⁴ km.
  • Applications: telecommunications — television broadcast, telephone links, internet connectivity — because the fixed position gives reliable continuous links; also weather forecasting and monitoring, since the satellite keeps a constant view of a large area of the Earth.
  • Advantage: the stationary position permits continuous communication and observation.
  • Limitations: the large distance introduces a time delay in the signals, which is a problem for real-time applications; and because it is fixed above the equator it gives poor coverage of the polar regions.
  • Derivation route: put T = 24 h in T = 2π√(r³/GM) and solve for r, using G = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² and M = 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg; then height = r − R with R ≈ 6,371 km.

height = r − R ≈ 4.2 × 10⁴ − 0.64 × 10⁴ ≈ 3.6 × 10⁴ km (≈ 35,786 km)
T² = 4π²a³/GM ⇒ a = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3)

Energy of Satellites

  • The potential energy of the satellite–Earth system is negative — energy must be supplied to pull the satellite out of the Earth's field — and is measured with U = 0 at infinity.
  • The kinetic energy is positive and, for a circular orbit, is exactly half the magnitude of the potential energy: K = |U|/2.
  • The total energy E = K + U is NEGATIVE, and that negative sign IS the statement that the orbit is bound. If E were zero or positive the satellite would have enough energy to escape the Earth's pull and move away to infinity.
  • E stays constant throughout the orbit as long as no external force acts — conservation of energy.
  • Graph (p.230): K is a positive branch falling as 1/r, U a negative branch rising as −1/r towards zero, and E = K + U lies between them, below the axis, also going as −1/r.
  • Correct relation to distance: as r increases, K decreases, U increases (becomes less negative) and E increases (becomes less negative), so the MAGNITUDE |E| gets smaller.
  • Binding energy — the energy that must be supplied to free the satellite from its orbit — equals |E| = GMm/2r.
U = −GMm/r
K = (1/2)mv₀² = (1/2)m(GM/r) = GMm/2r [boxed]
E = K + U = GMm/2r − GMm/r = −GMm/2r [boxed]
K = −E = |U|/2 ; U = 2E ; binding energy = +GMm/2r
K ∝ 1/r , U ∝ −1/r , E ∝ −1/r

Both are wrong, and the first is even self-contradictory ('more negative' and 'decreases in magnitude' cannot both hold). Since E = −GMm/2r, moving outward makes E LESS negative and |E| SMALLER. The book's own Example-4(vii) states it correctly.

Kepler's Laws, Weightlessness, Inertial and Gravitational Mass

Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion — the three statements

  • Kepler's laws describe the fundamental principles governing the motion of planets around the Sun; they laid the foundation of celestial mechanics and led on to Newton's laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation.
  • 1. Law of Elliptical Orbits (first law): every planet moves in an elliptical orbit around the Sun, with the Sun at one of the two foci of the ellipse. The path is not a perfect circle but a slightly elongated ellipse.
  • 2. Law of Areas (second law): the radius vector drawn from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. Hence the planet's speed varies along the orbit — faster when nearer the Sun, slower when farther away.
  • 3. Law of Time Periods (third law): the square of a planet's time period (the time to complete one orbit) is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit. The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year.
T² ∝ a³ (a = semi-major axis)
dA/dt = constant (areal velocity)
★ RememberFor a circular orbit the semi-major axis a is just the orbital radius r, so T² ∝ r³ — the form used in almost every numerical.

Explanation of the first law

  • Newton demonstrated that a body under an attractive force proportional to 1/r², such as gravity, can only follow a circular or an elliptical orbit; open orbits take the form of parabolas or hyperbolas.
  • The shape of the orbit is decided by the total energy of the system: total energy negative ⇒ circular or elliptical; total energy zero ⇒ parabola; total energy positive ⇒ hyperbola.
  • Orbits under a general attractive force F = K/rⁿ are stable only for exponents less than 3, i.e. n < 3.
F = K/rⁿ , orbits stable for n < 3
E < 0 ⇒ ellipse/circle; E = 0 ⇒ parabola; E > 0 ⇒ hyperbola
💡 NoteEnergy decides the conic: negative = bound (ellipse), zero = just escapes (parabola), positive = escapes with speed to spare (hyperbola).

Explanation of the second law — areal velocity and angular momentum

  • As the planet turns through a small angle dθ at distance r, the radius vector sweeps a thin triangle of area dA = (1/2) r² dθ.
  • Dividing by dt gives the areal velocity dA/dt = (1/2) r² (dθ/dt) = (1/2) r² ω; since the planet's angular momentum is L = m r² ω, this equals L/2m.
  • Gravity is a central force, so no external torque acts about the Sun; angular momentum L is therefore conserved and dA/dt stays constant.
  • Constant areal velocity implies the planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun and slower when it is farther away.
  • Here A is the swept area, L the angular momentum and m the mass of the planet.
dA = (1/2) r² dθ
dA/dt = (1/2) r² (dθ/dt) = (1/2) r² ω = L/2m
dA/dt = L/2m = constant
At the apsides (v ⊥ r): m v₁ r₁ = m v₂ r₂ ⇒ v₁/v₂ = r₂/r₁
r_max = a(1 + e), r_min = a(1 − e) ⇒ v_max/v_min = (1 + e)/(1 − e)
★ RememberKepler's second law IS conservation of angular momentum — the most-asked assertion/reason pair from this lecture.
⚠ Trapv ∝ 1/r holds only at perihelion and aphelion, where the velocity is perpendicular to the radius vector. Elsewhere only the perpendicular component obeys v_perp × r = constant.

Explanation of the third law

  • The square of a planet's orbital period T is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis a of its elliptical orbit.
  • The law was first applied to circular orbits; Newton showed the same relation holds for elliptical orbits on replacing the orbit radius r by the semi-major axis a.
  • Newton's precise form fixes the constant of proportionality in terms of the Sun's mass M_s, so a single orbit (radius plus period) is enough to 'weigh' the central body.
  • This law lets us compare the orbital periods and distances of different planets, giving insight into the structure and dynamics of the solar system.
T = 2πa^(3/2)/√(GM_s)
T² = 4π²a³/(GM_s) ⇒ M_s = 4π²a³/(GT²)
T ∝ r^(3/2) ⇒ T₁/T₂ = (r₁/r₂)^(3/2)
💡 NoteAlways convert 'height h above the surface' into orbital radius r = R + h before using T ∝ r^(3/2).

Weightlessness

  • A satellite in a circular orbit has a centripetal acceleration directed towards the centre of the Earth, of magnitude a = GM/R², where M is the Earth's mass and R the radius of the satellite's orbit.
  • A body of mass m placed inside the satellite experiences this same acceleration, so Newton's second law for it reads GMm/R² − N = m(GM/R²), giving the normal reaction N = 0.
  • Seen from the satellite's frame, a centrifugal pseudo-force m(GM/R²) acts on the body directed away from the Earth, and it exactly balances the body's weight GmM/R² (= mg).
  • With the forces balanced the body has zero apparent weight: it is not pressed against the floor or any other surface and floats freely inside the satellite.
  • Example: water inside a glass would not fall out even if the glass were inverted, because glass and water are essentially in free fall together, sharing the same weightlessness.
  • Overall, weightlessness in a satellite is a consequence of the balance between the gravitational force and the centrifugal force, leaving no apparent weight on objects inside.
a = GM/R²
GMm/R² − N = m(GM/R²) ⇒ N = 0
Weight = GmM/R² ; centrifugal pseudo-force = m(GM/R²)
⚠ TrapWeightlessness does NOT mean gravity is absent at that height — g is still large there (at h = R, g = g₀/4). What vanishes is the apparent weight N, because body and satellite fall together.

Inertial and gravitational mass

  • Inertial mass measures an object's resistance to acceleration when a force is applied. By Newton's second law F = ma, the acceleration is directly proportional to the applied force and inversely proportional to the inertial mass.
  • Apply the SAME force F to two bodies A and B: F = m_A a_A and F = m_B a_B, hence m_A/m_B = a_B/a_A, i.e. m_A = (a_B/a_A) m_B.
  • Taking B to be the standard kilogram (m_B = 1 kg), the mass of any object follows from measuring the two accelerations under equal force; the mass so defined is the inertial mass.
  • Gravitational mass measures the strength of the gravitational force an object experiences. By Newton's law of universal gravitation the force between two objects is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
  • Let the earth attract A and B with forces F_A = Gm_A M/R² and F_B = Gm_B M/R²; then m_A/m_B = F_A/F_B, i.e. m_A = (F_A/F_B) m_B. With B the standard unit mass, measuring F_A and F_B gives the gravitational mass of A.
  • Experiments show that the inertial mass and the gravitational mass of an object are always equal. This is the Equivalence Principle, a fundamental concept in general relativity: a body's response to gravitational forces is the same as its response to non-gravitational forces, so inertial mass may be used as a measure of gravitational mass and vice versa.
Inertial: F = m_A a_A = m_B a_B ⇒ m_A/m_B = a_B/a_A ⇒ m_A = (a_B/a_A) m_B
Gravitational: F_A = Gm_A M/R², F_B = Gm_B M/R² ⇒ m_A/m_B = F_A/F_B ⇒ m_A = (F_A/F_B) m_B
Equivalence Principle: m_inertial = m_gravitational
★ RememberBecause the two masses are equal, m cancels in mg = GMm/r² — which is exactly why every body falls with the same g, whatever its mass.
⚠ TrapThe Equivalence Principle belongs to general relativity, not to Newton's laws of motion — a favourite true/false trick in the Quick Recap.

'Weight' felt by a body is the normal reaction N from the surface it rests on, not the gravitational pull itself.
Work in the satellite's rotating (non-inertial) frame: the real inward force GMm/R² and the outward pseudo-force (centrifugal) m(GM/R²) both act on the body.

Neither force alone produces the effect — gravity alone would press the body to the floor, and a centrifugal term exists only because gravity supplies the circular motion.
Since the cancellation needs both the gravitational force and the centrifugal pseudo-force, the correct choice is (c).

For a two-body circular binary the angular momentum about the centre of mass is L = μ√(GMr) with μ = m₁m₂/M ⇒ L = m₁m₂√(Gr/M), and L is conserved (no external torque).
Take logarithms: ln L = ln m₁ + ln m₂ + (1/2)ln r + constant.
Differentiate in time: (ṁ₁/m₁) + (ṁ₂/m₂) + (1/2)(ṙ/r) = 0 ⇒ (1/r)(dr/dt) = −2[(ṁ₁/m₁) + (ṁ₂/m₂)].
Put ṁ(donor) = −γ and ṁ(gainer) = +γ: (1/r)(dr/dt) = 2γ/m_donor − 2γ/m_gainer.
The gainer is the heavy star, so the second term is negligible ⇒ (1/r)(dr/dt) ≈ 2γ/m_light : the orbit EXPANDS at twice the fractional mass-loss rate of the donor.
The only option of this form is 2γ/m₁, which requires m₁ to be the light (donor) star ⇒ answer (c).
(c) 2γ/m₁ [general result: (1/r)(dr/dt) = 2γ/(mass of the lighter, mass-losing star)]
