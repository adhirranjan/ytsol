Concept "Freely falling" is a technical phrase, not a loose description. It means the object is released (not thrown), so it starts from rest, and that gravity is the only force acting — air resistance is neglected. Those two statements fix everything: the initial velocity is zero, and the acceleration is constant in magnitude g ≈ 9.8 m s⁻², directed downward, for the whole fall. The option list mixes a genuine definition with three statements that either contradict the definition or depend on an unstated sign convention.
Formula Free fall from rest, taking downward as positive: u = 0 m s⁻¹, a = +g = +9.8 m s⁻², so v = u + at = 0 + gt = gt (m s⁻¹) and h = ut + ½at² = ½gt² (m). The "dropped from a certain height" wording is what forces u = 0; had the body been thrown, u would be non-zero. Assumption: air resistance neglected, g constant over the fall.
Solution
1. Read the stem literally: the object is freely falling from a certain height, i.e. it was released, not projected.Release means the hand imparts no speed, so at the instant t = 0 the body is momentarily at rest.
2. Therefore u = 0 m s⁻¹. This is exactly option (d), and it directly contradicts option (a) which claims u ≠ 0.(a) and (d) are mutually exclusive, so exactly one of them can be right — and the stem picks (d).

2A ball is dropped from a height H; at half way it is stopped momentarily and released; find the total time of descentFree fall interrupted mid-flightPhysics, Kinematics, Free Fall (4 of 12) Solving for Time to Fall from Known Height🟡 SimilarT = 2√(H/g)
Concept The fall is broken into two independent stages by the momentary stop. The crucial physical point is that stopping the ball destroys the speed it had built up: when it is released again it must start from rest all over again, exactly as if it were being dropped fresh from the mid-point. Gravity is unchanged throughout — only the initial condition of the second stage is reset. Because a body from rest is slow at the start of each stage, the interruption lengthens the total descent time compared with an uninterrupted drop.
Formula For each stage, from rest, with downward positive: s = ut + ½gt² with u = 0 gives s = ½gt² ⇒ t = √(2s ⁄ g). Here s is in m, g in m s⁻², t in s. √(m ÷ m s⁻²) = √(s²) = s ✓ — dimensionally the formula returns a time. Each stage covers s = H/2 m. Assumptions: air resistance neglected; the stop is instantaneous (takes no time); g constant.
Solution
1. Stage 1 — drop from the top through the first half. Initial velocity u₁ = 0 m s⁻¹, distance s₁ = H/2 m, acceleration g m s⁻².Released from rest at the top, so u₁ = 0.
2. Substitute into s = ½gt²: H/2 = ½·g·t₁² ⇒ t₁² = (H/2)·(2/g) = H/g s² ⇒ t₁ = √(H/g) s.Multiply both sides by 2/g: the 2 and the ½ cancel and the H/2 doubles back to H.
3. Stage 2 — the ball is stopped at the half-way point, so its velocity is reset to u₂ = 0 m s⁻¹, and then released.This is the whole trick: the √(gH) it had earned is thrown away, so stage 2 is another from-rest drop.
4. Stage 2 also covers s₂ = H/2 m from rest under the same g, so it is arithmetically identical to stage 1: H/2 = ½·g·t₂² ⇒ t₂ = √(H/g) s.
5. Add the stage times (the stop itself is instantaneous): T = t₁ + t₂ = √(H/g) + √(H/g) = 2√(H/g) s.
6. Compare with an uninterrupted drop through the full H: T₀ = √(2H/g) s. Ratio T/T₀ = 2√(H/g) ÷ √(2H/g) = 2/√2 = √2 ≈ 1.414.The interrupted ball takes about 41% longer — a sensible sanity check, since killing the mid-way speed can only slow it down.

Concept This is the mirror image of the "stopped and released" problem. Here the speed is preserved but the acceleration is removed. Newton's first law then takes over: with g gone there is no force at all, so the body keeps whatever velocity it had at the half-way mark and coasts through the second half at constant speed — uniform motion, not accelerated motion. So stage 1 is ordinary free fall (use the kinematic equations) and stage 2 is plain distance = speed × time. Removing g mid-flight lengthens the descent, because the body stops speeding up.
Formula Stage 1 (free fall from rest, downward positive): v² = u² + 2gs with u = 0 ⇒ v = √(2g·s) (m s⁻¹), and s = ½gt² ⇒ t₁ = √(2s ⁄ g) (s). Stage 2 (no acceleration, g = 0): t₂ = s ⁄ v (m ÷ m s⁻¹ = s). With a = 0 the equation s = ut + ½at² collapses to s = ut, i.e. uniform velocity. Each stage covers s = H/2 m. Assumptions: air resistance neglected; g vanishes exactly at the mid-point and instantaneously.
Solution
1. Stage 1 — free fall from rest through the first half: u₁ = 0 m s⁻¹, s₁ = H/2 m, a = g m s⁻².
2. Time for stage 1: H/2 = ½·g·t₁² ⇒ t₁² = H/g s² ⇒ t₁ = √(H/g) s.
3. Speed reached at the half-way point, from v² = 0 + 2g·(H/2) = gH m² s⁻²: v = √(gH) m s⁻¹.Units: √(m s⁻² × m) = √(m² s⁻²) = m s⁻¹ ✓ — a speed.
4. Stage 2 — g disappears. Net force = 0, so by Newton's first law the body continues at the constant velocity √(gH) m s⁻¹ it already has.Losing gravity does not stop the body; it only stops the body from accelerating. The speed it earned is kept.
5. Stage 2 is uniform motion over s₂ = H/2 m: t₂ = s₂ ÷ v = (H/2) ÷ √(gH) s.
6. Simplify t₂ by writing H = √(H)·√(H): t₂ = H ⁄ (2√(gH)) = √H·√H ⁄ (2√g·√H) = √H ⁄ (2√g) = ½√(H/g) s.One factor of √H cancels top and bottom, leaving ½√(H/g).

8. Sanity check against the normal drop T₀ = √(2H/g): ratio = 1.5√(H/g) ÷ 1.414√(H/g) = 1.06 — slightly longer ✓.It must be longer: over the second half the body plods along at √(gH) instead of continuing to speed up past it.

4The ratio of distances traversed in successive intervals of time when a body falls freely under gravity from a certain height is: (a) 1:2:3 (b) 1:5:9 (c) 1:3:5 (d) √1:√2:√3Ratio of distances in successive secondsNEET 2022 Physics — ratio of distances travelled by a freely falling body in 1st, 2nd, 3rd… · The ratios of the distance traversed, in successive intervals of time by a body, falling from rest🟢 Exactc1 : 3 : 5
Concept A body released from rest covers equal successive time intervals, and the distances it sweeps in those intervals grow by the odd-number rule (Galileo's law): 1, 3, 5, 7, … . The trap is to confuse the cumulative distance (measured from the start) with the distance covered within each interval. Cumulative distances go as t² (1, 4, 9), and it is their differences that give the per-interval ratio. Getting this straight is the whole question.
Formula Total distance from rest: s = ½gt², so after 1, 2, 3 equal intervals the cumulative distances scale as 1² : 2² : 3² = 1 : 4 : 9. Distance in the nth interval: sn = ½g[n² − (n−1)²] = ½g(2n−1). The bracket collapses to (2n−1), an odd number — the g and interval-length cancel out of the ratio, so it is universal. Take one interval = τ; here only the ratio matters, so g and τ drop out.
Solution
1. Write cumulative distances at the ends of intervals 1, 2, 3: s(1τ) = ½g(1)², s(2τ) = ½g(2)², s(3τ) = ½g(3)², i.e. proportional to 1, 4, 9.Distance from rest depends on the square of the elapsed time, so equal time steps give squares of 1, 2, 3.
2. Distance in the 1st interval = 1 − 0 = 1 (unit ½gτ²).
3. Distance in the 2nd interval = 4 − 1 = 3.Subtract the previous cumulative value — this is the "difference of squares" step students skip.
4. Distance in the 3rd interval = 9 − 4 = 5. Ratio = 1 : 3 : 5.
5. Cross-check with sn = ½g(2n−1): n = 1, 2, 3 → (2n−1) = 1, 3, 5 ✓ — identical, confirming the odd-number rule.

Trap: option (a) 1:2:3 is the ratio of cumulative distances at times 1τ, 2τ, 3τ only if you (wrongly) read s ∝ t; and √1:√2:√3 (d) is the time ratio for fixed distance, not distance for fixed time. Concept "Distance travelled in the nth second" is not the total distance after n seconds — it is the distance covered during the one-second window from t = (n−1) s to t = n s. Because a falling body speeds up steadily, each successive one-second window is longer than the last by a fixed amount. The beauty of this problem is that we never need to know n or g separately: the nth-second distances form an arithmetic progression with common difference g×(1 s²), so once you know one of them, the next ones follow by simply adding g.
Formula Sn = u + ½·a·(2n − 1) — distance in the nth second (m), with u in m s⁻¹, a in m s⁻², and n a pure number. For free fall from rest u = 0 m s⁻¹ and a = g m s⁻², so Sn = ½·g·(2n − 1) m. This comes from Sn = (total in n s) − (total in n−1 s) = ½gn² − ½g(n−1)² = ½g(2n−1); the "per 1 second" is implicit, which is why the units still read metres. Assumptions: dropped from rest, air resistance neglected, g constant.
Solution
1. Write the given: in the nth second the object travels X m, so with u = 0 and a = g: X = ½·g·(2n − 1) m. This is the one equation linking the unknowns X, g and n.
2. Write the (n+1)th second by replacing n with (n+1): Sn+1 = ½·g·(2(n+1) − 1) = ½·g·(2n + 2 − 1) = ½·g·(2n + 1) m.
3. Subtract to express it via X: Sn+1 − X = ½g(2n+1) − ½g(2n−1) = ½g·[(2n+1) − (2n−1)] = ½g·(2) = g m.The 2n terms cancel exactly, leaving only the constant g — this is why the increment does not depend on n.
4. Therefore Sn+1 = (X + g) m. With g = 9.8 m s⁻²: Sn+1 = (X + 9.8) m.
5. Now the (n+2)th second, replacing n with (n+2): Sn+2 = ½·g·(2(n+2) − 1) = ½·g·(2n + 3) m.
6. Subtract X again: Sn+2 − X = ½g[(2n+3) − (2n−1)] = ½g·(4) = 2g m.
7. Therefore Sn+2 = (X + 2g) m = (X + 19.6) m with g = 9.8 m s⁻².
8. Units check: X is in m and g×(1 s)² is in m, so X + g and X + 2g are both metres ✓ — the additions are dimensionally legal.

Concept A sign in kinematics is never a property of nature — it is a statement about the axis you chose. Gravity's direction is a physical fact: it always points toward the Earth's centre, i.e. vertically downward, whether the object is rising, falling, or at rest. What varies is the sign we attach to it, and that depends entirely on which direction we declared positive. The "standard physics coordinate system" takes upward as positive, so a downward-pointing gravitational acceleration must be written with a minus sign. The distractors all commit the classic error of letting the sign of a follow the object's motion.
Formula With the standard convention (up = +ve): a = −g = −9.8 m s⁻² for every phase of vertical motion. The minus records only that the acceleration vector points opposite to the chosen +ve (upward) axis — its magnitude 9.8 m s⁻² is unchanged. Key fact: a is constant at −g whether v > 0 (rising), v = 0 (at the top) or v < 0 (falling). Assumptions: near Earth's surface, air resistance neglected.
Solution
1. State the convention named in the stem: a "standard physics coordinate system" means the +y axis points upward.
2. State the physical direction of gravity: the Earth pulls the object toward its centre, i.e. downward. This is a fact about the force, independent of how the object is moving.The pull exists even for an object thrown upward or held at rest — motion does not create or steer gravity.
3. Combine 1 and 2: a vector pointing downward, in a frame where upward is positive, has a negative component. So a = −g = −9.8 m s⁻².
4. This is exactly option (d) — "negative, directed toward the Earth's centre, opposite to the chosen positive direction." It names both the physical direction and the reason for the sign.
5. Eliminate (a) "positive, as the object is moving downward": this makes the sign of a depend on the sign of v, which is wrong — a and v are independent.A ball thrown up has v > 0 but still a = −9.8 m s⁻²; if a followed v, the ball would never turn around.
6. Eliminate (b) "negative, but its direction is upward due to the object's motion": self-contradictory. In an up-positive frame, negative means downward.
7. Eliminate (c) "positive, because it is directed toward the Earth": the reason given actually proves the opposite — toward the Earth = downward = negative in this frame.

For free fall from rest the displacement grows as t², and the graph of a variable against the square of another is a parabola. The trap is mixing up the three kinematic graphs: the a–t graph is a horizontal line (a = g constant), the v–t graph is a straight sloping line (v = gt), and only the x–t graph curves as t². Naming which graph the question asks about is half the battle.
Formula From rest, downward positive: x = ½gt². Compare with the standard parabola y = kx² with k = ½g > 0. Any relation of the form (variable) = constant × (other variable)² plots as a parabola opening in the direction of the constant's sign. Companion graphs: v = gt (straight line), a = g (constant). Solution
1. Write the motion of a freely falling object released from rest: x = ½gt².u = 0 because it is "falling", so the ut term vanishes and only ½gt² remains.
2. Recognise the algebraic form: x is proportional to t² — this is exactly y = kx² with k = ½g.A power of 2 on the independent variable is the signature of a parabola, not a line.
3. Tabulate a few points (g ≈ 10): t = 0,1,2,3 s → x = 0, 5, 20, 45 m. The gaps 5, 15, 25 widen — the curve steepens.Equal time steps giving ever-larger position steps means the graph bends upward, ruling out a straight line.
