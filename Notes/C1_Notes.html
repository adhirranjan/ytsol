L-1

Mole, Pressure & Manometry

molesn = m/M = V/22.4 (at STP) = N/NA
  • Pressure P = F/A; SI unit = pascal (Pa).
  • Column / hydrostatic pressure — the master equation for barometers & manometers.
column pressureP = h · d · g (height × density × g)
Why P = h·d·g
A liquid column of height h and cross-section A has volume A·h, so weight = (A·h·d)·g. Pressure = weight/area = h·d·g — the area cancels, so column pressure is independent of the tube's cross-section. A barometer works because the atmosphere pushes mercury up until h·d·g balances Patm (h = 760 mm at 1 atm).

Manometer rules

SetupP(gas)
BarometerPatm = h·d·g
Closed-end (vacuum above Hg)Pgas = h (level difference, read directly)
Open-end, gas limb LOWER (gas stronger)Pgas = Patm + h
Open-end, gas limb HIGHER (gas weaker)Pgas = Patm − h
💡 Mnemonic"Gas low, pressure high (add); gas high, pressure low (subtract)."
⚠ TrapClosed-end manometer has no atmospheric term — never add Patm.

Book Example · L-1
A manometer is connected to a gas bulb. The open arm reads 43.7 cm, the arm connected to the bulb reads 15.6 cm; barometric pressure = 743 mm Hg. Find the gas pressure in bar.
Level difference = 43.7 − 15.6 = 28.1 cm = 281 mm. Gas limb lower ⇒ gas stronger ⇒ ADD.
P(gas) = 743 + 281 = 1024 mm Hg = 1.3474 atm; × 1.01325 bar/atm.
≈ 1.365 bar
Book Example · L-1
A closed-end manometer reads 100 mm in the closed arm and 70 mm in the arm attached to the system. Find the system pressure.
Closed end ⇒ pressure = difference of the two Hg levels = 100 − 70.
30 mm Hg
Book Example · L-1
An open-end manometer on a container shows a level difference of 60 mm (take Patm = 760 mm). Find the gas pressure.
Case I (gas > atm): P = 760 + 60 = 820 mm Hg.
Case II (gas < atm): 760 = P + 60 ⇒ P = 700 mm Hg.
820 mm Hg or 700 mm Hg
L-2

Temperature Scales · Boyle's & Charles' Laws

scalesT(K) = t(°C) + 273.15 · (C)/5 = (F − 32)/9
★ RememberCelsius and Fahrenheit read equal at −40°.

Boyle's Law (constant T, n)

BoylePV = k ⇒ P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (P ∝ 1/V)
  • P vs V → rectangular hyperbola; P vs 1/V → straight line.

Charles' Law (constant P, n)

CharlesV/T = k ⇒ V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ (V ∝ T in Kelvin)
  • V vs T(K) → straight line through origin. To double volume, double the Kelvin temperature.
The graphs that get tested
Each Boyle isotherm (PV = k) is a hyperbola; a higher-temperature isotherm lies farther from the origin. P–V is a curve, but P–(1/V), (PV)–P and (PV)–V are straight lines. For Charles, V–t(°C) is a straight line hitting V = 0 at −273.15 °C (extrapolated absolute zero).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-2
Convert (a) 30 °C to Kelvin, (b) 40 °C to °F.
(a) T(K) = 30 + 273.15. (b) 40/5 = (F − 32)/9 ⇒ 72 = F − 32 ⇒ F = 104.
(a) 303.15 K (b) 104 °F
Book Example · L-2
A 120 mL vessel holds a gas at 35 °C and 1.2 bar. The gas is transferred to a 180 mL vessel at 35 °C. Find the new pressure.
Constant T ⇒ Boyle: P₂ = P₁V₁/V₂ = (1.2 × 120)/180.
0.8 bar
Book Example · L-2
A gas occupies 2.5 L at 9 × 10⁵ N m⁻². Find the additional pressure needed to compress it to 1.5 L at constant T.
P₂ = P₁V₁/V₂ = (9 × 10⁵ × 2.5)/1.5 = 15 × 10⁵. Additional = 15 × 10⁵ − 9 × 10⁵.
6 × 10⁵ N m⁻²
Book Example · L-2
At what Celsius temperature will a gas at 0 °C double its volume at constant pressure?
V/273 = 2V/T₂ ⇒ T₂ = 546 K ⇒ t = 546 − 273.
273 °C
L-3

Gay-Lussac, Avogadro & Ideal Gas Equation

Gay-Lussac (const V,n)P/T = k ⇒ P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ (P–T curves = isochores)
AvogadroV ∝ n at same T,P ⇒ V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂
Ideal gasPV = nRT · combined: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂

Density & molar mass

densityd = PM/RT ⇒ M = dRT/P
Deriving d = PM/RT
From PV = nRT with n = m/M: PV = (m/M)RT ⇒ P = (m/V)(RT/M). Since d = m/V, P = d·RT/M ⇒ d = PM/RT. So at fixed T, P: d ∝ M; for one gas d ∝ P/T.
⚠ TrapMatch R to your units: 0.0821 for atm·L, 0.083 for bar·dm³, 8.314 for SI/energy.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
The density of a gas is 1.50 g L⁻¹ at 760 mm and 60 °C. Find its molar mass.
M = dRT/P = (1.50 × 0.083 × 333)/1.01325 (T = 333 K, R in bar·dm³).
≈ 41 amu
Book Example · L-3
A gas has density 5.46 g dm⁻³ at 27 °C and 2 bar. Find its density at STP.
d ∝ P/T ⇒ d₁T₁/P₁ = d₂T₂/P₂ ⇒ d₂ = (5.46 × 300 × 1.01325)/(273 × 2).
≈ 3.04 g dm⁻³
Book Example · L-3
A gaseous oxide at 1.5 bar and 27 °C has the same density as O₂ at 4.5 bar and 127 °C. Find the molar mass of the oxide.
d = PM/RT; equate densities: (1.5·M)/300 = (4.5·32)/400 ⇒ M = (4.5 × 32 × 300)/(400 × 1.5).
72 g mol⁻¹

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

DaltonPtotal = ΣPi · Pi = xi · Ptotal (x = mole fraction)
⚠ TrapValid only for non-reacting gases. Fails for reacting mixtures (NH₃+HCl, NO+O₂).

Gas collected over water (aqueous tension)

dry gasPdry = Patm − P(H₂O)
  • Aqueous tension = vapour pressure of water; depends on temperature only, not on the gas.
Worked examples from the module

0.5 L of H₂ at 0.8 bar and 2.0 L of O₂ at 0.7 bar are introduced into a 1 L vessel at 27 °C. Find the total pressure.
Each gas expands to 1 L: P(H₂) = 0.8 × 0.5/1 = 0.40 bar; P(O₂) = 0.7 × 2/1 = 1.40 bar.
Ptotal = 0.40 + 1.40.

A mixture has 96 g O₂ and 140 g Ne at a total pressure of 25 bar. Find the partial pressures.
n(O₂) = 96/32 = 3, n(Ne) = 140/20 = 7 ⇒ x(O₂) = 0.3, x(Ne) = 0.7.
P(O₂) = 0.3 × 25; P(Ne) = 0.7 × 25.
P(O₂) = 7.5 bar, P(Ne) = 17.5 bar
Book Example · L-4
82.1 mL of gas is collected over water at 27 °C and 1.0317 atm; it weighs 0.160 g. Aqueous tension = 0.0317 atm. Find the molar mass.
Pdry = 1.0317 − 0.0317 = 1 atm. M = mRT/PV = (0.160 × 0.0821 × 300)/(1 × 0.0821).

L-5

Graham's Law of Diffusion / Effusion

Grahamr ∝ 1/√M = 1/√d ⇒ r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁) = √(d₂/d₁)
with pressure / tempr₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁) × (P₁/P₂) · r ∝ √T (same gas)
  • Effusion = escape through a tiny orifice; Diffusion = spreading through space.
  • Lighter gas effuses faster. 4× heavier ⇒ only √4 = 2× slower.
Where 1/√M comes from
Rate ∝ average speed ∝ urms = √(3RT/M); at the same T the √(3RT) is common, so r ∝ 1/√M (≡ 1/√d). If rate is measured as volume/time (r = V/t), equal volumes give t₁/t₂ = √(M₁/M₂).
⚠ TrapIt's the square root of mass — a 4× mass ratio gives a 2× (not 4×) rate ratio.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-5
Relative densities of CO₂ and O₂ are 25 and 16. If 25 cc of CO₂ diffuses in 75 s, what volume of O₂ diffuses in 96 s under similar conditions?
r(O₂)/r(CO₂) = √(dCO₂/dO₂) = √(25/16) = 5/4.
V(O₂) = (96 × 25/75) × 5/4 = 12000/300.
40 cc
Book Example · L-5
An unknown gas diffuses four times as fast as O₂. Find its molar mass.
r(O₂)/r(x) = √(Mx/32) = 1/4 ⇒ Mx/32 = 1/16.
M = 2 amu (H₂)
Book Example · L-5
A 4:1 molar He:CH₄ mixture is at 20 bar. Gas leaks through a hole. What is the composition effusing out initially?
P(He) = 16 bar, P(CH₄) = 4 bar. r(He)/r(CH₄) = (PHe/PCH₄)·√(MCH₄/MHe) = (16/4)·√(16/4) = 4 × 2.
He : CH₄ = 8 : 1
L-6

Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT)

kinetic gas equationPV = ⅓ m N u²rms = ⅔ Ek
kinetic energyEk(total, n mol) = (3/2) nRT · per molecule = (3/2) kBT
★ Key ideaAverage kinetic energy depends on absolute temperature only — not on pressure, volume, or the gas's identity.

Postulates

  • Molecules = point masses (negligible volume); no intermolecular forces.
  • Collisions are perfectly elastic (KE conserved).
  • Average KE ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Constant random motion; pressure arises from wall collisions; straight-line travel between collisions (mean free path).
From PV = ⅓mNu² to KE = 3/2 nRT
PV = ⅓ m N u²rms = ⅔ · [½ m N u²rms] = ⅔ Ek. Equating to PV = nRT gives Ek = (3/2) nRT; per molecule Ek = (3/2) kBT.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-6
Calculate the average and total kinetic energy of 32 g of methane at 27 °C (R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹).
Avg per molecule = (3/2)(R/NA)T = (3/2)(8.314/6.022×10²³)(300) = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.
Total: n = 32/16 = 2 mol ⇒ Ek = (3/2)(2)(8.314)(300).
Avg = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J/molecule; Total = 7482.6 J
Book Example · L-6
An N₂ molecule has radius ≈ 0.2 nm (spherical). Find (a) volume of one molecule, (b) % empty space in 1 mol of N₂ at STP.
(a) V = (4/3)πr³ with r = 2 × 10⁻⁸ cm ⇒ 3.35 × 10⁻²³ cm³.
(b) Volume of 6.022 × 10²³ molecules ≈ 20.17 cm³; molar volume = 22400 cm³ ⇒ % empty = (22379.83/22400) × 100.
(a) 3.35 × 10⁻²³ cm³ (b) ≈ 99.9 % empty

Molecular Speeds (Maxwell–Boltzmann)

SpeedFormula
Most probable ump√(2RT/M)
Average uav√(8RT/πM)
Root-mean-square urms√(3RT/M) = √(3P/d) = √(3PV/M)
★ Orderump < uav < urms (ratio 1 : 1.128 : 1.224). All speeds ∝ √(T/M).
💡 TipGiven density instead of T, use urms = √(3P/d). Also urms = 1.085 × uav.

Distribution curve

  • Higher T → peak shifts right & lowers; curve broadens. Area = total molecules (constant).
  • Heavier gas at the same T → narrower curve shifted left (lower speeds).

≈ 683.9 m s⁻¹

Real Gases & Compressibility Factor

compressibilityZ = PV/nRT = Vreal/Videal
Z valueMeaningExamples
Z = 1Ideal gas—
Z < 1Attractions dominate; more compressibleCO₂, SO₂
Z > 1Repulsions dominate; less compressibleH₂, He
★ Ideal behaviourGases behave most ideally at low pressure & high temperature.
Z–P curves & Boyle temperature
Most gases dip below Z = 1 at low/moderate pressure (attractions win), then rise above 1 at high pressure (finite size wins). H₂ and He rise from the start (Z > 1 always). The Boyle temperature TB = a/(Rb) is where the initial dip vanishes and the gas behaves ideally over a range of pressure; above TB, Z > 1 (positive deviation).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-8
(i) What is Z for an ideal gas? (ii) For a real gas, what happens to Z above the Boyle temperature?
(i) Ideal gas: PV = nRT ⇒ Z = 1. (ii) Above TB the gas shows positive deviation.
(i) Z = 1 (ii) Z > 1
Book Example · L-8
1 mol SO₂ occupies 350 mL at 27 °C and 50 atm. Find Z and comment.
Z = PV/nRT = (50 × 0.35)/(1 × 0.0821 × 300) = 17.5/24.63.
Z ≈ 0.711 (< 1 → negative deviation, more compressible than ideal)
Book Example · L-8
N₂ data: P = 800 atm, Z = 1.95, T = −50 °C. Find the mass of N₂ needed to fill a 500 mL flask.
n = PV/ZRT = (800 × 0.5)/(1.95 × 0.0821 × 223) = 11.2 mol; mass = 11.2 × 28.
≈ 313.7 g (11.2 mol)

van der Waals Equation & Critical Constants

van der Waals(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT
  • a = intermolecular attraction (atm·L²·mol⁻²); larger a → easier to liquefy.
  • b = excluded volume (co-volume) = 4 × actual molecular volume (L·mol⁻¹).
What the two corrections fix
Real molecules attract, so they hit the wall softer — add an²/V² to recover the ideal pressure. They also occupy space, so subtract the co-volume nb. Low P → attractions dominate (Z < 1); high P → size term dominates (Z > 1).
critical constantsTc = 8a/27Rb · Pc = a/27b² · Vc = 3b · PcVc/RTc = 3/8 · Tb = a/(Rb)
★ Tb > TcSince Tb/Tc = 27/8. Higher Tc (larger a) ⇒ liquefies first on cooling.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-9
A 25 L vessel contains 10 mol of steam at 50 bar. Find its temperature using the van der Waals equation (water: a = 5.46 bar L² mol⁻², b = 0.031 L mol⁻¹).
T = [(P + an²/V²)(V − nb)] / (nR) = [(50 + 5.46×100/625)(25 − 0.31)] / (10 × 0.083).
≈ 1531.7 K
Book Example · L-9
For NO, Tc = 177 K and Pc = 64.5 atm. Find the van der Waals constants a and b.
a = 27(RTc)²/(64 Pc); b = RTc/(8Pc) = (0.082 × 177)/(8 × 64.5).
a ≈ 1.34 atm L² mol⁻²; b ≈ 0.0281 L mol⁻¹
Book Example · L-9
Critical temperatures: NH₃ = 405.5 K, CO₂ = 304.1 K. Cooling from 500 K, which liquefies first?
The gas with the higher critical temperature reaches its liquefiable range first.
NH₃ liquefies first (higher Tc); CO₂ needs more cooling
L-10

Liquid State

  • Liquid = intermediate state: definite volume, no definite shape, nearly incompressible; IMF between gas and solid.

Vapour pressure & boiling

  • Vapour pressure = pressure of vapour in equilibrium with liquid; a dynamic equilibrium (rate of evaporation = rate of condensation).
  • Weaker IMF or higher T → higher VP. Boiling point: VP = external pressure (normal bp at 760 mm); altitude → lower bp.
  • Evaporation causes cooling (high-energy molecules escape).

Surface tension & viscosity

surface tensionforce per length — N·m⁻¹ (= dyne·cm⁻¹). Surface energy = work/area, same value.
viscosityf = η·A·v/x · 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s = g·cm⁻¹·s⁻¹
  • Drops are spherical (min surface area). Surface tension decreases with T → 0 at Tc; viscosity decreases with T (~2%/°C).
  • Capillary rise: h = 2T cosθ / (r ρ g) — thinner tube → higher rise.
⚠ TrapSurface tension (N/m) vs surface energy (J/m²) — same dimensions and value, different names.

Intermolecular Forces

ForceBetweenExample
Dipole–dipolePolar molecules (permanent dipole)HCl, SO₂
Ion–dipoleIon + polar moleculeNaCl in water
Ion–induced dipoleIon + nonpolarNO₃⁻ … I₂
Dipole–induced dipolePolar + nonpolarHe + polar
London / dispersionAll molecules (only force in nonpolar)CH₄, noble gases
★ van der Waals forces= dipole–dipole + dipole–induced dipole + dispersion. Ion–dipole & ion–induced dipole are NOT van der Waals.
  • Dispersion ∝ 1/r⁶; increases with size/polarizability: CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄. Linear > spherical bp (n-pentane > neo-pentane).
  • Keesom (dipole–dipole): ∝ 1/r³ (stationary), ∝ 1/r⁶ (rotating).

Hydrogen bonding

  • H bonded to F, N, or O — the strongest intermolecular force. Raises mp/bp: HF > HCl, H₂O > H₂S, NH₃ > PH₃; increases water solubility (alcohols, amines, aldehydes, ketones).
Polarizability & the anomalous hydrides
Bigger electron clouds are more polarizable, so dispersion forces and boiling points rise down a group (CH₄ < SiH₄ < GeH₄ < SnH₄). The exceptions H₂O, HF, NH₃ boil abnormally high for their small size because of hydrogen bonding.
⚠ TrapIntramolecular H-bond (o-nitrophenol) → lower bp than intermolecular (p-nitrophenol).
