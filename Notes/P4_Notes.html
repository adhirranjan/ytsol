L-1

Rest, Motion, Distance & Displacement

  • Rest & motion are relative — defined only with respect to a reference point / frame.
  • Distance = actual length of the path travelled (scalar, always > 0). SI unit: metre.
  • Displacement = shortest (straight-line) vector from initial to final position (vector). SI unit: metre.
displacement vectors = Δr = rfinal − rinitial = x î + y ĵ  ·  |s| ≤ distance
When are they equal?
Only when the motion is along a straight line without reversal of direction is |displacement| = distance. Any turn, curve or return trip makes distance strictly larger. For a closed loop, displacement = 0 while distance = perimeter.
💡 Speed vs velocityAverage speed = total distance / time; average velocity = displacement / time. A body can have zero average velocity yet non-zero average speed (e.g. a round trip).

Book Example · L-1
Ria walks 5 km eastward, then 3 km southward, then 1 km westward to reach the ice-cream shop. Find the distance covered and her displacement from the school.
Distance = 5 + 3 + 1 = 9 km.
Net east = 5 − 1 = 4 km; net south = 3 km. Displacement = √(4² + 3²) = √25.
Distance = 9 km · Displacement = 5 km
Book Example · L-1
Ria walks along a rectangular path from P to R with PQ = 5 km and QR = 2 km. Find the distance travelled and the displacement.
Distance = PQ + QR = 5 + 2 = 7 km.
Displacement = diagonal PR = √(PQ² + QR²) = √(5² + 2²) = √29.
Distance = 7 km · Displacement = √29 ≈ 5.385 km
Book Example · L-1
Ria moves along a circular path of radius r = 8 km from P → Q → R → S, covering three-quarters of the circle. Find the distance and the displacement.
Distance = ¾ × 2πr = ¾ × 2 × 3.14 × 8 = 37.68 km.
P and S are ends of two perpendicular radii ⇒ PS = √(8² + 8²) = √128.
Distance = 37.68 km · Displacement = √128 ≈ 11.313 km
L-2

Acceleration & Equations of Motion

accelerationaavg = Δv/Δt = (vf − vi)/(tf − ti)  ·  ainst = dv/dt
  • Acceleration is a vector (SI: m·s⁻²). Speeding up ⇒ a along v; slowing down ⇒ a opposite v (retardation / deceleration).
  • A change of direction alone (constant speed) is also acceleration.
  • Uniform acceleration = equal Δv in equal Δt; non-uniform = unequal.
kinematic equations (constant a)v = u + at · s = ut + ½at² · v² = u² + 2as · sn = u + (a/2)(2n − 1)
Deriving v = u + at and v² = u² + 2as
From a = dv/dt, ∫du = ∫a dt gives v = u + at. Writing a = v(dv/dx) and integrating ∫v dv = ∫a dx gives v² = u² + 2as. Integrating v = u + at once more (x = ∫v dt) gives s = ut + ½at². All three are valid only when a is constant.
⚠ TrapThese three equations fail the moment acceleration varies with time — then you must use calculus (see L-5).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-2
An object on a straight path speeds up from 30 m/s to 40 m/s in 100 s. Find its average acceleration.
a = (vf − vi)/t = (40 − 30)/100 = 10/100.
0.1 m·s⁻²
Book Example · L-2
A particle in the x–y plane has x = 2t² and y = t³ (x, y in m, t in s). Find the average acceleration between t = 0 and t = 2 s.
vx = dx/dt = 4t, vy = dy/dt = 3t² ⇒ v = 4t î + 3t² ĵ.
At t = 0: v = 0; at t = 2 s: v = 8î + 12ĵ. aavg = Δv/Δt = (8î + 12ĵ − 0)/2.
aavg = (4î + 6ĵ) m·s⁻²
Book Example · L-2
Body A starts from rest with uniform acceleration a. Another body B starts from the same point at the same instant moving in the same direction with constant velocity v. After what time t do they meet?
They meet when SA = SB ⇒ ½at² = vt.
t = 2v/a
L-3

Acceleration Due to Gravity — Free Fall

  • Near Earth all bodies fall with the same acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m·s⁻², independent of mass (air resistance neglected).
  • Replace a → g and s → h in the kinematic equations.
dropped from rest (u = 0, down = +)v = gt · h = ½gt² · v = √(2gh) · hnth = (g/2)(2n − 1)
Height where v = ½ (impact speed)
Impact speed from height h is V = √(2gh). Solving for the height h′ where the speed is V/2 gives (V/2)² = 2g(h − h′) ⇒ h′ = 3h/4. So the body reaches half its landing speed only after falling three-quarters of the way down.
⚠ TrapDistance in the nth second, hnth = (g/2)(2n − 1), is a distance for that one second — its unit is metre, not m/s, even though it looks like a rate.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-3
A body falling freely under gravity passes two points 30 m apart in 1 s. From what height above the upper point did it begin to fall? (g = 9.8 m/s²)
Let it reach upper point B at time t₁: AB = ½gt₁²; AC = ½g(t₁ + 1)². Given AC − AB = 30 m.
(t₁ + 1)² − t₁² = 60/g ⇒ 2t₁ + 1 = 60/9.8 ⇒ t₁ = 2.56 s.
AB = ½(9.8)(2.56)² = 32.1 m.
≈ 32.1 m above the upper point
Book Example · L-3
Two diamonds begin a free fall from rest from the same height, 1.0 s apart. How long after the first begins to fall are the two diamonds 10 m apart? (g = 10 m/s²)
½gt² − ½g(t − 1)² = 10 ⇒ t² − (t − 1)² = 20/g.
2t − 1 = 2 ⇒ t = 1.5 s.
1.5 s after the first diamond is released
L-4

Object Projected Vertically Upwards

projected up with speed u (up = +, a = −g)hmax = u²/2g · tascent = u/g · tdescent = u/g · tflight = 2u/g
  • At the highest point v = 0 but a = g (still acts downward).
  • Time of ascent = time of descent = u/g; the body returns to the launch level with the same speed u (opposite direction).
  • Speed at a given height going up = speed at that height coming down.
Projection from a tower / building
Throw up with speed u from a tower of height h; the object lands on the ground below, so the displacement is s = −h. The equation becomes −h = ut − ½gt² (up positive), i.e. h = −ut + ½gt². This single quadratic in t handles the whole up-and-down flight — no need to split it.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-4
A ball is thrown vertically upwards with a velocity of 30 m/s. Find the time of flight, neglecting air resistance. (g = 10 m/s²)
tflight = 2u/g = (2 × 30)/10.
6 s
Book Example · L-4
An object falls from a height of 80 m to the ground. Taking g = 10 m/s², find the time taken to reach the ground.
u = 0 ⇒ h = ½gt² ⇒ 80 = ½(10)t² ⇒ t² = 16.
4 s
Book Example · L-4
Ria throws two balls simultaneously from the terrace with the same speed — one straight up, one straight down. The upward ball reaches the ground in 9 s, the downward ball in 4 s. Find the height of the building. (g = 10 m/s²)
The up-ball rises to C and returns to terrace level A (time 2tAC) moving downward with the same speed, then falls like the down-ball: 2tAC + 4 = 9 ⇒ tAC = 2.5 s.
Throw speed u = g·tAC = 10 × 2.5 = 25 m/s. For the downward ball: h = ut + ½gt² = 25(4) + ½(10)(4²) = 100 + 80.
Height of building = 180 m
L-5

Non-Uniform (Variable) Acceleration — Calculus

differentiatev = dx/dt  ·  a = dv/dt = v (dv/dx)
integratex = ∫v dt  ·  Δv = ∫a dt  ·  ∫v dv = ∫a ds
  • Use these whenever a (or v) is a function of t, x or v — the three constant-a equations no longer apply.
  • Choose the form that pairs the variables you have: a=f(t) → use dv=a dt; a=f(x) → use v dv=a dx.
💡 Tip“Velocity zero” means solve dx/dt = 0; “uniformly accelerated?” means check whether a = dv/dt is constant (free of t).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-5
The displacement-time equation of a particle along the x-axis is x = 20 + t³ − 12t (SI units). (a) Find its position and velocity at t = 0. (b) Is the motion uniformly accelerated? (c) Find the position when the velocity is zero.
(a) x(0) = 20 m; v = dx/dt = 3t² − 12 ⇒ v(0) = −12 m/s.
(b) a = dv/dt = 6t — depends on t ⇒ not uniformly accelerated.
(c) v = 0 ⇒ 3t² − 12 = 0 ⇒ t = 2 s ⇒ x = 20 + 8 − 24 = 4 m.
(a) 20 m, −12 m/s · (b) non-uniform (a = 6t) · (c) 4 m
Book Example · L-5
The motion of a particle is x = (2t − 3)² (x in m, t in s). Find (a) position, velocity and acceleration at t = 2 s, and (b) the velocity of the particle at the origin.
(a) x(2) = (2·2 − 3)² = 1 m; v = dx/dt = 4(2t − 3) ⇒ v(2) = 4 m/s; a = dv/dt = 8 m/s².
(b) x = 0 ⇒ 2t − 3 = 0 ⇒ t = 1.5 s ⇒ v = 4(2·1.5 − 3) = 0.
(a) x = 1 m, v = 4 m/s, a = 8 m/s² · (b) v = 0 at the origin
Book Example · L-5
Velocity of a particle in the x–y plane is v = 2t î + 3t² ĵ m/s. At t = 0 the particle is at (2 m, 4 m). Find (a) its acceleration at t = 1 s and (b) its coordinates at t = 2 s.
(a) a = dv/dt = 2î + 6t ĵ ⇒ at t = 1 s, a = 2î + 6ĵ m/s².
(b) s = ∫v dt = t²î + t³ĵ ⇒ r(2) = (2î + 4ĵ) + (2²î + 2³ĵ) = 6î + 12ĵ.
(a) a = (2î + 6ĵ) m·s⁻² · (b) coordinates (6 m, 12 m)
L-6

Graphical Representation of Motion

GraphSlope givesArea gives
Displacement–time (s–t)velocity—
Velocity–time (v–t)accelerationdisplacement
Acceleration–time (a–t)—change in velocity
  • Uniform speed ⇒ s–t graph is a straight line; steeper line ⇒ greater speed.
  • s–t line parallel to the time axis ⇒ body at rest.
  • Curved s–t (concave up) ⇒ increasing velocity ⇒ acceleration; curve flattening ⇒ retardation.
⚠ TrapThe s–t graph of a body can never be vertical — that would mean infinite velocity (two positions at one instant).

Book Example · L-6
Interpret an s–t graph that is a straight line passing through the origin and making an acute angle with the time axis. Give a real situation.
Constant slope ⇒ constant velocity; passing through origin ⇒ zero initial displacement.
Uniform velocity from rest position — e.g. Ria riding her bike at a steady 15 km/h on a straight road
Book Example · L-6

Negative slope ⇒ displacement decreasing uniformly with time ⇒ uniform velocity in the direction opposite to the initial displacement.
Body returning with uniform velocity — e.g. a car driving back along a straight road at constant speed
L-7

Motion in Two Dimensions & Projectile from Ground

2-D componentsv = vxî + vyĵ · |v| = √(vx² + vy²) · tanθ = vy/vx
  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent; velocity is always tangent to the path.
  • Projectile from ground: ux = u cosθ (constant), uy = u sinθ, ax = 0, ay = −g.
projectile (ground level)T = 2u sinθ/g · R = u² sin2θ/g · H = u² sin²θ/2g · Rmax at θ = 45°
Path is a parabola
Eliminating t from x = (u cosθ)t and y = (u sinθ)t − ½gt² gives y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u²cos²θ) — a downward parabola. At the top vy = 0, so only the horizontal component u cosθ survives; the speed is minimum there.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-7
A particle of mass 1 kg has a velocity of 2 m/s. A constant force of 2 N acts on it for 1 s in a direction perpendicular to its initial velocity. Find the velocity and displacement at the end of 1 s.
a = F/m = 2 m/s² (along ĵ, ⊥ to initial v along î). Along î: velocity stays 2 m/s, x = ut = 2 × 1 = 2 m.
Along ĵ: vy = at = 2 m/s, y = ½at² = ½(2)(1²) = 1 m.
v = 2(î + ĵ) m/s · s = (2î + ĵ) m
Book Example · L-7
At t = 0 a particle has u = (2î + 3ĵ) m/s and constant acceleration a = (4î + 2ĵ) m/s². Find its velocity and displacement at t = 2 s.
v = u + at = (2î + 3ĵ) + (4î + 2ĵ)(2) = 10î + 7ĵ.
s = ut + ½at² = (2î + 3ĵ)(2) + ½(4î + 2ĵ)(2²) = (4î + 6ĵ) + (8î + 4ĵ).
v = (10î + 7ĵ) m/s · s = (12î + 10ĵ) m
L-8

Projectile Motion with Complementary Angles

★ Key resultAngles θ and (90° − θ) fired with the same speed give the same horizontal range R, because sin2(90° − θ) = sin(180° − 2θ) = sin2θ.
for the pair θ, 90°−θT1/T2 = tanθ · H1 + H2 = u²/2g · R = 4√(H1H2) · R same
Why the ranges match but heights swap
Range depends on sin2θ, which is symmetric about 45°, so θ and its complement share R. But H ∝ sin²θ, so the steeper angle gives the greater height and the longer time of flight; the flatter angle gives the smaller height. Maximum range occurs at the self-complementary angle 45°.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-8
Show that the horizontal range is the same when a projectile is fired at angle θ or (90° − θ) with the same speed.
R₁ = u² sin2θ/g. R₂ = u² sin2(90° − θ)/g = u² sin(180° − 2θ)/g = u² sin2θ/g.
R₁ = R₂ — complementary angles give equal range
Book Example · L-8
An object is projected at 30° with the horizontal at u = 10 m/s, and again at 60° with the same speed. Find the range, maximum height and time of flight in both cases. (g = 10 m/s²)
At 30°: T = 2u sinθ/g = 2(10)(0.5)/10 = 1 s; H = u² sin²θ/2g = 100(0.25)/20 = 1.25 m; R = u² sin2θ/g = 100 sin60°/10 = 5√3 m.
At 60°: T = 2(10)(sin60°)/10 = √3 s; H = 100(0.75)/20 = 3.75 m; R = 100 sin120°/10 = 5√3 m.
30° → T = 1 s, H = 1.25 m, R = 5√3 m · 60° → T = √3 s, H = 3.75 m, R = 5√3 m (equal ranges)
L-9

Projectile Fired from a Certain Height

launched at angle θ from height hT = [u sinθ + √(u²sin²θ + 2gh)]/g
height & rangeH = u²sin²θ/2g + h · R = (u cosθ/g)[u sinθ + √(u²sin²θ + 2gh)]
  • Horizontal projection is the special case θ = 0: T = √(2h/g), R = u√(2h/g).
  • Two bodies thrown horizontally from the same height (any speeds) hit the ground at the same time — vertical motion is identical.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-9
A particle is projected from a tower of height 25 m with velocity 20√2 m/s at 45°. Find the time to strike the ground, the horizontal distance from the foot of the tower, and the velocity at impact. (g = 10 m/s²)
u sinθ = 20√2·sin45° = 20; u cosθ = 20. T = [20 + √(20² + 2·10·25)]/10 = [20 + √900]/10 = [20 + 30]/10 = 5 s.
R = u cosθ·T = 20 × 5 = 100 m.
vy = u sinθ − gT = 20 − 50 = −30 m/s; vx = 20 m/s.
T = 5 s · R = 100 m · v = (20î − 30ĵ) m/s
Book Example · L-9
An aeroplane flies horizontally at 600 km/h at a height of 1960 m. When it is vertically above point A, a bomb is released. Find the horizontal distance AB where the bomb strikes the ground. (g = 10 m/s²)
u = 600 km/h = 166.67 m/s, θ = 0. R = (u cosθ/g)[u sinθ + √(u²sin²θ + 2gh)] = (166.67/10)·√(2·10·1960).
= 16.667 × √39200 ≈ 16.667 × 198 ≈ 3300 m.
AB ≈ 3300 m = 3.30 km
L-10

Relative Motion in One Dimension

relative quantities (1-D)xBA = xB − xA · vBA = vB − vA · aBA = aB − aA
  • Assign a sign to each velocity first (choose a positive direction), then subtract.
  • Opposite directions ⇒ relative speed = sum of speeds; same direction ⇒ difference.
  • Relative speed = |vBA| (magnitude only, direction dropped).
Overtaking & crossing
On position–time graphs, two bodies with the same velocity plot as parallel lines (they never meet); if vB ≠ vA the lines cross where they meet. For one object to overtake / cross another of combined length L, use time = L / (relative speed).
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-10
Train A moves on a straight track from North to South at 60 km/h. Train B moves from South to North at 70 km/h. What is the velocity of B relative to A?
Take South → North as positive: vB = +70 km/h, vA = −60 km/h.
vBA = vB − vA = 70 − (−60).
130 km/h (directed South → North) — opposite directions add
L-11

Relative Motion in 2D — Rain & Umbrella

rain relative to manvrm = vr − vm  (hold the umbrella along vrm)
  • vr = velocity of rain, vm = velocity of man, vrm = apparent (relative) velocity of rain.
  • “Rain appears vertical” ⇒ the horizontal component of vrm is zero.
  • Resolve into î (horizontal) and ĵ (vertical) and match the given apparent angle.
💡 MethodWrite vr = a î + b ĵ, subtract vm, then apply each observation (vertical ⇒ î-component 0; angle ⇒ tan = |horizontal|/|vertical|) to solve for a and b.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-11
To a man walking at 3 km/h the rain appears to fall vertically. When he speeds up to 6 km/h it appears to meet him at 45° with the vertical. Find the speed of the rain.
Let vr = a î + b ĵ. At 3 km/h: vrm = (a − 3)î + b ĵ; vertical ⇒ a − 3 = 0 ⇒ a = 3.
At 6 km/h: vrm = (3 − 6)î + b ĵ = −3î + b ĵ; 45° with vertical ⇒ |b| = 3.
|vr| = √(a² + b²) = √(9 + 9).
Speed of rain = 3√2 km/h
Book Example · L-11
A man moving down an incline (37° with horizontal) at 5 m/s finds the rain falling vertically. Moving up the same incline at the same speed, he finds the rain makes θ = tan⁻¹(7/8) with the horizontal. Find the speed of the rain.
Let vr = a î + b ĵ. Down-incline vm = −4î − 3ĵ ⇒ vrm = (a + 4)î + (b + 3)ĵ vertical ⇒ a + 4 = 0 ⇒ a = −4.
Up-incline vm = 4î + 3ĵ ⇒ vrm = −8î + (b − 3)ĵ; tanθ = |b − 3|/8 = 7/8 ⇒ |b − 3| = 7 ⇒ b = −4 (reject b = 10).
|vr| = √((−4)² + (−4)²) = √32.
Speed of rain = √32 ≈ 5.66 m/s
L-12

River-Boat & Wind-Aeroplane Problems

boat (heading θ from the normal)vb = vr + vbr · t = d/(vbr cosθ) · drift x = (vr − vbr sinθ)·t
  • vr = river velocity, vbr = boat speed w.r.t. water, vb = ground velocity of boat.
  • Minimum-time crossing: head straight across (θ = 0) ⇒ tmin = d/vbr (but you drift downstream).
  • Shortest-path crossing: aim upstream so drift = 0 ⇒ sinθ = vr/vbr (needs vr < vbr).
Wind–aeroplane = same idea
Replace vbr → vaw (air speed of plane) and vr → vw (wind), with va = vaw + vw the ground velocity. To fly straight from A to B, head the plane so the wind is cancelled across the track; find the heading with the sine rule and the ground speed from va = √(vaw² − vw²) when the wind is perpendicular to AB.
Worked examples from the module
Book Example · L-12
A river 400 m wide flows at 2.0 m/s. A boat sails at 10.0 m/s w.r.t. the water, headed perpendicular to the river. (a) Find the time to reach the opposite bank. (b) How far downstream from the point directly opposite does it land?
(a) θ = 0 ⇒ t = d/(vbr cos0°) = 400/10 = 40 s.
(b) drift x = (vr − vbr sin0°)·t = (2 − 0)(40).
(a) 40 s · (b) 80 m downstream
Book Example · L-12
A man crosses a river. Crossing in minimum time takes 10 min with a drift of 120 m. Crossing by the shortest path takes 12.5 min. Find (a) the width of the river, (b) the boat speed w.r.t. water, (c) the speed of the current.
Min time (θ = 0): t = 600 s = d/vbr ⇒ d = 600 vbr. Drift 120 = vr·600 ⇒ vr = 0.2 m/s.
Shortest path: 750 = d/(vbr√(1 − (vr/vbr)²)); sub d = 600 vbr ⇒ 1 − (vr/vbr)² = (600/750)² = 16/25 ⇒ (vr/vbr)² = 9/25.
vbr = 5vr/3 = 5(0.2)/3 = ⅓ m/s; d = 600(⅓).
(a) width = 200 m · (b) vbr = ⅓ m/s · (c) vr = 0.2 m/s
Book Example · L-12
An aeroplane must fly from P to Q, 1000 km due north. Wind blows due east at 200 km/h; the plane's air speed is 500 km/h. (a) In which direction should the pilot head? (b) Find the time from P to Q.
(a) To cancel the eastward wind: sinθ = vw/vaw = 200/500 = 0.4 ⇒ θ = sin⁻¹(0.4) west of north.
(b) Ground speed va = √(vaw² − vw²) = √(500² − 200²) = 100√21 km/h; t = 1000/(100√21).
(a) sin⁻¹(0.4) west of north · (b) t = 10/√21 h ≈ 2.18 h
