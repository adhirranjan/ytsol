1Unit of measurement of an angle in the Circular SystemSystems of measurementMeasurement of Angle — 3 systems🟡 Similarcradian
Concept An angle can be measured in three different systems, and each has its own unit. The Sexagesimal (British) system uses the degree (a full turn = 360°); the Centesimal (French) system uses the grade (a full turn = 400ᵍ); and the Circular (radian) system uses the radian. The radian is the one system tied to the geometry of the circle itself, which is why calculus and all higher maths use it.
Definition 1 radian = the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. Because arc length s = rθ, when the arc equals the radius (s = r) the angle θ = 1 — so the radian is a pure ratio of arc to radius, independent of the circle's size. A full circle (circumference 2πr) subtends 2π radians.
Solution
1. List the three systems and their units:Sexagesimal → degree (°); Centesimal → grade (ᵍ); Circular → radian (ᶜ).
2. The question asks specifically for the Circular System unit.
3. Therefore the unit is the radian (symbol ᶜ or "rad").
 system         a full circle    unit of angle
 Sexagesimal    360°             degree
 Centesimal     400ᵍ             grade
 Circular       2π               radian   <-- asked
⚡ JEE Trick Anchor everything to a full turn: 360° = 400ᵍ = 2π radians. From this one identity you can convert between any two systems instantly (e.g. 1 rad = 180/π degrees). Trap: the "radian" is dimensionless — it is a length ÷ length ratio, so it is often written with no unit at all.
Answer: radian (the unit of angle in the Circular / radian system).
2Convert 180° into radiansDegree → radianConvert 180° to radians🟢 Exactaπ radians (πᶜ)
Concept Degrees and radians are two scales for the same rotation. A full turn is 360° in the sexagesimal system and 2π radians in the circular system — because a circle of radius r has circumference 2πr, and the radian counts "how many radius-lengths of arc" the angle sweeps. Halving the full-turn identity gives the single most-used fact in all of trigonometry: 180° = π radians. Converting 180° is therefore the defining identity itself, verified by the standard conversion factor.
Formula radians = degrees × (π / 180). Since 360° = 2π rad, dividing both sides by 360 gives 1° = π/180 rad; multiplying by the number of degrees converts the whole angle.
Solution
1. Start from the full-circle identity: 360° = 2π radians.One revolution wraps the whole circumference 2πr around the centre, and 2πr ÷ r = 2π radii of arc.
2. Divide both sides by 2: 180° = π radians — a straight angle is exactly π.
3. Confirm with the factor: radians = 180 × (π/180) = π. The 180's cancel completely, leaving exactly π, with no decimals to compute.
4. Sanity check numerically: π ≈ 3.1416 rad and 1 rad ≈ 57.3°, so 3.1416 × 57.3° ≈ 180° ✓.
5. So 180° = πᶜ, matching option (a).The superscript ᶜ marks a radian (circular-system) measure.

⚡ JEE Trick Burn in the anchor 180° = π; every common angle is a fraction of it (90° = π/2, 60° = π/3, 45° = π/4, 30° = π/6) — never re-derive these in the exam. Trap: writing 180° = 2π. That is the full turn (360°); a straight angle is only half of it, so 180° = π, not 2π.
Answer: (a) 180° = π radians (πᶜ ≈ 3.1416).

6Sum of two angles = 5πᶜ, difference = 60°; in degreesSystems + equationsSum 5πᶜ, difference 60°🟢 Exact480°, 420°
Concept Two unknown angles are pinned down by two facts — their sum and their difference — but the two facts are quoted in different systems: the sum in radians (5πᶜ), the difference in degrees (60°). Before any algebra, convert both statements into ONE unit (degrees is easiest here), then solve the classic sum-and-difference pair by adding and subtracting the equations. Note the angles are allowed to exceed 360° — nothing restricts them to one revolution.
Formula radians → degrees: × 180/π; then for angles A > B: A + B = S, A − B = D ⇒ A = (S+D)/2, B = (S−D)/2. Adding the equations eliminates B; subtracting eliminates A — one unknown at a time.
Solution
1. Convert the sum: 5π rad = 5 × 180° = 900°.Each π of radians is one straight angle, 180°; the π cancels against 180/π.
2. Write the system in degrees: A + B = 900° and A − B = 60°.
3. Add the equations: 2A = 900 + 60 = 960 ⇒ A = 480°.Adding makes the +B and −B cancel, isolating A in one line.
4. Subtract them: 2B = 900 − 60 = 840 ⇒ B = 420°.
5. Verify: 480 + 420 = 900° = 5π rad ✓ and 480 − 420 = 60° ✓.Both original statements are satisfied, so no root was lost.
      A + B = 900°   (5πᶜ = 5 × 180°)
      A − B =  60°

⚡ JEE Trick Sum-and-difference pairs need no elimination steps: bigger = (sum + diff)/2, smaller = (sum − diff)/2 — straight to 480° and 420°. Trap: mixing units — 5πᶜ is 900°, not 5π "degrees"; and do not "reduce" 480° to 120° — the question asks for the angles themselves, which genuinely exceed a full turn.
Answer: the two angles are 480° and 420°.

Concept Angles are co-terminal when, drawn in standard position (vertex at origin, initial arm on the positive x-axis), their terminal arms coincide. Adding or removing whole revolutions (multiples of 360°) never moves the terminal arm, so co-terminal angles differ by 360°k, k ∈ ℤ. A negative angle like −45° is measured 45° clockwise from the positive x-axis; its positive co-terminal partner is found by adding one full turn.
Formula co-terminal angles of θ: θ + 360°k, k = ±1, ±2, … Each complete 360° rotation returns the terminal arm to exactly the same ray, so only the remainder mod 360° matters.
Solution
1. Take θ = −45° and add one full revolution: −45° + 360° = 315°.k = 1 gives the smallest positive co-terminal angle.
2. Confirm the position: −45° points 45° below the positive x-axis, into Quadrant IV; 315° = 360° − 45° lands on the same ray ✓.
3. Test by the general rule: 315° − (−45°) = 360° = 360° × 1, an integer multiple of 360°, so they are co-terminal.
4. Rule out near-misses: 45° differs from −45° by 90° (not a multiple of 360°), and −315° differs by −270° — neither is co-terminal; only 315° qualifies.
5. Other valid partners would be 675°, −405°, … but among the options 315° is the one.

Concept In any right-angled triangle one angle is 90°, so the other two (the acute angles) must add up to the remaining 90° — they are complementary. Given their difference as well, we have two facts (a sum and a difference) about two unknowns, which is a classic pair of simultaneous equations solved by adding and subtracting. The one subtlety: the difference is given in radians, so convert it to degrees first to keep both equations in the same unit.
Formula For acute angles A and B: A + B = 90° (angle sum minus the right angle) and A − B = given difference. Adding the two equations gives 2A; subtracting gives 2B — the standard sum-and-difference method. Convert with radians × 180/π.
Solution
1. Convert the difference to degrees: 3π/10 rad × 180/π = 3 × 180/10 = 54°.The π cancels; 3×180 = 540, ÷10 = 54.
2. Write the two equations: A + B = 90° and A − B = 54°.
3. Add them: (A+B) + (A−B) = 90 + 54 → 2A = 144 → A = 72°.
4. Subtract: (A+B) − (A−B) = 90 − 54 → 2B = 36 → B = 18°.
5. Check: 72 + 18 = 90 ✓ (complementary) and 72 − 18 = 54 ✓. In radians A = 2π/5, B = π/10.

Concept The interior angles of any quadrilateral add to 360° (= 2π radians). When the four angles stand in a ratio, introduce one multiplier x: the angles are 3x, 4x, 5x, 6x. The fixed angle-sum turns the ratio into an equation in x alone. Solve, list the angles in degrees, then convert each to radians with ×π/180 — or, faster, work the whole ratio directly against 2π.
Formula sum of interior angles of a quadrilateral = 360° = 2π rad; ratio a:b:c:d ⇒ (a+b+c+d)x = 360°. A quadrilateral splits into two triangles by a diagonal, each contributing 180°, hence 360° total.
Solution
1. Let the angles be 3x, 4x, 5x, 6x. Sum of parts: 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 18.
2. Impose the angle-sum: 18x = 360° ⇒ x = 360/18 = 20°.x is the size of one ratio-part in degrees.
