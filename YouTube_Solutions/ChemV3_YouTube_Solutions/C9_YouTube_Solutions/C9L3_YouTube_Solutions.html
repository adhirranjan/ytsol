1 (i)Fill in the blank: Hardness of water is due to the presence of ________ and ________ of ________ calcium and magnesium.Cause of hardnessHardness of water is due to the presence of and of calcium and magnesium. 🟢 ExactBicarbonates, Chlorides, Sulphates
Concept Hardness is the failure of water to lather with soap. It comes from dissolved salts of calcium and magnesium only; sodium and potassium salts do not harden water. The anion decides whether the hardness is temporary or permanent.
Key relation Hardness = Ca2+/Mg2+ + HCO3−, Cl−, SO42−; soap (RCOONa) + Ca2+ → (RCOO)2Ca↓ The insoluble scum is what wastes soap and stops lather, which is how hardness shows itself.
Solution
1. Hardness arises when rain water, which is nearly pure, trickles over rocks and dissolves salts of Ca and Mg.
2. Limestone and dolomite, CaCO3 and MgCO3, dissolve in CO2-rich water as the bicarbonates Ca(HCO3)2 and Mg(HCO3)2. CaCO3 + CO2 + H2O → Ca(HCO3)2
3. Gypsum and Epsom-type minerals supply the sulphates CaSO4 and MgSO4.
4. Contaminated rock salt and sea-water seepage supply the chlorides CaCl2 and MgCl2.
5. The blanks are three anions, so the answer is bicarbonates, chlorides and sulphates (of calcium and magnesium).
6. Soap reacts with these cations: 2C17H35COONa + Ca2+ → (C17H35COO)2Ca + 2Na+, a curdy precipitate, so no lather forms until all Ca2+/Mg2+ is used up.
7. Bicarbonates give temporary hardness; chlorides and sulphates give permanent hardness.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the split: bicarbonate = temporary (removed by boiling); chloride and sulphate = permanent. Na+ and K+ salts never cause hardness, so NaCl in water is not a trap answer here.
Answer: bicarbonates, chlorides and sulphates (of calcium and magnesium).
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: Calgon is the trade name of ________.CalgonCalgon is the trade name of. 🟢 ExactSodium hexametaphosphate
Concept Calgon is the commercial name of a polyphosphate that softens water by sequestering Ca2+ and Mg2+ in a soluble complex instead of precipitating them. The key as printed reads “Sodium hexa interphase”, a misprint for hexametaphosphate.
Key relation Calgon = Na6P6O18 = Na2[Na4(PO3)6]; Na2[Na4(PO3)6] + 2Ca2+ → Na2[Ca2(PO3)6] + 4Na+ Ca2+ displaces Na+ from the large anion, giving a soluble complex.
Solution
1. Calgon is the trade name of sodium hexametaphosphate, also called Graham’s salt, formula (NaPO3)6 = Na6P6O18.
2. The printed key word “hexa interphase” is a misprint (the Match question in this same recap says “sodium hexa meta phosphate”). The intended answer is hexametaphosphate; this is a misprint, not a wrong answer.
3. Writing the salt as Na2[Na4(PO3)6] shows two Na+ outside and four inside the complex anion.
4. Hard water supplies M2+: Na2[Na4(PO3)6] + 2M2+ → Na2[M2(PO3)6] + 4Na+.
5. Here M2+ sits inside the anion, so the Ca2+/Mg2+ can no longer react with soap. No precipitate forms, so no sludge.
6. Both temporary and permanent hardness are removed, because the method acts on the cations regardless of the anion.
7. The treated water keeps Na+ but the soluble complex does not form scum, so it can be used for boilers and laundry.
⚡ JEE Trick Calgon sequesters rather than precipitates: no solid forms, which is why it suits boiler feed water. Do not confuse hexametaphosphate with metaphosphoric acid (HPO3)n or with Na3PO4.
Answer: sodium hexametaphosphate, Na6P6O18 (key prints “hexa interphase”, a misprint).
1 (iii)Fill in the blank: Heavy water is used as a ________ in ________ nuclear reactors.Use of heavy waterHeavy water is used as a moderator in a nuclear reactor. 🟢 ExactModerator
Concept Neutrons from fission are fast and must be slowed to thermal energy before they split more U-235. D2O is an excellent moderator because deuterium slows neutrons efficiently yet absorbs very few of them. The blank asks for its role, not the type of reactor.
Key relation fast neutron (about 2 MeV) + D2O (moderator) → thermal neutron (about 0.025 eV) Neutron collisions with D nuclei transfer energy because D has mass comparable to the neutron.
Solution
1. Fission of 235U releases fast neutrons, but the fission cross-section is far larger for slow (thermal) neutrons.
2. A moderator slows neutrons through elastic collisions without capturing them.
3. Ordinary H2O slows neutrons well but captures them (1H + n → 2H), so a reactor using it needs enriched uranium.
4. Deuterium has a much smaller neutron-capture cross-section, so D2O moderates with little loss and natural uranium can sustain a chain reaction.
5. In a pressurised heavy-water reactor (CANDU type) D2O also acts as the coolant, but the primary role the blank asks for is moderator.
6. Control rods (Cd, B) absorb neutrons to regulate the reaction and are a different component, not heavy water.
7. So heavy water is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors.
⚡ JEE Trick Moderator slows, control rod absorbs, coolant removes heat. If an option says “control rod” for D2O, reject it; absorbing neutrons is exactly what a good moderator avoids.
Answer: moderator.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: Temporary hardness of water can be removed by ________.Removal of temporary hardnessBoiling of hard water is helpful in removing the temporary hardness by converting calcium hydrog.... 🟡 SimilarBoiling
Concept Temporary hardness is due to bicarbonates of Ca and Mg, which are unstable to heat. Heat converts them to insoluble carbonates that settle out. Permanent hardness (chlorides, sulphates) survives boiling.
Key relation Ca(HCO3)2 →(Δ) CaCO3↓ + H2O + CO2↑ Bicarbonates decompose on heating; chlorides and sulphates do not.
Solution
1. Temporary hardness comes from Ca(HCO3)2 and Mg(HCO3)2.
2. On boiling, Ca(HCO3)2 → CaCO3↓ + H2O + CO2↑. CO2 leaves, so the equilibrium moves to the carbonate.
3. Likewise Mg(HCO3)2 → Mg(OH)2↓ + 2CO2↑, because Mg(OH)2 is less soluble than MgCO3, so magnesium is removed as the hydroxide.
4. The precipitates are filtered off, leaving soft water.
5. Chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg do not decompose, so boiling cannot remove permanent hardness.
6. The lime-based Clark’s method is also used for temporary hardness, but the simplest answer to the blank is boiling.
7. Hence temporary hardness is removed by boiling.
⚡ JEE Trick Test: boil a sample; if it still gives no lather, hardness is permanent. Common trap: Mg gives Mg(OH)2, not MgCO3, on boiling.
Answer: boiling.
1 (v)Fill in the blank: Temporary hardness is due to presence of ________ of ________ and ________.Cause of temporary hardnessTemporary hardness of water is due to the presence of: (a) Magnesium bicarbonate (b) Calcium chlo... 🟡 SimilarBicarbonates, Calcium, Mg
Concept Temporary hardness is the part that vanishes on boiling, so it must be due to a thermally unstable anion. Only bicarbonate behaves like that, and the cations are Ca and Mg. The key prints “mg”, which means magnesium.
Key relation Temporary hardness = Ca(HCO3)2 + Mg(HCO3)2 These two bicarbonates alone decompose on heating, so they alone constitute the temporary part.
Solution
1. Temporary hardness is hardness removable by simple boiling.
2. Only bicarbonates decompose on heating to give an insoluble carbonate or hydroxide and CO2.
3. The cations that make bicarbonates responsible for hardness are calcium and magnesium; NaHCO3 and KHCO3 do not harden water.
4. So the blanks are: bicarbonates, of calcium and magnesium.
5. They form when CO2-charged rain water attacks limestone: CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 → Ca(HCO3)2.
6. Its reverse on heating explains the scale in kettles and the stalactites of caves.
7. The key writes “mg” for magnesium; reading it as the unit milligram would be a slip.
⚡ JEE Trick Kettle scale is CaCO3 from temporary hardness. If the question says “removed on boiling”, answer bicarbonate at once; if it says “not removed”, answer chloride or sulphate.
Answer: bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium.
2 (i)True or false: Hydronium ion is represented as [H9O4]+Hydrated hydronium ionIn aqueous solution, the hydronium ion gets further hydrated to give which of the following species? 🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept A free proton cannot exist in water; it attaches to a water molecule as H3O+, the hydronium ion. That ion is itself hydrogen-bonded to further water molecules, and a common formula for the hydrated species is H9O4+.
Key relation H+ + H2O → H3O+; H3O+ + 3H2O → H9O4+ H9O4+ is H3O+·3H2O, the central ion with three H-bonded waters.
Solution
1. The proton has no electrons and a radius of only about 10−3 pm, so it has an enormous charge density and cannot remain free in water.
2. It bonds at once to a lone pair of water: H+ + H2O → H3O+, the hydronium ion.
3. The H3O+ has three O–H bonds, each of which can H-bond to another water molecule.
4. Attaching three waters gives H3O+·3H2O = H(3+6)O(1+3) = H9O4+. 3 + 3×2 = 9 H and 1 + 3 = 4 O.
5. So H9O4+ is a correct representation of the hydrated hydronium ion, as NCERT also notes.
6. The simpler H3O+ is the same species before the extra hydration is counted, so both statements are consistent.
7. The statement is therefore True.
⚡ JEE Trick H3O+ is the bare hydronium ion; H9O4+ is H3O+ plus three H-bonded waters. Both appear in options, so read whether the question asks for the simple or hydrated form.
Answer: True.

2 (iii)True or false: Chemical properties of water and heavy water are similar.H2O vs D2OPhysical and chemical properties of water and heavy water@chemistryisfun4477 🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept H2O and D2O have the same electronic structure, so they undergo the same kinds of reactions. What differs is the rate, because D is heavier than H (kinetic isotope effect). So qualitatively their chemical properties are similar.
Key relation same reactions; rate(H2O) > rate(D2O) because the D–O bond is stronger Chemical behaviour is set by electrons, not by neutrons, so isotopes behave alike chemically.
Solution
1. Isotopes have the same number of electrons, so their chemical behaviour is the same in kind.
2. D2O reacts like H2O: 2Na + 2D2O → 2NaOD + D2. Same stoichiometry as with H2O.
3. It hydrolyses salts, gives acids and bases: SO3 + D2O → D2SO4, Al4C3 + 12D2O → 4Al(OD)3 + 3CD4.
4. The difference is only in speed: the heavier D forms a slightly stronger bond, so D2O reacts a little more slowly (kinetic isotope effect).
5. Physical properties differ more: boiling point 374.4 K vs 373 K, density 1.107 vs 1.000 g/mL.
6. A statement that says “similar” is therefore correct, because the reactions are the same and only the rates change.
7. The statement is True.
⚡ JEE Trick Rule of thumb: isotopes are chemically alike but physically different, and the only chemical difference is a rate difference that grows as the relative mass change grows (H to D is the biggest, a factor of 2).
Answer: True (same reactions; D2O is only a little slower).
2 (iv)True or false: Clark’s process is employed for removing the temporary hardness of water.Clark’s processThe method used to remove temporary hardness of water is (A) Calgon's method (B) Clark's method ... 🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Clark’s process adds a calculated amount of slaked lime, Ca(OH)2, which converts the soluble bicarbonates into insoluble carbonates. It works only on the bicarbonate (temporary) part, so the statement is true.
Key relation Ca(HCO3)2 + Ca(OH)2 → 2CaCO3↓ + 2H2O Lime neutralises HCO3− to CO32−, which precipitates with Ca2+.
Solution
1. Clark’s method adds slaked lime Ca(OH)2 to hard water in the exact stoichiometric amount.
2. For calcium: Ca(HCO3)2 + Ca(OH)2 → 2CaCO3↓ + 2H2O.
3. For magnesium: Mg(HCO3)2 + 2Ca(OH)2 → 2CaCO3↓ + Mg(OH)2↓ + 2H2O.
4. Both products are insoluble and are filtered, so the temporary hardness goes.
5. Chlorides and sulphates do not react with lime to a useful extent, so permanent hardness remains.
6. Excess lime must be avoided, because excess Ca(OH)2 would itself make the water hard.
7. So Clark’s process is meant for temporary hardness, and the statement is True.
⚡ JEE Trick Clark = lime = temporary only; Calgon/permutit/ion-exchange/washing soda handle permanent too. Do not add excess lime: the question may test that over-liming re-hardens the water.
Answer: True.
2 (v)True or false: Ion exchange resins can be used for softening hard water.Ion-exchange resinsDiscuss the principle of softening hard water by ion-exchange resins. 🟡 SimilarTrue
Concept Ion-exchange resins are synthetic polymers carrying acidic or basic groups that swap their own ions for those in water. A cation-exchange resin removes Ca2+ and Mg2+, so it can soften water, and a combination of cation and anion resins also gives fully demineralised water.
Key relation 2RNa + Ca2+ → R2Ca + 2Na+; regeneration: R2Ca + 2NaCl → 2RNa + CaCl2 The resin trades Na+ or H+ for Ca2+/Mg2+ and is regenerated with brine or acid.
Solution
1. A cation-exchange resin (R–SO3H or R–SO3Na) holds replaceable cations on a polymer.
2. When hard water passes through the bed: 2RNa + Ca2+ → R2Ca + 2Na+, so Ca2+ and Mg2+ are retained.
3. The outflow contains Na+, which does not form scum with soap, so the water behaves as soft.
4. An anion-exchange resin (R–NH3+OH−) can then exchange Cl−, SO42− for OH−, giving deionised water.
5. Both kinds remove temporary and permanent hardness, since the cation is what matters.
6. When exhausted, the bed is regenerated with brine (NaCl) for the Na-form, so it is reusable.
7. The statement is True.
⚡ JEE Trick Resin and permutit act by the same exchange; both remove both kinds of hardness. If an option says resin removes only temporary hardness, reject it.
Answer: True.

4 (iii)The maximum possible number of hydrogen bonds a water molecule can form is

📝 PYQ: IIT-JEE 1992Solution ↗
H-bonds per water moleculeThe maximum possible number of hydrogen bonds a water molecule can ... 🟢 Exact(b) 4
Concept Each water molecule has two H atoms that can donate and two lone pairs on O that can accept. That gives a maximum of four H-bonds per molecule, two as donor and two as acceptor, producing the tetrahedral network of ice.
Key relation H-bonds per H2O = 2 (donor, via H) + 2 (acceptor, via lone pairs) = 4 The count equals the number of O–H bonds plus the number of lone pairs on oxygen.
Solution
1. Oxygen in water has 2 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs (sp3, bent shape).
2. Each of the two O–H hydrogens can bond to a lone pair on a neighbouring oxygen, giving 2 donor H-bonds.
3. Each of the two lone pairs on O can accept an H from a neighbour, giving 2 acceptor H-bonds.
4. Total = 2 + 2 = 4. This is the maximum, reached in ice.
5. In ice every O is surrounded tetrahedrally by four other O atoms, two at covalent-bond distance 100 pm and two at H-bond distance 175 pm.
6. In liquid water the average is a little less than 4 because the network keeps breaking and reforming.
7. So the maximum possible number is 4, option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Count donors (H atoms) and acceptors (lone pairs) separately, as water has 2 of each, so 4 in total. Do not stop at 2, which counts only the H atoms. This extensive bonding explains the high boiling point, 373 K, of water compared with H2S at 212 K, and also the open cage structure and low density of ice.
Answer: (b) 4.

Zeolite ion exchangeWhen zeolite, which is hydrated sodium aluminium silicate, is treated with hard water, the sodiu... 🟢 Exact(c) Mg²⁺ ions
Concept Zeolite (permutit) is a three-dimensional framework of hydrated sodium aluminium silicate, Na2Al2Si2O8·xH2O, whose Na+ ions are only loosely held. Hardness comes from Ca2+ and Mg2+, so these are the ions that swap places with Na+. The trap is to pick an anion or H+; the framework cannot exchange anions.
Key relation Na2Z + M2+ → MZ + 2Na+ (M = Ca, Mg) Cation exchange: each divalent ion entering the solid displaces two monovalent Na+ ions, so charge is conserved.
Solution
1. Write zeolite as Na2Z, where Z2− is the fixed aluminosilicate framework and Na+ is the mobile cation.
2. Hard water contains Ca2+ and Mg2+ as bicarbonate, chloride and sulphate salts.
3. When it trickles through the bed the framework holds the doubly charged ions more strongly: Na2Z + Ca2+ → CaZ + 2Na+. Higher charge means stronger binding in the lattice.
4. The same happens for magnesium: Na2Z + Mg2+ → MgZ + 2Na+. So the sodium ions leave in exchange for Mg2+ (and Ca2+).
5. Check the others: (a) H+ would acidify the water and is not what is used (that is a resin); (b) SO42− and (d) OH− are anions, and a negative framework cannot trade anions.
6. So only option (c) is a hardness ion that is taken up by the framework.
7. Net effect: hardness ions are replaced by an equivalent amount of Na+, which gives no scum with soap.
⚡ JEE Trick Zeolite is a cation exchanger and Na+ is its only mobile ion, so the answer is always one of the hardness cations. Anion options are instant eliminations.
Answer: (c) Mg2+ ions.

Concept Hardness of water is caused by dissolved salts of calcium and magnesium: bicarbonates (temporary hardness), and chlorides and sulphates (permanent hardness). These ions form insoluble scum with soap. Sodium and potassium soaps are soluble, so Na and K salts never cause hardness.
Key relation Hardness = Ca2+, Mg2+ salts; 2RCOONa + M2+ → (RCOO)2M↓ + 2Na+ Soap fails to lather because the Ca/Mg soap is insoluble.
Solution
1. Define hardness: water that does not give an easy lather with soap because of dissolved salts.
2. Soap is sodium stearate, C17H35COONa. It is soluble, and the lather forms from it.
3. Ca2+ or Mg2+ in water precipitates the stearate: 2C17H35COONa + CaCl2 → (C17H35COO)2Ca↓ + 2NaCl.
4. (d) Sodium and potassium salts give soluble soaps, so they do not cause hardness. Option (d) is out.
5. (b) and (c) contain Ba or Sr, which also precipitate soap in principle, but barium and strontium salts are rare in natural water; the accepted cause is Ca and Mg only.
6. Natural sources: Ca(HCO3)2, Mg(HCO3)2, CaCl2, MgCl2, CaSO4, MgSO4.
7. So the answer is (a) calcium and magnesium salts.
⚡ JEE Trick “Hardness = Ca and Mg” is the one-line rule. Options with Na/K are wrong at once; options pairing Ca or Mg with Ba or Sr are the distractors.
Answer: (a) calcium and magnesium salts.

Concept A water softener removes or sequesters Ca2+ and Mg2+. Calgon, permutit and washing soda all do this. Sodium sulphate adds no ion that can precipitate or bind Ca2+ / Mg2+ in a way that lowers hardness; it is not a softener.

2Explain the terms (i) electron deficient (ii) electron precise (iii) electron rich compounds of hydrogen with suitable example.Molecular hydride typesWhat do you understand by (i) electron-deficient, (ii) electron-precise and (iii) electron-rich ... 🟢 Exactdeficient B₂H₆; precise CH₄; rich NH₃/H₂O
Concept Molecular hydrides of p-block elements are classified by comparing the number of valence electrons available with those needed for normal two-centre bonds. Boron group is short, carbon group is exact, and groups 15–17 have extra electrons left as lone pairs.
Key relation electron deficient: e− short · electron precise: e− exact · electron rich: lone pairs left Counting electrons in the central atom’s bonding shows which class applies.
Solution
1. (i) Electron deficient: the hydride has too few electrons to write a normal two-centre bond for each atom. Example: BH3, which exists as the dimer B2H6 with 3-centre 2-electron bonds; also AlH3 (group 13).
2. B2H6 has 12 valence electrons for 8 atoms; four terminal B–H bonds use 8 electrons and only 4 are left for the 2 bridges, hence 3c–2e.
3. (ii) Electron precise: the number of electrons is exactly enough for normal bonds, with no lone pairs on the central atom. Example: CH4 (also SiH4, GeH4) of group 14, with 4 bonds and tetrahedral shape.
4. (iii) Electron rich: there are more electrons than bonds need, so the central atom carries lone pairs. Examples: NH3 (1 lone pair), H2O (2 lone pairs), HF (3 lone pairs).
5. These lone pairs make the compounds good H-bond donors/acceptors, which explains the high boiling points of NH3, H2O and HF.
6. Electron deficient hydrides act as Lewis acids; electron rich ones act as Lewis bases.
7. Quick table: group 13 → deficient, group 14 → precise, groups 15, 16, 17 → rich.
⚡ JEE Trick Count the central atom’s valence electrons against the bonds: short = deficient, exact = precise, surplus = rich. B2H6, CH4, NH3 is the standard example triple.
Answer: deficient → B2H6; precise → CH4; rich → NH3 / H2O.

5. Two lone pairs push the bond pairs together: 109.5° → 104.5°. Option (b).

2Water shows maximum density at which temperature?
(a) 0°C   (b) 100°C   (c) 4°C   (d) −4°C		Maximum density of waterDensity of water is maximum at \( 4^{\circ} \mathrm{C} \). Explain. (W) 🟡 Similar(c) 4°C
Concept Water has maximum density at 4 °C (more precisely 3.98 °C), where d = 1.000 g/mL. Above this temperature thermal expansion lowers the density. Below it, ice-like open hydrogen-bonded clusters begin to form, which also lowers it.
Key relation dmax at 4 °C = 1.000 g/mL; d(ice, 0 °C) ≈ 0.92 g/mL Two competing effects, thermal expansion and the opening up of H-bonded structure, balance at 4 °C.
Solution
1. On cooling from 100 °C, ordinary thermal contraction raises the density.
2. Near 4 °C, molecules start arranging into tetrahedral H-bonded clusters, which have large open cavities.
3. Below 4 °C this opening-up wins over contraction, so the density falls again on cooling to 0 °C.
4. So the density is highest at 4 °C, option (c).
5. (a) 0 °C: water here is at the freezing point and ice (d = 0.92) floats, so it is not the maximum.
6. (b) 100 °C: density is about 0.958 g/mL, far less. (d) −4 °C is supercooled water or ice, lower still.
7. Consequence: lakes freeze from the top, with the 4 °C water sinking to the bottom, so aquatic life survives.
⚡ JEE Trick Anomaly of water: it is densest at 4 °C, and ice is lighter than water. Any question with the word “maximum density” and water is 4 °C.

Key relation Ice: tetrahedral O, 2 covalent O–H + 2 H-bonded H; open cage → lower density (0.92 g cm−3) than water at 4 °C (1.00 g cm−3) Each statement must be tested against the known ice structure.
Solution
1. (a) Ice has an open, cage-like hexagonal structure with large empty channels. True, so not asked.
2. (b) Because of the open cage, ice is less dense than water; that is why ice floats. “More density than water” is false.
3. (c) Each O has four H atoms around it, tetrahedrally arranged: two are its own covalently bonded H and two are H atoms of neighbouring molecules joined by H-bonds. True.
4. (d) The statement says each O atom has four H-bonds around it. Only two of the four surrounding H atoms are linked to this oxygen by H-bonds (the other two are its own covalent H), so the book treats (d) as false.
5. To avoid confusion: each molecule takes part in four H-bonds overall, two as donor with its own H and two as acceptor on the O, but the question counts the bonds at the O atom as in the printed key, which gives (d) false.
6. (a) and (c) are therefore the true statements; (b) is wrong on density and (d) is wrong on the count of bonds to O.
7. The false statements are (b) and (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Ice floats, so any claim that it is denser than water is false. For the O environment remember 2 + 2: two covalent, two hydrogen-bonded; four H atoms but only two H-bonds directed at that oxygen.
Answer: (b) and (d).

Concept Na2CO3 reacts 1 : 1 with Ca2+ or Mg2+ salts. So the moles of Na2CO3 needed equal the moles of hardness, and ppm is mg of hardness per litre. The trap is mixing up the molar masses of CaCO3 (100) and MgCO3 (84) and the volume (10 L).
Key relation mol Na2CO3 = mol hardness (1 : 1); mass of hardness = ppm × volume (L) / 1000 g CaSO4 + Na2CO3 → CaCO3 + Na2SO4 and CaCl2 + Na2CO3 → CaCO3 + 2NaCl both show 1 : 1.
Solution
Given 10 L of hard water, 100 ppm as CaCO₃, or 420 ppm as MgCO₃
Find mass of Na₂CO₃ needed in each case (check options a–d)
1. Case 1: 100 ppm CaCO3 means 100 mg per litre, so in 10 L the mass is 100 × 10 = 1000 mg = 1 g.
2. Moles of hardness = 1 g / 100 g mol−1 = 0.01 mol, so n(Na2CO3) = 0.01 mol.
3. Mass of Na2CO3 = 0.01 × 106 = 1.06 g. So (a) is correct and (b) (10.6 g) is ten times too much.
4. Case 2: 420 ppm MgCO3 in 10 L = 420 × 10 = 4200 mg = 4.2 g.
5. Moles of MgCO3 = 4.2 / 84 = 0.05 mol, giving n(Na2CO3) = 0.05 mol.
6. Mass of Na2CO3 = 0.05 × 106 = 5.3 g. So (d) is correct and (c) (53.0 g) is ten times too much.
7. The correct statements are (a) and (d); both wrong options are the right values multiplied by 10, the usual unit trap in this question.
⚡ JEE Trick The options (b) and (c) are exactly 10 times (a) and (d): they come from forgetting that ppm is mg/L, so 10 L of 100 ppm water holds 1 g, not 10 g. Quick check: 0.01 mol × 106 = 1.06 g and 0.05 mol × 106 = 5.3 g.
Answer: (a) and (d).
6[One or more than one option may be correct] The hardness of water due to HCO3− is 122 ppm. Select the correct statement(s).
(a) The hardness of water in terms of CaCO3 is 200 ppm.   (b) The hardness of water in terms of CaCO3 is 100 ppm.   (c) The hardness of water in terms of CaCl2 is 222 ppm.   (d) The hardness of water in terms of MgCl2 is 95 ppm.		Hardness from HCO3- ppmThe hardness of water due to `HCO_3` is `122pm`. Select the correct statement(s). 🟢 Exact(b), (d) bd
Concept Bicarbonate hardness in ppm gives moles of HCO3− per litre, and each divalent metal ion needs two bicarbonates. Convert the moles of M(HCO3)2 into the CaCO3, CaCl2 or MgCl2 equivalent by using the molar mass of the reference salt.
Key relation n[M(HCO3)2] = n(HCO3−)/2; hardness as X = n × MX (mg per litre) Hardness is expressed per mole of M2+, and one M2+ corresponds to one mole of CaCO3, CaCl2 or MgCl2.
Solution
Given hardness due to HCO₃⁻ = 122 ppm
Find hardness expressed as CaCO₃, CaCl₂ and MgCl₂ (check options a–d)
1. Take 1 L water: 122 ppm HCO3− = 122 mg. Molar mass of HCO3− = 61 g mol−1.
2. n(HCO3−) = 122 mg / 61 = 2 mmol per litre.
3. Each M(HCO3)2 has two HCO3−, so n(M2+) = 2/2 = 1 mmol per litre.
4. As CaCO3: 1 mmol × 100 mg mmol−1 = 100 ppm. So (b) is correct and (a) 200 is wrong.
5. As CaCl2: 1 mmol × 111 = 111 ppm, not 222. So (c) is wrong.
6. As MgCl2: 1 mmol × 95 = 95 ppm. So (d) is correct.
7. The 200 and 222 values come from taking 2 mmol of metal ion, i.e. forgetting that two bicarbonates share one cation. The correct set is (b) and (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Count cations, not anions: 2 HCO3− = 1 M2+. So ppm as salt X = (ppm HCO3−/122) × MX = 1 × MX. Anything that comes out as 2 × MX is the trap.
Answer: (b) and (d).

Concept Soap is sodium or potassium stearate (RCOONa). In hard water Ca2+ and Mg2+ convert it into insoluble scum, so no lather forms until these ions have been used up. The trap is option (d): hard water is not acidic.
Key relation 2C17H35COONa + Ca2+ → (C17H35COO)2Ca↓ + 2Na+ Soap lathers only when it is free in solution, which happens after M2+ has been precipitated.
Solution
1. (a) Ca2+ and Mg2+ react with soap to give insoluble calcium and magnesium stearates, the greasy scum. Correct.
2. (b) Lather needs free soap; the soap first precipitates all Ca2+/Mg2+, and only the excess forms lather. Correct.
3. (c) The dissolved Ca/Mg salts consume soap without cleaning, so soap is wasted; hardness is judged by the soap-consuming power. Correct.
4. (d) Hard water is not due to acidic impurities; it contains Ca/Mg bicarbonates, chlorides and sulphates, and may be neutral or slightly alkaline. Incorrect.
5. Even if acid were present it would convert soap to fatty acid, but that is not the standard reason; the standard reason is precipitation by M2+.
6. Washing with synthetic detergents works because their Ca/Mg salts are soluble, which shows the cations are the cause.

⚡ JEE Trick Explanations must involve Ca2+/Mg2+: scum, no lather until they are gone, soap wasted. An option invoking acidity or some other impurity is the distractor.

9[One or more than one option may be correct] Some of the properties of water are described below. Which of them is/are not correct?
(a) Water is known as a universal solvent   (b) There is no hydrogen bonding in frozen water   (c) Frozen water is heavier than liquid water   (d) Hydrogen bonding is present in liquid water		Properties of water & iceSome of the properties of water are described below. Which of them is/are not correct? (i) Water ... 🟢 Exact(b), (c) bc
Concept We pick the INCORRECT statements. Water is a good solvent, ice and liquid water both contain H-bonds, but ice has an open structure and is less dense. The trap is thinking that freezing destroys H-bonds; it actually completes the network.
Key relation Ice: full tetrahedral H-bond network, open cage, density 0.92 g cm−3 < water A statement is “not correct” if it contradicts the structure of ice or liquid water.
Solution
1. (a) Water dissolves many ionic and polar substances because of its high dielectric constant and H-bonding, so it is called the universal solvent. Correct statement, so not asked.
2. (b) “No hydrogen bonding in frozen water” is wrong: ice has the most extensive H-bonding, each molecule linked to four neighbours. Not correct.
3. (c) “Frozen water is heavier than liquid water” is wrong: the open cage of ice makes it less dense (0.92 g cm−3), so it floats. Not correct.
4. (d) Hydrogen bonding is present in liquid water as well, in a continually breaking and reforming network. Correct statement, so not asked.
5. Because H-bonds in ice hold the molecules in an open arrangement, volume increases on freezing; this explains why ice floats and why pipes burst.
6. Liquid water has maximum density at 4 °C, because of partial collapse of the cage on melting.
7. So the incorrect properties are (b) and (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Reading the stem: it asks which are NOT correct. Ice floats (so lighter) and is fully H-bonded; a statement that denies either is the false one. Mark the words “not correct” before choosing.
Answer: (b) and (c).
10[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following correctly describe the permutit (zeolite) process of water softening?
(a) Sodium ions of permutit are exchanged with calcium and magnesium ions   (b) The softened water contains sodium salts   (c) Permutit is regenerated using a dilute sodium chloride solution   (d) This process removes only temporary hardness		Permutit processWhat is Zeolite Process for Water Softening | Permutit Process | Water Softening Processes 🟡 Similar(a), (b), (c) abc
Concept Permutit (hydrated sodium aluminium silicate, Na2Z) softens water by exchanging its Na+ for Ca2+ and Mg2+. It removes both temporary and permanent hardness, and is regenerated by washing with brine. The trap is (d), which says it removes only temporary hardness.
Key relation Na2Z + M2+ → MZ + 2Na+; MZ + 2NaCl → Na2Z + MCl2 The exchange acts on the cations, so it does not matter which anion (HCO3−, Cl−, SO42−) accompanies them.
Solution
1. (a) Na+ ions of the zeolite are exchanged with Ca2+ and Mg2+ from water: Na2Z + Ca2+ → CaZ + 2Na+. Correct.
2. (b) The effluent contains the Na+ released, as NaHCO3, NaCl or Na2SO4, so softened water holds sodium salts. Correct.
3. (c) When all Na+ are used up, the bed is CaZ/MgZ; it is regenerated by a concentrated (10%) NaCl solution, which reverses the exchange. The book calls this brine/NaCl regeneration. Correct.
4. (d) Since the exchange removes the cations, both temporary and permanent hardness are removed. Incorrect.
5. The water is not deionised: it still has dissolved anions and Na+, which is why it is called softened rather than pure.
6. Washing soda and lime add precipitation sludge, but permutit gives no sludge, an advantage of the method.
7. So the correct statements are (a), (b) and (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Ion exchange works on the cation, so the associated anion is irrelevant and both types of hardness go. Remember brine for regeneration: Na+ in large excess pushes the equilibrium back to Na2Z.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).
