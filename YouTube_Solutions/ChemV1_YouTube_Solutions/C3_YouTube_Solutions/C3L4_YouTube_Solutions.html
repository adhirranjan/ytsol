1Which statement correctly describes an empirical formula? (simplest whole-number ratio of atoms)Empirical formula meaningFrom molecular formula to empirical formula🟡 SimilarbIt gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
Concept The empirical formula (EF) tells you the ratio in which atoms combine, reduced to the smallest whole numbers — it does not claim to give the actual atom count in one molecule. That job belongs to the molecular formula (MF). Because analysis (combustion, % composition) yields ratios of moles, the raw output of every such experiment is always the empirical formula; the molecular formula follows only once the molar mass is known.
Key relation MF = n × EF, where n = 1, 2, 3… EF is the ratio stripped to lowest terms; MF re-multiplies it by whatever integer n makes the masses agree, so EF is by definition the simplest whole-number ratio.
Solution
1. Define each option's claim and test it against the meaning of "empirical.""Empirical" here means "the reduced ratio found by experiment," not the true molecular size.
2. "Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms" is exactly this definition — option (b).
3. Reject the rest: the actual number of atoms is the molecular formula; the arrangement of bonds is the structural formula; neither is the empirical formula.
4. Confirm with examples where MF ≠ EF:
 compound        molecular formula   empirical formula (÷GCD)
 hydrogen perox.  H₂O₂               HO      (÷2)
 glucose          C₆H₁₂O₆            CH₂O    (÷6)
 benzene          C₆H₆               CH      (÷6)
 acetic acid      C₂H₄O₂             CH₂O    (÷2)
⚡ JEE Trick Empirical formula = molecular formula divided by the GCD of the subscripts. Glucose, formaldehyde and acetic acid all share EF = CH₂O yet are totally different molecules — proof that EF fixes only the ratio, never the identity. Trap: an option saying "actual number of atoms in a molecule" describes the molecular formula, not the empirical one.
Answer: (b) It gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
2A compound contains 40% carbon (12) and 6.67% hydrogen (1). Mole ratio of carbon?Mole ratio from %An organic compound contains 40% carbon and 6.67% hydrogen by mass — mole ratio / empirical formula🟢 Exacta3.33
Concept To turn a mass percentage into a mole ratio you exploit a simple trick: imagine you have exactly 100 g of the compound, so every percentage becomes that many grams directly. Then each element's relative number of moles is its grams divided by its atomic mass. This "relative moles" figure is the raw material from which the empirical formula is later built.
Formula relative moles = (mass % taken as grams) ÷ (atomic mass). In a 100 g basis the % is the mass in grams, and dividing grams by g mol⁻¹ leaves moles. Atomic masses given: C = 12, H = 1.
Solution
1. Take a 100 g basis, so 40% C becomes 40 g of carbon.Choosing 100 g makes each percentage equal the grams present, with no extra conversion.
2. Apply the mole-ratio formula for carbon: moles C = mass ÷ atomic mass = 40 ÷ 12.
3. Divide: 40 ÷ 12 = 3.33 (i.e. 3.333…).12 × 3 = 36, remainder 4; 4 ÷ 12 = 0.33, so 3.33.
4. (For completeness, hydrogen would give 6.67 ÷ 1 = 6.67, but the question asks only for carbon.)
 element   mass %   as grams   atomic mass   relative moles = g ÷ M
 C         40 %     40 g       12            40 ÷ 12 = 3.33   ← asked
 H         6.67 %   6.67 g     1             6.67 ÷ 1 = 6.67
⚡ JEE Trick "Mole ratio of element X" always means simply %X ÷ (atomic mass of X) on a 100 g basis — no need to compute the whole formula if only one element is asked. Trap: don't divide by the molecular mass or multiply by anything; the single division by the atomic mass is the entire step.
Answer: (a) 3.33.

Concept The empirical formula is found by converting each element's mass percentage into relative moles, then reducing those moles to the smallest whole-number ratio. The standard trick is a 100 g basis: every percentage becomes grams directly. "Rest oxygen" means oxygen makes up whatever is left after C and H — you must compute it yourself before you can divide.

3. Divide each by the smallest (2.27): C = 4.54 ÷ 2.27 = 2; H = 9.1 ÷ 2.27 = 4; O = 2.27 ÷ 2.27 = 1.
4. Ratio C : H : O = 2 : 4 : 1 is already whole, so EF = C₂H₄O — option (b).
5. Check by recomputing %C from C₂H₄O (mass 44): 24 ÷ 44 × 100 ≈ 54.5% ✓.The back-check confirms no arithmetic slip.

4Empirical formula mass 29, molecular mass 58. Molecular formula?MF = n × EFMolecular formula from EF & molar mass🟡 Similarb2 × empirical formula (C₄H₁₀)
Concept The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula, and that multiple n is fixed entirely by mass: how many empirical-formula "units" fit inside one real molecule. So you never need the elements themselves — just divide the molecular mass by the empirical-formula mass to get n, then multiply the empirical formula by n.
Formula n = (molecular mass) ÷ (empirical formula mass), then MF = n × EF. Both masses are in the same units (u), so their ratio is a pure integer counting how many EF units make one molecule.
Solution
1. Read the data: empirical formula mass = 29, molecular mass = 58.
2. Find the multiple: n = 58 ÷ 29 = 2.29 × 2 = 58 exactly, so two empirical units make one molecule.
3. Therefore MF = 2 × EF — option (b).
4. Identify the compound: an EF of mass 29 is C₂H₅ (2×12 + 5×1 = 24 + 5 = 29); doubling gives C₄H₁₀ (butane), mass 48 + 10 = 58 ✓.The recomputed molecular mass 58 matches the given value, confirming n = 2 and MF = C₄H₁₀.

⚡ JEE Trick The multiplier is just molar mass ÷ EF mass — a single division that must come out a whole number; if it doesn't, you mis-read a mass. Trap: the question asks for the relationship (2 × EF), so you don't even have to name C₄H₁₀ — recognising n = 2 is enough to pick (b).

Concept The mass percentage of an element in a compound is the fraction of the compound's molar mass contributed by that element, expressed out of 100. You compute it by working out the molar mass of one formula unit, then asking what share of it comes from each kind of atom. For urea, CO(NH₂)₂, count the atoms carefully: 1 C, 1 O, 2 N and 4 H.
Formula % of element = (total mass of that element in the formula ÷ molar mass) × 100. Each element's contribution is (number of its atoms × its atomic mass); dividing by the whole molar mass and ×100 turns it into a percentage. Atomic masses: C = 12, O = 16, N = 14, H = 1.

2. Molar mass = 12 + 16 + (2×14) + (4×1) = 12 + 16 + 28 + 4 = 60 g mol⁻¹.
3. % C = (12 ÷ 60) × 100 = 20.0%.
4. % O = (16 ÷ 60) × 100 = 26.67%.
5. % N = (28 ÷ 60) × 100 = 46.67%.
6. % H = (4 ÷ 60) × 100 = 6.67%.
7. Check: 20.0 + 26.67 + 46.67 + 6.67 = 100.0% ✓.The four percentages must total 100, which confirms no atom was miscounted.

2How many particles are present in 1 mole of a substance?Avogadro numberNumber of particles using Avogadro's constant🟢 Exact—
Concept The mole is defined as a fixed count of elementary entities — atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons — chosen so that a mole of any substance is a convenient, weighable amount. That fixed count is Avogadro's number, NA. So the phrase "one mole of a substance" is simply shorthand for "NA particles of it," whatever the particle happens to be.
Key relation 1 mole = NA particles = 6.022 × 10²³ particles, with NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹. More generally N = n × NA; setting n = 1 mol gives exactly NA particles.
Solution
1. Recall the definition of the mole: it contains as many particles as NA.The mole was defined precisely so its particle count is this universal constant.
2. State the value: NA = 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole.
3. Therefore 1 mole of any substance contains 6.022×10²³ of its characteristic particles — 1 mol H₂O = 6.022×10²³ water molecules, 1 mol Na = 6.022×10²³ Na atoms, 1 mol e⁻ = 6.022×10²³ electrons.The number is independent of the substance; only the type of particle changes.
 sample        particle type   particles in 1 mole
 1 mol He       He atoms        6.022×10²³
 1 mol H₂O      H₂O molecules   6.022×10²³
 1 mol NaCl     formula units   6.022×10²³
 1 mol e⁻       electrons       6.022×10²³
⚡ JEE Trick "One mole" ⇒ instantly write 6.022×10²³ — it is a count, not a mass, and is the same for every substance. Trap: the particles differ (atoms vs molecules vs ions), so 1 mol of O₂ is 6.022×10²³ molecules but 2 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.204×10²⁴ oxygen atoms — always check what particle is being counted.
Key point: one mole of any substance contains Avogadro's number of particles, NA = 6.022 × 10²³.
3Mass of an H atom is 1.008 u. What is the mass of 18 atoms of hydrogen?Mass of atomsMass of an atom of hydrogen is 1.008 u🟢 Exact—
Concept The unified mass unit u is a single-particle mass scale: the mass of one atom is quoted in u, and the mass of a handful of atoms is simply that number times the count. No moles, no Avogadro's number — those enter only when grams are involved. Here we stay entirely on the atomic scale: 18 identical hydrogen atoms, each of mass 1.008 u — so the whole calculation is one honest multiplication.
Formula mass of N atoms = N × (mass of one atom). Mass is additive: identical particles simply multiply the single-particle mass by the count. Given: mass of one H atom = 1.008 u, N = 18.
Solution
1. Identify the scale: the data is in u (per-atom), and 18 is a plain count of atoms — not grams, not moles.This is the conceptual gate of the question; everything after it is one multiplication.
2. Multiply: mass = 18 × 1.008 = 18.144 u.
3. Arithmetic check: 18 × 1 = 18 and 18 × 0.008 = 0.144, so 18 + 0.144 = 18.144 ✓.
4. Contrast (the trap the question is built around): 18 grams of H atoms would instead be 18 ÷ 1.008 ≈ 17.86 mol ≈ 1.075 × 10²⁵ atoms — a completely different situation.u-scale questions never need N_A; gram-scale questions always do.
 quantity                     value
 mass of 1 H atom             1.008 u
 number of atoms              18
 mass = 18 × 1.008            18.144 u   <-- answer
 (compare: 18 g of H = 17.86 mol, not 18 atoms)
⚡ JEE Trick When the mass is quoted in u, atoms are counted by simple multiplication/division — Avogadro's number never appears. Trap: reflexively converting "18" into moles (because 18 reminds you of water's molar mass) turns a one-line question into a wrong answer; here 18 is literally a count of eighteen atoms.
Answer: mass of 18 hydrogen atoms = 18 × 1.008 = 18.144 u.
4Number of atoms in (a) 254 u of iodine, (b) 254 g of iodine (I = 127 u)Atoms from u and gramsCalculate the number of atoms: (a) 254 u of iodine (b) 254 g of iodine (atomic mass I = 127 u)🟢 Exact—
Concept This question deliberately contrasts two identical-looking numbers with different units — 254 u versus 254 g — to test whether you know that u measures a single-particle mass while g measures a bulk mass. In u, you count atoms directly by dividing by the atomic mass; in g, you must first go to moles and then multiply by NA. The two answers differ by a factor of Avogadro's number.
Formula On the u-scale: number of atoms = (mass in u) ÷ (atomic mass in u). On the gram-scale: number of atoms = (mass in g ÷ atomic mass) × NA. u already lives at the atomic scale, so no NA is needed; grams are macroscopic, so NA bridges moles to atoms. Given I = 127 u, NA = 6.022×10²³.
Solution
(a) 254 u of iodine.
1. Divide by the atomic mass in u: atoms = 254 ÷ 127 = 2 atoms.Each iodine atom weighs 127 u, so 254 u is exactly two atoms — no Avogadro's number involved.
(b) 254 g of iodine.
2. Moles first: n = mass ÷ atomic mass = 254 ÷ 127 = 2 mol.
3. Atoms = n × NA = 2 × 6.022×10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms.Grams are a bulk mass, so each mole must be expanded into NA atoms.
 given       what it is        route                       atoms

 254 g       bulk mass         (254 ÷ 127) × 6.022×10²³    1.204×10²⁴
⚡ JEE Trick u ⇒ never multiply by NA; g ⇒ always multiply by NA. The same number 254 gives 2 atoms in u but 1.2×10²⁴ atoms in g — a factor of NA apart. Trap: reading "254 u" as if it were grams (or vice-versa) is the single mistake this question is built to catch.

Concept Finding an empirical formula from percentage composition is a fixed four-step recipe, and this question asks which step comes first. Before you can do any arithmetic you must set up a tidy table listing the elements present — i.e. write down each element's symbol and put its percentage beside it. Every later step (÷ atomic mass, ÷ smallest, ×integer) operates on that table.
Key relation The standard order is symbols → moles (% ÷ atomic mass) → divide by smallest → ×integer if fractional. Listing the symbols/percentages organises the data so the mole calculation can be done element-by-element without confusion.
Solution
1. Lay out the problem: identify which elements are present and write their symbols with the given % beneath each — option (b).This is the organising step; nothing can be divided until the data is tabulated.
2. Only after the symbols are written do you convert each % to moles (÷ atomic mass).
3. Then divide all mole values by the smallest, and finally multiply up if any ratio is fractional.
4. So the distractors (divide by smallest, ×integer, compute moles) are all later steps, not the first.

5If mole ratios are fractional, we: (multiply by same number)Clearing fractionsCalculating EF from percent composition🟡 SimilarbMultiply by same number
Concept Sometimes dividing by the smallest mole value leaves ratios that are not whole numbers — such as 1.5, 1.33 or 2.5. Since a formula can only contain whole atoms, you must clear these fractions by multiplying every ratio by the same small integer. Multiplying all terms by the same factor keeps the ratio intact while turning the decimals into integers.
Key relation whole-number ratio = (fractional ratio) × k, where k is the smallest integer that clears all fractions. A ratio is unchanged when every term is multiplied by the same k; choosing k = 2 clears .5, k = 3 clears .33/.67, k = 4 clears .25/.75.
Solution
1. Inspect the ratios after dividing by the smallest; if any is fractional, note the fraction.
2. Choose the smallest integer k that makes them all whole, and multiply the whole set by k — option (b).Multiplying only one term would change the ratio; every term must be scaled by the same k.
3. Example: ratio C 1, O 2.5 → multiply all by 2 → C 2, O 5 → C₂O₅-type formula.
4. Example: N 1, O 2.5 (nitrogen oxide) → ×2 → N₂O₅.

1Molecular weight if vapour density is 29.Mol. wt = 2 × VDMolecular weight = 2 × vapour density🟢 Exacta58
Concept Vapour density (VD) compares the density of a gas with that of hydrogen gas at the same temperature and pressure. Since equal volumes hold equal numbers of molecules (Avogadro's law), the density ratio equals the ratio of molecular masses, and H₂ has molecular mass 2 — so molecular weight is always exactly twice the vapour density. VD is therefore a dimensionless number (a pure ratio), while molecular weight carries the unit u; keeping the two distinct is the entire content of this question.
Formula VD = M(gas) ÷ M(H₂) = M ÷ 2 ⇒ M = 2 × VD. Avogadro's law converts the density ratio into a molecular-mass ratio, and the reference H₂ contributes the fixed 2 in the denominator. Given VD = 29.
Solution
1. Write the defining relation: M = 2 × VD.The 2 comes from H₂ (2 × 1), the reference gas — not from any property of the unknown.
2. Substitute: M = 2 × 29 = 58 — option (a).
3. Sanity check with a real gas: butane C₄H₁₀ has M = 48 + 10 = 58, so its VD would indeed be 58 ÷ 2 = 29 ✓.
