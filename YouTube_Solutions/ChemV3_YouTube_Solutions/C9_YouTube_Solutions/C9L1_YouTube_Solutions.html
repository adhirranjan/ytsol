1 (i)
Fill in the blank: The lightest element known is ________.
C9L1VRecapQ1iLightest element
🟡 Similar001 - Hydrogen - The…
Hydrogen
Concept The lightest element is the one with the smallest atomic mass. Hydrogen has atomic number 1 and a nucleus of a single proton (in protium), so its atomic mass is about 1.008 u, smaller than that of every other element. The trap is to confuse lightest element with lightest gas; here they coincide.
Key relation atomic mass of H = 1.008 u < He (4.003 u) < Li (6.94 u) … Atomic mass rises with the number of nucleons, and H has the fewest possible.
Solution
1. Atomic mass is roughly the number of protons plus neutrons in the nucleus, weighted over the natural isotopes.
2. Protium, the isotope making up 99.985% of natural hydrogen, has 1 proton and 0 neutrons, so its mass number is 1.
3. The next element, helium, has 2 protons and 2 neutrons (mass number 4), four times as heavy.
4. No element has fewer than one proton, so no element can be lighter than hydrogen: the minimum atomic mass is 1.008 u.
5. The same smallness makes H2 (molar mass 2.016 g/mol) the lightest gas: 1 L at NTP weighs only 0.0899 g, about (1/14) of air.
6. Therefore the blank is filled by hydrogen.
7. Exam use: this fact often appears in the form “which is the lightest element / lightest gas / least dense gas”, and every version has the same answer: hydrogen (H2 for the gas). The blank asks only for the element, so the word to write is hydrogen, not dihydrogen.
⚡ JEE Trick Lightest element = first element of the periodic table = hydrogen. Do not write helium: helium is only the lightest noble gas and the second-lightest element.
Answer: Hydrogen.
1 (ii)
Fill in the blank: The isotope of hydrogen does not contain neutron is ________.
C9L1VRecapQ1iiIsotopes of hydrogen
🟢 ExactWhich isotope of hyd…
protium (1H1)
Concept Hydrogen has three isotopes with the same atomic number (1) but different mass numbers, because they differ only in neutron count. The one with no neutron is the lightest and by far the commonest. The trap is to name deuterium, which has one neutron, or to forget that mass number = protons + neutrons.
Key relation mass number A = Z + N, so N = A − Z; for hydrogen Z = 1, hence N = A − 1 Z is fixed at 1 for every hydrogen isotope, so only A changes the neutron count.
Solution
1. Every hydrogen isotope has one proton, Z = 1, so the neutron number is N = A − 1.
2. For A = 1: N = 1 − 1 = 0. This is protium, written 11H, abundance about 99.985%.
3. For A = 2: N = 2 − 1 = 1. This is deuterium, 21H or D, abundance about 0.015%.
4. For A = 3: N = 3 − 1 = 2. This is tritium, 31H or T, radioactive and extremely rare.
5. Only protium has N = 0, which also makes it the only nucleus (other than a free proton) that is just a single nucleon.
6. So the isotope with no neutron is protium (11H).
7. Cross-check with the notation: in AZH the lower number is always 1, so the upper number minus one gives the neutrons directly for all three isotopes. The relative abundance order is protium >> deuterium >> tritium.
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the neutron count as 0, 1, 2 for protium, deuterium, tritium. The names go up with the mass number; protium is the only one without a special symbol.
Answer: Protium (11H).
1 (iii)
Fill in the blank: ________ is radioactive isotope of hydrogen.
C9L1VRecapQ1iiiTritium radioactivity
🟢 ExactThe isotope of hydro…
Tritium
Concept Of the three isotopes of hydrogen, protium and deuterium are stable while tritium is radioactive. Its nucleus (1 proton, 2 neutrons) has too high a neutron-to-proton ratio and decays by beta emission. The trap is to say deuterium, which is only heavy, not radioactive.
Key relation 31H → 32He + 0−1e (β− decay), t1/2 ≈ 12.3 years A neutron converts into a proton and an electron, which raises Z by one.
Solution
1. Nuclear stability depends on the neutron-to-proton ratio; for the lightest nuclei it should be close to 1 or lower.
2. Protium has n/p = 0 and deuterium n/p = 1, and both are stable (deuterium is present naturally at about 0.015%).
3. Tritium has n/p = 2/1 = 2, too high to be stable, so it is radioactive.
4. It decays by β− emission: one neutron turns into a proton, so mass number stays 3 and Z becomes 2, giving 3He.
5. The half-life is about 12.3 years, and tritium emits only soft β particles (no γ).
6. So the radioactive isotope of hydrogen is tritium.
7. Because tritium is so unstable it occurs naturally only in traces, about 1 atom in 1018 of ordinary hydrogen, and it is made artificially by bombarding lithium with neutrons. The reaction is 6Li + 1n → 4He + 3H.
⚡ JEE Trick Only one radioactive isotope of hydrogen exists in the syllabus and it is the heaviest. Deuterium D2O is heavy water, not radioactive water.
Answer: Tritium.
1 (iv)
Fill in the blank: When steam is passed over red hot coke ________ is formed.
C9L1VRecapQ1ivWater gas formation
🟢 ExactWhat is formed when…
water gas (CO + H2)
Concept Steam passed over white-hot coke (or coal) at about 1000 °C is reduced to hydrogen while carbon is oxidised to carbon monoxide. The mixture of CO and H2 is called water gas, also known as synthesis gas or syngas. The trap is to answer producer gas, which is CO + N2 made from air, not steam.
Key relation C + H2O → CO + H2 (1270 K, ΔH = +131 kJ/mol) Carbon takes the oxygen of steam, and hydrogen is released.
Solution
1. Heat coke to red-hot (white-hot, about 1270 K) and pass steam over it.
2. Carbon is oxidised from 0 to +2 and hydrogen is reduced from +1 to 0: C + H2O → CO + H2.
3. The product is an equimolar mixture (1 : 1 by volume) of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, which is water gas.
4. The reaction is endothermic (ΔH = +131 kJ/mol), so the coke cools and air must be blown in alternately to reheat it.
5. It is called water gas because it is made from water (steam); it is a fuel and the starting point for H2 in the Bosch process.
6. Hence the blank is water gas (a mixture of CO and H2).
7. Water gas, mixed with more steam over an Fe2O3/Cr2O3 catalyst at about 673 K, is the feedstock for the Bosch process, which converts CO to CO2 and produces more H2. This is the link to the lecture’s industrial preparation methods.
⚡ JEE Trick Steam + coke = water gas (CO + H2); air + coke = producer gas (CO + N2). The reagent tells the name.
Answer: Water gas (CO + H2).
1 (v)
Fill in the blank: In some properties hydrogen resembles with ________ and in some other properties it resembles with ________.
C9L1VRecapQ1vPosition of hydrogen
🟡 SimilarResemblance of hydro…
metals, Non metals
Concept Hydrogen has one electron in its 1s orbital, which can either be lost to give H+, as alkali metals lose their ns1 electron, or can be joined by one more electron to complete the 1s2 (helium) configuration, as halogens gain one electron. The key prints “metals, non metals”; the textbook form is alkali metals and halogens.
Key relation H → H+ + e− (like Na) and H + e− → H− (like Cl) One electron short of the helium shell, one electron beyond the proton, so it plays both roles.
Solution
1. Electronic configuration of hydrogen is 1s1, the same ns1 pattern as Li, Na, K.
2. Like the alkali metals, it loses its one electron to give a unipositive ion H+ and has reducing character; it also forms oxides (H2O) and halides (HCl).
3. Like the halogens, it needs one more electron to reach 1s2: H + e− → H−, as in NaH and CaH2.
4. It is diatomic (H2, like F2, Cl2), non-metallic in its physical state, and its ionisation enthalpy (1312 kJ/mol) is close to the halogens, far above the alkali metals.
5. So in some properties it resembles the alkali metals (i.e. metals) and in others the halogens (i.e. non-metals).
6. The key’s “metals, non metals” is the same idea stated loosely, so the cell shows the key text.
⚡ JEE Trick Hydrogen is placed in both Group 1 and Group 17 in some tables, hence the dual resemblance. Its position is not fixed, which is the next question.
Answer: Alkali metals; halogens (key: metals, non metals).
2 (i)
True or false: The position of hydrogen is not fixed in the periodic table.
C9L1VRecapQ2iPosition of hydrogen
🟡 SimilarPosition of Hydrogen…
True
Concept Hydrogen has one electron in 1s, so it resembles Group 1 (ns1) in configuration and Group 17 (one electron short of a noble-gas shell) in behaviour, and it also resembles Group 14 in its ionisation enthalpy and electronegativity. No single group fits all of its properties, so it has no fixed position.
Key relation IE(H) = 1312 kJ/mol; electronegativity 2.1; H+ or H− or covalent bonds Properties of both metals and non-metals are shown, so placement is a convention.
Solution
1. Configuration 1s1 points to Group 1: it loses one electron to form H+ and acts as a reducing agent.
2. Gaining one electron to reach 1s2 points to Group 17, as in the hydrides NaH and CaH2 containing H−.
3. Its ionisation enthalpy (1312 kJ/mol) is far above the alkali metals (Li 520, Na 496) and close to the halogens, so the Group 1 fit is poor.
4. Its size is unique: H+ is a bare proton (about 1.5 × 10−3 pm), so it cannot copy any group exactly.
5. Periodic tables therefore show it at the top of Group 1, at the top of Group 17, or floating above the table, and this is a convention.
6. The statement “position of hydrogen is not fixed” is therefore True.
7. This is the standard conclusion in NCERT: hydrogen is placed with Group 1 in the modern table for convenience, but it is a class apart.
⚡ JEE Trick If a statement says hydrogen belongs definitely to Group 1, it is false; any statement that says its position is debatable or not fixed is true.
Answer: True.
2 (ii)
True or false: Metals below hydrogen in the electrochemical series react with dilute acids to liberate hydrogen.
C9L1VRecapQ2iiElectrochemical series, H2 from acids
🟡 SimilarDisplacement of hydr…
True
Concept A metal liberates H2 from dilute acids if it reduces H+, that is, if its reduction potential E° is lower (more negative) than that of H+/H2 = 0. In the NCERT electrochemical series (E° falling down the table, F2 at the top and Li at the bottom), metals below hydrogen have negative E° and displace H2. In the activity series, which is written the other way up, the same metals sit above hydrogen.
Key relation M + nH+ → Mn+ + (n/2)H2 if E°(Mn+/M) < 0 The metal is the stronger reducing agent than H2, so it pushes electrons onto H+.
Solution
1. The reduction potential of the standard hydrogen electrode is defined as exactly 0.00 V.
2. Metals such as Zn (−0.76 V), Fe (−0.44 V), Mg (−2.37 V) have E° below 0 and are therefore oxidised by H+.
3. In the standard E° table (decreasing E° downwards) these metals lie below hydrogen, and e.g. Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2.
4. Metals above hydrogen in that table (Cu +0.34 V, Ag, Au) cannot reduce H+ and do not release H2 with dilute non-oxidising acids.
5. The statement matches the first rule, so under the convention used by the lecture it is True.
6. Caution: if the series were written as the activity (reactivity) series, the same fact would read “metals above hydrogen”, so always identify the table’s direction.
7. Also use a non-oxidising acid (HCl, dilute H2SO4); HNO3 normally gives NO or NO2 instead.
⚡ JEE Trick The word below is decisive: it depends on the table. Use the sign of E°: negative E° means H2 is liberated from acid.
Answer: True (E° table convention).
2 (iii)
True or false: magnesium with dil. HNO3 produces hydrogen.
C9L1VRecapQ2iiiMg + very dilute HNO3
🟡 SimilarMagnesium on reactio…
True
Concept Nitric acid is an oxidising acid, and normally the nitrate ion rather than H+ is reduced, giving NO2 (conc.) or NO (dil.). Only with very dilute HNO3 (about 2%) and the very reactive metals Mg and Mn is the H+ reduced first, so H2 is evolved. The statement is therefore true in that special case.
Key relation Mg + 2HNO3 (very dil., about 2%) → Mg(NO3)2 + H2 A very dilute acid has little NO3− activity, while Mg is a strong enough reductant to reduce H+.
Solution
1. Mg has a very negative E° (−2.37 V), so it reduces H+ quickly in any acid.
2. In concentrated or moderately dilute HNO3, NO3− is the better oxidant, giving NO2 or NO instead of H2.
3. In very dilute HNO3 (about 2%) at low temperature the reduction of NO3− is slow, so Mg (and Mn) reduce H+: Mg + 2HNO3 → Mg(NO3)2 + H2.
4. Check the balance: Mg 0 → +2 (loses 2e−); 2H+ → H2 (gains 2e−), so 2 electrons balance.
5. So the statement, as taught in the lecture, is True.
6. This is the lone exception to the rule that metals + HNO3 never give H2, and the exceptional metals are only Mg and Mn.
7. For other metals (Zn, Fe, Cu) and for stronger HNO3, nitrogen oxides are formed instead and H2 is not.
⚡ JEE Trick The exceptions to remember: only Mg and Mn with very dilute HNO3 give H2. Write “true, with that condition”, and do not generalise to other metals.
Answer: True (only very dilute HNO3, Mg or Mn).
2 (iv)
True or false: Hydrogen is most abundant element on the earth.
C9L1VRecapQ2ivAbundance of hydrogen
🟡 SimilarUnderstanding the Mo…
False
Concept Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (about 70% by mass, 90% by atoms), because it makes up the sun and stars. On the earth it is far less common, because the light H2 escaped from the atmosphere and the earth is mostly rock and iron. The statement says “on the earth”, so it is false.
Key relation Earth’s crust (by mass): O 46%, Si 28%, Al 8%, Fe 5%, … H about 0.15% Hydrogen on the earth is mostly tied up in water and hydrocarbons, a small mass fraction.
Solution
1. In the universe: H about 70% by mass and He about 28%, so hydrogen is number 1.
2. On the earth’s crust, the order is O (46%) > Si (28%) > Al (8%) > Fe (5%) > Ca > Na > K > Mg, and hydrogen is 0.15%, about ninth.
3. Over the whole earth including oceans and atmosphere, hydrogen is still below oxygen by mass (in oceans O is about 86% of water by mass).
4. Free H2 in the atmosphere is only about 0.5 ppm, because earth’s gravity cannot hold such a light gas.
5. Most earthly hydrogen exists in combined form: water, acids, hydrocarbons and organic compounds.
6. The statement “most abundant element on the earth” is therefore False.
7. Correct form: “most abundant element in the universe”.
⚡ JEE Trick The two-word change decides it: universe gives hydrogen, earth’s crust gives oxygen. Read the location in the statement before answering.
Answer: False (true for the universe, not for the earth).
2 (v)
True or false: Tritium can be obtained from natural sources.
C9L1VRecapQ2vOccurrence of tritium
🟡 SimilarTritium/ its half li…
False
Concept Tritium is radioactive with a half-life of about 12.3 years, so any primordial tritium has long decayed. A tiny steady amount (about 1 atom in 1018 of hydrogen) is created in the upper atmosphere by cosmic rays, but this is not a source from which it can be obtained. It is manufactured artificially in nuclear reactors.
Key relation 63Li + 10n → 42He + 31H Neutron capture by lithium-6 is the usual source of tritium.
Solution
1. Tritium decays by β− emission with t1/2 = 12.3 years, so over geological time it has disappeared.
2. Its natural abundance is only about 1 atom per 1018 hydrogen atoms, formed by cosmic-ray neutrons striking atmospheric nitrogen.
3. Such traces are far too small to be a practical natural source, so we say tritium cannot be obtained from natural sources in usable amounts.
4. It is obtained artificially by irradiating lithium-6 with slow neutrons in a reactor: 6Li + n → 4He + 3H.
5. It can also be produced by bombarding deuterium with deuterons: 2H + 2H → 3H + 1H.
6. So the given statement is False.
7. Contrast: deuterium (0.015%) is obtained from natural water by electrolysis or fractional distillation, which is why it is called a natural isotope.
⚡ JEE Trick If the isotope is radioactive with a short half-life, it is not found naturally in usable amounts and must be made artificially. Tritium is the only radioactive hydrogen isotope.
Answer: False.
3 (i)
Hydrogen has the tendency to gain one electron to acquire helium configuration; in this respect it resembles:
(a) alkali metals   (b) carbon   (c) alkaline earth metals   (d) halogens
C9L1VRecapQ3iResemblance with halogens
🟢 ExactHydrogen has the ten…
(d) halogens
Concept Gaining one electron to reach a helium configuration (1s2) means hydrogen forms H−, a uninegative ion. A halogen also gains one electron to reach the nearest noble-gas configuration. Alkali metals do the opposite (they lose one electron), and carbon and alkaline earth metals do neither.
Key relation H + e− → H− (1s2)    X + e− → X− (ns2np6) Both complete their valence shell by taking exactly one electron.
Solution
1. Hydrogen is 1s1, one electron short of helium 1s2, so it can gain one electron to give H−.
2. (a) Alkali metals have ns1 and lose one electron (M+), which resembles hydrogen forming H+, not gaining; so (a) does not fit.
3. (c) Alkaline earth metals have ns2 and lose two electrons (M2+), so (c) is wrong.
4. (b) Carbon (2s22p2) completes its octet by sharing four electrons, not by gaining one, so (b) is wrong.
5. (d) Halogens (ns2np5) gain one electron to reach a noble-gas shell, as hydrogen does, e.g. in NaH like NaCl: this fits.
6. Evidence: H2 + 2Na → 2Na+H−, just as Cl2 + 2Na → 2Na+Cl−; both are diatomic and form ionic compounds with active metals.
7. So the answer is (d) halogens.
⚡ JEE Trick The tendency to gain one electron is the halogen signature; the tendency to lose one is the alkali signature. Hydrogen has both, which is why it is described as having dual character.
Answer: (d) halogens.
3 (ii)
Tritium contains ________ neutron/neutrons:
(a) 4   (b) 3   (c) 2   (d) 1
C9L1VRecapQ3iiNeutrons in tritium
🟢 ExactThe nucleus of a tri…
(c) 2
Concept Tritium is the isotope 31H: mass number 3 and atomic number 1. The number of neutrons is the mass number minus the atomic number. The trap is to pick 3 (the mass number) or 1 (the proton count).
Key relation N = A − Z = 3 − 1 = 2 The mass number counts protons plus neutrons together.
Solution
1. Tritium has atomic number Z = 1 (it is hydrogen) so it has 1 proton.
2. Its mass number is A = 3, the total number of nucleons.
3. Neutrons: N = A − Z = 3 − 1 = 2.
4. Compare with protium (A = 1, N = 0) and deuterium (A = 2, N = 1); the series 0, 1, 2 rises by one each time.
5. Check the options: (a) 4 and (b) 3 exceed A − Z; (d) 1 is deuterium’s count; only (c) 2 matches.
6. The n/p ratio of 2 explains why tritium is unstable and radioactive, decaying by β− emission to 3He.
7. Tritium therefore has 1 proton, 1 electron and 2 neutrons.
8. Mass-number check using atomic masses: tritium weighs about 3.016 u, which is roughly three nucleons, consistent with 1 proton plus 2 neutrons; its nucleus is called a triton. The same subtraction works for any isotope: for 2H it gives 1, for 3H it gives 2, and for the heavy water oxygen isotope 18O it gives 10.
⚡ JEE Trick Neutrons = mass number minus atomic number. For the three isotopes of hydrogen the neutron counts are simply 0, 1 and 2.
Answer: (c) 2.
3 (iii)
Pure hydrogen is obtained by carrying out electrolysis of:
(a) Water containing H2SO4   (b) Water containing NaOH   (c) KOH solution   (d) Ba(OH)2 solution
C9L1VRecapQ3iiiPure H2 by electrolysis
🟢 ExactPure hydrogen is obt…
(d) Ba(OH)₂ solution
Concept Hydrogen of very high purity (above 99.95%) is obtained by electrolysing warm aqueous barium hydroxide between nickel electrodes. The reason is that Ba(OH)2 is a strongly basic, non-volatile electrolyte whose ions are not discharged in competition with water. The trap is to treat all acids and bases as equivalent: they differ in the gases they contaminate.
Key relation Cathode: 2H2O + 2e− → H2 + 2OH−; anode: 4OH− → 2H2O + O2 + 4e− Net: 2H2O → 2H2 + O2, with only H2 at the cathode.
Solution
1. Pure water is a very poor conductor, so a small amount of electrolyte is added; it must not itself be discharged at the electrodes.
2. Warm aqueous Ba(OH)2 between Ni electrodes gives H2 at the cathode and O2 at the anode, and the H2 is more than 99.95% pure.
3. (a) With H2SO4, anodic side reactions (S2O82−, O3) and mist can contaminate the gases.
4. (b) NaOH and (c) KOH solutions also give H2, but they are the technical electrolytes of commercial cells and the gas is not taken as the purest.
5. The textbook (NCERT) states that it is the electrolysis of warm aqueous Ba(OH)2 that gives the highest purity, so (d) is the intended option.
6. Electrolysis uses electricity (about 4.5 kWh per m3), so it is used only when purity matters, not for bulk hydrogen.
7. So the answer is (d) Ba(OH)2 solution.
⚡ JEE Trick The phrase to remember is “pure hydrogen = electrolysis of warm aqueous Ba(OH)2 using Ni electrodes”. Do not pick (a) just because acidified water is the common school set-up.
Answer: (d) Ba(OH)2 solution.
3 (iv)
In Bosch’s process which gas is utilised for the production of hydrogen?
(a) Producer gas   (b) Water gas   (c) Coal gas   (d) Natural gas
C9L1VRecapQ3ivBosch process
🟢 ExactIn Bosch's process w…
(b) Water gas
Concept In the Bosch process, water gas (CO + H2) is mixed with more steam and passed over an Fe2O3/Cr2O3 catalyst at about 673 K. The CO is oxidised to CO2 and extra hydrogen is formed, then CO2 is removed. The trap is to pick producer gas (CO + N2), which would leave nitrogen in the product.
Key relation CO + H2 (water gas) + H2O → CO2 + 2H2 (Fe2O3/Cr2O3, 673 K) The water-gas shift reaction converts CO to CO2 and makes more H2.
Solution
1. Make water gas: C + H2O → CO + H2 (steam over red-hot coke, about 1270 K).
2. Mix the water gas with excess steam and pass over Fe2O3 promoted by Cr2O3 at about 673 K: CO + H2O → CO2 + H2.
3. The net gas is H2 plus CO2 (and traces of CO); CO2 is removed by scrubbing with water under 25–30 atm pressure (or with alkali).
4. Pure H2 then remains, with the hydrogen yield doubled compared with the water-gas content alone.
5. (a) Producer gas (CO + N2) carries inert nitrogen into the product, so it is not used for hydrogen.
6. (c) Coal gas (H2, CH4, CO) and (d) natural gas (CH4) belong to different processes (steam reforming for CH4).
7. So the gas used in Bosch’s process is (b) water gas.
⚡ JEE Trick Bosch = water gas + steam over an iron catalyst. Lane’s process is the one with iron and steam directly. Note that the same water-gas-shift step is used in the Haber process hydrogen plant.
Answer: (b) Water gas.
3 (v)
The high density of water as compared to ice is due to:
(a) hydrogen bonding interactions   (b) dipole-dipole interactions   (c) dipole-induced dipole interactions   (d) induced dipole induced dipole interactions
C9L1VRecapQ3vDensity of water vs ice
🟢 ExactThe high density of…
(a) hydrogen bonding interactions
Concept Ice is less dense than liquid water, and the cause is the geometry that hydrogen bonding imposes. The question asks for the intermolecular force responsible, and only hydrogen bonding is directional enough to build an open lattice.
Key relation density = mass/volume; open H-bonded lattice → larger volume per molecule The same 18 g of water occupies more volume as ice, so ice is the less dense phase.
Solution
1. In ice each O atom is bonded to its own two H atoms and, through two hydrogen bonds, to two more H atoms of neighbours, giving four tetrahedrally arranged neighbours.
2. This fixed tetrahedral arrangement forms a hexagonal open cage with large empty channels, so a mole of ice takes about 19.6 cm3.
3. On melting, about 15% of the hydrogen bonds break and the rigid lattice collapses; molecules slip into the empty spaces and the molar volume falls to about 18 cm3.
4. Density = 18 g / 18 cm3 = 1.00 g/cm3 for water at 4 °C, against 18 g / 19.6 cm3 = 0.92 g/cm3 for ice, so water is denser.
5. The maximum density of water at 4 °C arises because above 4 °C ordinary thermal expansion takes over, while below it more ordered H-bonded clusters form and enlarge the volume.
6. Check the options: (b), (c) and (d) are weak, non-directional forces that exist in many liquids, and none of them explains why the solid is less dense than its liquid. Only (a) fits.
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question asks about an anomalous property of water (high boiling point, ice floating, maximum density at 4 °C), the answer is nearly always hydrogen bonding. Distractors name general van der Waals forces, which operate in every liquid and so cannot be the special reason.
Answer: (a) hydrogen bonding interactions.

Concept Match each description to the correct species by recalling what each is made of. Syngas is a gas mixture, while tritium and deuterium are isotopes of hydrogen that differ only in neutron number.
Key relation water gas: C + H2O → CO + H2; isotopes: 1H, 2H (D), 3H (T) Isotopes share Z = 1 and differ only in mass number A = 1 + number of neutrons.
Solution
1. (A) Water gas / syngas. Steam passed over red-hot coke gives C + H2O → CO + H2, a 1 : 1 mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, so (A) matches (q) CO + H2.
2. (B) Radioactive. Of the three isotopes of hydrogen, protium and deuterium are stable and only tritium is radioactive, so (B) matches (r).
3. Tritium emits a weak β− particle: 3H → 3He + e−, with a half-life of about 12.3 years.
4. (C) One proton + one neutron. The nucleus with Z = 1 and one neutron has A = 2, which is deuterium (D), so (C) matches (p).
5. By contrast, tritium has one proton and two neutrons (A = 3), and protium has no neutron at all, so there is no ambiguity between (p) and (r).
6. Collecting the pairs gives the matching A-q, B-r, C-p.
⚡ JEE Trick Count neutrons: 0, 1, 2 for protium, deuterium, tritium; the one with the unstable neutron-rich nucleus is tritium. Do not mix up (p) and (r): deuterium is the 1p + 1n species, and it is not radioactive.
Answer: A-q, B-r, C-p.

2. (b) Al2O3: the lattice enthalpy and the Al–O bond energy are so large that hydrogen cannot reduce it. Not reduced.
3. (c) Fe2O3: Fe2O3 + 3H2 → 2Fe + 3H2O (heated). Reduced.
4. (d) CuO: CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, the classical black-to-red demonstration. Reduced.
5. Rule of thumb: oxides of metals below hydrogen in the activity series, and of those moderately above it such as Fe, Sn and Zn, are reducible by H2.
6. Oxides of Al, Mg, Ca and the alkali metals need a much stronger route such as electrolysis or very high temperature carbon reduction.
7. Hence the oxides reduced are SnO2, Fe2O3 and CuO: (a), (c), (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Only the oxide of the strongly electropositive metal (Al here) resists. Spot it by asking whether the metal sits near the top of the activity series; the other three are all easy.
Answer: (a), (c) and (d).

[One or more than one option may be correct] Hydrogen will reduce :
(a) heated cupric oxide   (b) heated ferric oxide   (c) heated zinc oxide   (d) sodium oxide
C9L1V6Q5Reduction of oxides by H2
🟢 ExactHydrogen will reduce…
(a), (b), (c) CuO, Fe2O3, ZnO
Concept The same principle as the previous question: heated hydrogen takes oxygen from the oxides of moderately reactive metals but not from those of the alkali metals.
Key relation MO + H2 → M + H2O (heat) Only metals whose oxide is less stable than water can be freed this way.
Solution
1. (a) Heated CuO: CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O. Reduced.
2. (b) Heated Fe2O3: Fe2O3 + 3H2 → 2Fe + 3H2O. Reduced.
3. (c) Heated ZnO: ZnO + H2 → Zn + H2O is accepted in the book’s list; it needs high temperature and a steady stream of hydrogen that removes the water formed. Counted as reduced.
4. (d) Na2O: sodium is so strongly electropositive that its oxide is far more stable than water, so H2 cannot reduce it. Not reduced.
5. In fact sodium reacts with hydrogen the other way round, 2Na + H2 → 2NaH, so hydrogen is the oxidant there, never the reductant.
6. The pattern: reducible oxides belong to Cu, Fe, Zn, Sn, Pb; non-reducible oxides to Al, Mg, Ca and the alkali metals.
7. Hence H2 reduces (a), (b) and (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Anything with an alkali-metal oxide in a ‘hydrogen reduces’ list is the odd one out. Zn is borderline, but the book counts it, so it joins Cu and Fe.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).

[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following pairs of substances on reaction will evolve H2 gas?
(a) Iron and dilute H2SO4   (b) Copper and dilute HCl   (c) Sodium and ethyl alcohol   (d) Iron and steam
C9L1V6Q6Reactions evolving H2
🟢 ExactWhich of the followi…
(a), (c), (d) a, c, d
Concept H2 is evolved whenever an active metal supplies electrons to a proton donor: an acid, water or an alcohol. A metal that cannot reduce H+ gives no gas.
Key relation M + 2H+ → M2+ + H2 The metal must lie above hydrogen in the activity series (negative E°).
Solution
1. (a) Fe + dil. H2SO4: Fe + H2SO4 → FeSO4 + H2↑, since E°(Fe2+/Fe) = −0.44 V. Evolves H2.
2. (b) Cu + dil. HCl: E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V, which is above hydrogen, so copper cannot displace H2 from a non-oxidising acid. No gas.
3. (c) Na + C2H5OH: 2Na + 2C2H5OH → 2C2H5ONa + H2↑, the alcoholic O–H acts as a weak acid. Evolves H2.
4. (d) Fe + steam: 3Fe + 4H2O(g) → Fe3O4 + 4H2, the reaction of Lane’s process. Evolves H2.
5. The one failing pair is the unreactive metal with a non-oxidising acid; copper dissolves only in oxidising acids such as HNO3, which give NO or NO2, not H2.
6. Hence the pairs that evolve H2 are (a), (c) and (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Scan for the metal below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au, Hg) and discard it. The tempting wrong idea is that every metal plus acid gives H2.
Answer: (a), (c) and (d).

[One or more than one option may be correct] Which of the following metals on treatment with NaOH will liberate H2 gas?
(a) Zn   (b) Sn   (c) Al   (d) Mg
C9L1V6Q7Metals + NaOH → H2
🟢 ExactWhich of the followi…
(a), (b), (c) Zn, Sn, Al
Concept Hydrogen is liberated from aqueous alkali only by amphoteric metals. The trap is assuming that any active metal such as Mg reacts the same way with acid and with base.
Key relation M + OH− + H2O → [M-oxo anion] + H2 This works only if the metal hydroxide is amphoteric and can dissolve in excess alkali.
Solution
1. Metals that give H2 with aqueous alkali are the amphoteric ones, whose oxides and hydroxides dissolve in both acid and base, forming oxo-anions (zincate, stannate, aluminate).
2. (a) Zn: Zn + 2NaOH → Na2ZnO2 + H2↑. Zn(OH)2 is amphoteric, so zinc dissolves in hot concentrated alkali. Correct.
3. (b) Sn: Sn + 2NaOH + H2O → Na2SnO3 + 2H2↑ (sodium stannate on heating; the stannite form is also quoted). Tin is amphoteric. Correct.
4. (c) Al: 2Al + 2NaOH + 2H2O → 2NaAlO2 + 3H2↑. The alkali strips the protective Al2O3 film as aluminate and the bare metal then reduces water. Correct.
5. (d) Mg: Mg(OH)2 is purely basic, so there is no soluble magnesate to form and the metal is not attacked by NaOH. Not correct.
6. In each correct case the metal is oxidised and hydrogen of water is reduced to H2, so these are also valid laboratory preparations of dihydrogen.
7. Hence the metals are Zn, Sn and Al: (a), (b), (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the trio Zn, Al, Sn (also Be, Pb) as the amphoteric metals. Mg and Ca react with acids and water but never with NaOH, which is the usual distractor. This question repeats Level 5 Q10.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).

[One or more than one option may be correct] According to the methods described for preparation of dihydrogen, hydrogen gas can be obtained by which of the following processes?
(a) Passing steam over red-hot iron   (b) Electrolysis of water containing a small amount of acid or base   (c) Treating copper with dilute sulphuric acid   (d) Treating aluminium with boiling alkali solution
C9L1V6Q8Preparation of dihydrogen
🟡 SimilarCh 9 - Hydrogen | Pr…
(a), (b), (d) a, b, d
Concept Each option names a route to dihydrogen. A route is valid if the reagents actually release H2; the odd one will involve a metal that cannot displace hydrogen.
Key relation Fe/steam, electrolysis, amphoteric metal/alkali all liberate H2 These three are the methods quoted in the lecture’s preparation list.
Solution
1. (a) Steam over red-hot iron: 3Fe + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 4H2 (Lane’s process). Yes.
2. (b) Electrolysis of acidified or alkaline water: 2H2O → 2H2 + O2; the dissolved acid or base only makes the water conducting, and H2 collects at the cathode. Yes.
3. (c) Copper + dil. H2SO4: copper lies below hydrogen (E° = +0.34 V), so it cannot reduce H+. No H2.
4. (d) Aluminium + boiling alkali: 2Al + 2NaOH + 2H2O → 2NaAlO2 + 3H2↑, because aluminium is amphoteric. Yes.
5. In (b) the cathode reaction 2H+ + 2e− → H2 gives twice the volume of H2 as O2 at the anode.
6. Hence the valid methods are (a), (b) and (d); (c) fails.
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever Cu, Ag or Au is paired with dilute acid in a hydrogen-preparation list, strike it out at once. Copper reacts only with oxidising acids and gives SO2 or NOx.
Answer: (a), (b) and (d).

(a) Hydrogen can reduce heated cupric oxide to copper   (b) Hydrogen can reduce oxides of alkali metals   (c) Hydrogen generally acts as a reducing agent   (d) Hydrogen can reduce some metal ions in aqueous solution
C9L1V6Q9Reducing behaviour of H2
🟡 SimilarShow with suitable e…
(a), (c), (d) a, c, d
Concept Hydrogen is a good reducing agent when heated or catalysed, but its reducing power is limited by the stability of the oxide it must attack.
Key relation MO + H2 → M + H2O; Mn+ + (n/2)H2 → M + nH+ Reduction needs the oxide (or ion) to be easier to reduce than H+.
Solution
1. (a) CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O on heating. Correct.
2. (b) The oxides of alkali metals (Na2O, K2O) are very stable; H2 cannot reduce them. Incorrect.
3. (c) With most oxides and with non-metals such as Cl2 (H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl), hydrogen is the electron donor, and the lecture states that it is generally a reducing agent. Only with active metals (hydrides) is it the oxidant. Correct.
4. (d) Hydrogen reduces the ions of noble and less active metals, e.g. Ag+, Cu2+, Pd2+ in aqueous solution, under pressure or with a catalyst: 2Ag+ + H2 → 2Ag + 2H+. Correct.
5. Option (b) is the only wrong one, because the metals of Group 1 sit far above hydrogen; with them hydrogen is itself reduced to give NaH.
6. The oxidation number of hydrogen rises from 0 to +1 in (a), (c), (d), confirming reductant behaviour.
7. Hence the correct statements are (a), (c) and (d).
⚡ JEE Trick The wrong statement in this family is nearly always the one that says H2 reduces an s-block oxide. Same logic as Q4 and Q5 above.
Answer: (a), (c) and (d).

[One or more than one option may be correct] The position of hydrogen in the periodic table is considered anomalous. Which of the following statements correctly explain this fact?
(a) Hydrogen shows similarities with alkali metals   (b) Hydrogen shows similarities with halogens   (c) Hydrogen readily forms free H+ ions like alkali metals   (d) Hydrogen does not fit completely in group 1 or group 17
C9L1V6Q10Anomalous position of H
🟡 SimilarThe position of hydr…
(a), (b), (d) a, b, d
Concept Hydrogen is placed in Group 1 in most tables, yet it also resembles the halogens. Its position is called anomalous because it fits neither group completely.
Key relation 1s1 (like alkali metals), one electron short of 1s2 (like halogens) The explanation must cite genuine similarities, not properties that hydrogen does not have.
Solution
1. (a) Like alkali metals, hydrogen has one valence electron (1s1) and can form H+ (as in H3O+), with oxidation number +1. Valid reason.
2. (b) Like halogens, it is diatomic (H2), is one electron short of a noble-gas configuration and forms H− (NaH, like NaCl). Valid reason.
3. (c) Hydrogen does not readily form free H+: its I.E. is 1312 kJ mol−1, much higher than Na (496), and the bare proton is not free. This is a difference, not an explanation. Invalid.
4. (d) Because it matches alkali metals in some respects and halogens in others, it does not belong wholly to Group 1 or Group 17. Valid reason.
5. Many tables therefore place it separately at the top, or in Group 1 by convention.
6. Hence the statements that explain the anomaly are (a), (b) and (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Any option claiming hydrogen forms H+ readily or exists as a free ion is a trap; its high ionisation enthalpy is what keeps it out of Group 1.
Answer: (a), (b) and (d).
