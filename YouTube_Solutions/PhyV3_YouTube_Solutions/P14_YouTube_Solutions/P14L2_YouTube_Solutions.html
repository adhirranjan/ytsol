1What is the SI unit of stress? (a) Newton (b) Pascal (c) Joule (d) MeterSI unit of stressWhat is SI Unit of Stress? 🟢 ExactbPascal (1 Pa = 1 N/m2)
Concept Stress is the internal restoring force developed per unit area of cross-section when a body is deformed, σ = F/A. Because it is a force divided by an area, stress carries the units of pressure, never of force or of energy. The four options are a deliberate trap: they offer force (newton), energy (joule) and a length (metre) alongside the correct force-per-area unit, which in SI has the special name pascal.
Formula σ = F/A, so [σ] = [force]/[area] = N/m2 = Pa. The pascal is a derived SI unit: 1 Pa is the stress produced by a normal force of 1 N spread over 1 m2.
Solution
1. Write the definition of stress: restoring force per unit area, σ = F/A.
2. Insert SI units: force F is measured in newton (N), area A in square metre (m2).
3. Divide to get the unit of stress: N/m2.Newton is kg·m·s−2, so N/m2 = kg·m−1·s−2, exactly the dimensions of pressure.
4. This combination N/m2 is given the special SI name pascal: 1 Pa = 1 N/m2.
5. Reject the distractors: newton is a force, joule (N·m) is energy, metre is a length — none is a force per unit area.
6. Therefore the SI unit of stress is the pascal, option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Any quantity defined as a force spread over an area shares pressure's unit, the pascal. Keep newton for force, joule for work or energy, watt for power; a one-second units check eliminates three of the four options here without any arithmetic.
Answer: (b) Pascal — the SI unit of stress is 1 Pa = 1 N/m2.
2A wire with cross-sectional area A = 2 × 10−6 m2 and Young’s modulus Y = 2 × 1011 Pa is stretched by force F = 100 N. What is the stress? (a) 2 × 104 Pa (b) 5 × 104 Pa (c) 1 × 105 Pa (d) 5 × 107 PaStress = F/AHow To Calculate The Stress Of A Wire | Elasticity in Physics 🟡 Similard5 × 107 Pa
Concept The stress in a stretched wire is fixed by the load and the cross-section alone, σ = F/A; it has nothing to do with how stiff the material is. The Young's modulus Y = 2 × 1011 Pa is quoted only to tempt you into multiplying or dividing by it — it would be needed to find the strain or the extension, but the stress is already determined by force over area.
Formula σ = F/A. Stress is defined before any material property enters; Y connects stress to strain through σ = Yε, a later step not asked for here.
Solution
1. List the data: F = 100 N, A = 2 × 10−6 m2, Y = 2 × 1011 Pa (not required).
2. Apply the definition of stress: σ = F/A = 100 / (2 × 10−6).
3. Evaluate: 100 / (2 × 10−6) = 50 / 10−6 = 50 × 106 = 5 × 107 Pa.Dividing by 10−6 is the same as multiplying by 106; that single step does all the work.
4. Check the unit: N divided by m2 gives N/m2 = Pa, so 5 × 107 Pa is dimensionally correct.
5. Confirm Y is a red herring: using it would give strain ε = σ/Y = 2.5 × 10−4, a different quantity than the one asked.
6. Match to the options: 5 × 107 Pa is option (d).
⚡ JEE Trick When a numerical problem hands you a Young's modulus but only asks for stress, ignore it — stress is force over area, full stop. The examiner plants Y so that a hurried student writes σ = YF/A or F/(AY) and lands on one of the wrong options.
Answer: (d) 5 × 107 Pa.
3The length of an elastic string is ‘a’ metre when the tension is 4 N, and ‘b’ metre when the tension is 5 N. Calculate the length in metre when the tension is 9 N.Natural length of an elastic stringThe length of an elastic string is a metre when the longitudinal tension is 4 N and the length is b 🟢 Exact(5b − 4a) metre
Concept An elastic string obeys Hooke's law, so its stretched length is its natural (unstretched) length plus an extension that is directly proportional to the tension: L = L0 + kT, where k is a constant for the string. The two given states (length a at 4 N, length b at 5 N) are just two points on this straight line; two points fix both unknowns L0 and k, after which any third tension can be read off. The answer must be linear in a and b.
Formula L = L0 + kT, with k = extension per unit tension. k here plays the role of 1/(stiffness); the natural length L0 is the intercept at T = 0.
Solution
Given length a at tension 4 N, length b at tension 5 N
Find the length when the tension is 9 N
1. Write the two data states: a = L0 + 4k and b = L0 + 5k.
2. Subtract the first from the second to isolate k: b − a = (5k − 4k) = k, so k = b − a.Each extra newton of tension lengthens the string by (b − a), the per-newton stretch.
3. Back-substitute for the natural length: L0 = a − 4k = a − 4(b − a) = 5a − 4b.
4. Write the length at T = 9 N: L = L0 + 9k = (5a − 4b) + 9(b − a).
5. Simplify: 5a − 4b + 9b − 9a = −4a + 5b = 5b − 4a.The natural length cancels neatly, leaving a clean combination of the two measured lengths.
6. Quick check: at T = 4, 5b − 4a with the slope gives back a; at T = 5 it gives b — both recovered, so the expression is consistent.
⚡ JEE Trick Treat length-versus-tension as a straight line y = c + kx: find the slope by subtracting the two states, then extrapolate. A fast shortcut uses extrapolation of equal steps: going 4→5 N adds (b−a); going 5→9 N is four more steps, adding 4(b−a), so L = b + 4(b−a) = 5b − 4a — same answer in one line.
Answer: the length at a tension of 9 N is (5b − 4a) metre.
4What mass must be suspended from a steel wire 2 m long and 1 mm diameter to stretch it by 1 mm? Given: Young’s modulus of steel = 2 × 1012 dyne cm−2, g = 981 cm s−2.Mass from extension (CGS)A steel wire of diameter 1 mm and length 2 m is stretched by applying a force 🟡 Similar≈ 8.01 kg
Concept The load needed to stretch a wire a given amount follows from Young's modulus rearranged for force: F = YAΔL/L. Since the data are in CGS (dyne, cm), it is cleanest to work entirely in CGS and convert the final force to a mass with the supplied g = 981 cm s−2. The diameter must be halved to a radius and the area taken as πr2 — using the diameter directly is the classic factor-of-four slip.
Formula F = YAΔL/L and m = F/g, with A = πr2. This is Y = FL/(AΔL) solved for F; every quantity is kept in CGS so no unit conversion creeps in.
Solution
1. Convert to CGS: L = 2 m = 200 cm, ΔL = 1 mm = 0.1 cm, diameter 1 mm = 0.1 cm so radius r = 0.05 cm, Y = 2 × 1012 dyne cm−2.
2. Cross-sectional area: A = πr2 = π(0.05)2 = π × 2.5 × 10−3 = 7.854 × 10−3 cm2.Halve the diameter first; squaring 0.05 (not 0.1) is where marks are usually lost.
3. Substitute into F = YAΔL/L = (2 × 1012)(7.854 × 10−3)(0.1)/200.
4. Evaluate step by step: (2 × 1012)(7.854 × 10−3) = 1.571 × 1010; times 0.1 = 1.571 × 109; divided by 200 = 7.854 × 106 dyne.
5. Convert force to mass: m = F/g = 7.854 × 106 / 981 = 8006 g.In CGS, force in dyne divided by g in cm s−2 gives mass in gram directly.
6. Express in kilogram: m = 8006 g ≈ 8.01 kg.
⚡ JEE Trick When a problem is served in dyne and centimetre, do not convert to SI midway — stay in CGS and the mass drops out in grams because 1 dyne = 1 g·cm·s−2. The two traps are using diameter for radius (factor 4) and mixing units; sidestep both and the arithmetic is a single line.
Answer: a mass of about 8.01 kg must be suspended.
5The figure shows the strain–stress curve for a given material (stress in 106 N m−2 plotted against strain, a straight line from the origin to point A at about strain 0.004, stress 300). What are (i) Young’s modulus and (ii) the approximate yield strength for this material?Young’s modulus & yield strength from a graphStress vs Strain Curve For Tensile Materials 🟡 SimilarY ≈ 7.5 × 1010 N m−2; yield strength ≈ 3 × 108 N m−2
Concept On a stress–strain graph two quite different numbers are read off. The Young's modulus is the slope of the initial straight (elastic) portion, σ/ε, because in that region stress is proportional to strain. The yield (yield strength) is the stress value at which the straight line ends and permanent deformation begins — a point on the vertical axis, not a slope. Keep the axis scale in mind: stress is plotted in units of 106 N m−2.
Formula Y = σ/ε (slope of the linear part) and yield strength = stress at the end of the linear part. Only the straight segment obeys Hooke's law, so the slope is meaningful solely there.
Solution
Given stress–strain line from origin to σ = 300 × 10⁶ N/m² at ε ≈ 0.004
Find (i) the Young’s modulus and (ii) the approximate yield strength
1. Identify the linear region: the line runs straight from the origin up to point A near strain 0.004 at a stress of 300 × 106 N m−2.
2. Read a convenient point on that line: (ε, σ) = (0.004, 300 × 106 N m−2).Using the top of the straight part maximises the run and keeps the slope reading accurate.
3. Compute the slope: Y = σ/ε = (300 × 106) / 0.004.
4. Evaluate: 300 × 106 / 4 × 10−3 = 75 × 109 = 7.5 × 1010 N m−2.
5. Read the yield strength: it is the stress where the curve stops being straight, i.e. at point A, so σyield ≈ 300 × 106 = 3 × 108 N m−2.Beyond A the material would not return to its original length — that onset stress is the yield strength.
6. State both results together: Y ≈ 7.5 × 1010 N m−2 and yield ≈ 3 × 108 N m−2.
⚡ JEE Trick Do not confuse the two readings: Young's modulus is a gradient (rise over run of the elastic line), while yield strength is a height (the stress where the line bends over). Watch the axis multiplier — here stress is in 106 N m−2, so 300 on the axis means 3 × 108 N m−2.
Answer: (i) Y ≈ 7.5 × 1010 N m−2; (ii) yield strength ≈ 3 × 108 N m−2.

1Copper and steel wires of the same length and same area of cross section are joined end to end. The composite wire is suspended from a rigid support and the load is applied to its free end. If the increase in length of the composite wire is 3.1 mm, find the increase in length of the copper wire and the steel wire. (Ycu = 1.1 × 1011 N/m2, Ysteel = 2 × 1011 N/m2)Series wires, same F and AA copper wire and a steel wire of the same length and same cross-section are joined end to end 🟢 Exactcopper 2.0 mm, steel 1.1 mm
Concept When two wires are joined end to end and hung in series, the same tension F runs through both (the lower wire pulls the upper one), and here they also share the same length L and the same area A. Under those conditions the elongation ΔL = FL/(AY) depends only on Y, so the softer copper (smaller Y) stretches more than the stiffer steel, in the inverse ratio of their moduli. The two elongations must add up to the measured 3.1 mm.
Formula ΔL = FL/(AY), hence ΔLcu/ΔLst = Yst/Ycu. F, L and A cancel because they are common to both wires, leaving a pure modulus ratio.
Solution
Given equal length and area; Y_Cu = 1.1 × 10¹¹ N/m², Y_steel = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m²; total extension 3.1 mm
Find the extension of the copper wire and of the steel wire
1. Same F, L, A for both wires, so ΔL is inversely proportional to Y.
2. Ratio of elongations: ΔLcu/ΔLst = Yst/Ycu = (2 × 1011)/(1.1 × 1011) = 20/11.Copper, being less stiff, carries the larger share of the total stretch.
3. Let the steel elongation be ΔLst; then ΔLcu = (20/11)ΔLst.
4. Add to the known total: (20/11)ΔLst + ΔLst = 3.1 mm, i.e. (31/11)ΔLst = 3.1 mm.
5. Solve for steel: ΔLst = 3.1 × 11/31 = 1.1 mm.The tidy 31 in the denominator is why 3.1 mm was chosen — it splits cleanly.
6. Copper follows by subtraction: ΔLcu = 3.1 − 1.1 = 2.0 mm.
⚡ JEE Trick In a series pair with equal F, L and A, remember ΔL ∝ 1/Y: the softer wire stretches more. Set the ratio first, then split the known total — never compute F from scratch, because it cancels. A sign check: copper (2.0 mm) must exceed steel (1.1 mm), as it does.
Answer: the copper wire stretches 2.0 mm and the steel wire stretches 1.1 mm.
2A mass of 2 kg is hung from a steel wire of radius 0.5 mm and length 3 m. Compute the extension produced. What should be the minimum radius of the wire so that the elastic limit is not exceeded? (Elastic limit of steel = 2.4 × 108 N/m2, Ysteel = 2 × 1011 N/m2)Extension & minimum radiusThe elastic limit of a steel cable is 2.40x10^8 Pa and the cross-section area is 4.00 mm^2 🟡 Similarextension ≈ 0.382 mm; minimum radius ≈ 0.163 mm
Concept This is two questions in one. The extension comes straight from Young's modulus, ΔL = FL/(AY), with the given radius. The minimum radius is a strength condition: the stress F/A must not exceed the elastic limit, so the smallest safe area is Amin = F/σlimit, from which the radius follows. Note the two parts use different cross-sections — the actual one for the stretch, the critical one for the limit.
Formula ΔL = FL/(AY) and rmin = √(F/(πσlimit)). The second comes from F/(πr2) = σlimit solved for r.
Solution
Given m = 2 kg, r = 0.5 mm, L = 3 m, σ_limit = 2.4 × 10⁸ N/m², Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m²
Find the extension produced and the minimum radius of the wire
1. Load: F = mg = 2 × 10 = 20 N (taking g = 10 m s−2). Given L = 3 m, r = 0.5 mm = 5 × 10−4 m.
2. Area of the actual wire: A = πr2 = π(5 × 10−4)2 = 7.854 × 10−7 m2.
3. Extension: ΔL = FL/(AY) = (20)(3)/((7.854 × 10−7)(2 × 1011)) = 60 / (1.571 × 105) = 3.82 × 10−4 m ≈ 0.382 mm.AY = 1.571 × 105 N is the wire's stiffness × length product; dividing FL by it gives the stretch.
4. Strength condition for the minimum radius: stress must not exceed the elastic limit, F/Amin = σlimit.
5. Minimum area: Amin = F/σlimit = 20 / (2.4 × 108) = 8.33 × 10−8 m2.
6. Minimum radius: rmin = √(Amin/π) = √(8.33 × 10−8/π) = 1.63 × 10−4 m ≈ 0.163 mm.Any thinner and the stress would cross the elastic limit and the wire would deform permanently.
⚡ JEE Trick Separate the two ideas: elongation uses Young's modulus, the safety radius uses the elastic-limit stress — they never mix. The real wire (r = 0.5 mm) is comfortably thicker than rmin = 0.163 mm, so it is operating safely; if the computed rmin ever exceeded the actual radius, the wire would already have yielded.
Answer: extension ≈ 0.382 mm; minimum safe radius ≈ 0.163 mm.
3One end of a steel wire is fixed to a ceiling and a load of 2.5 kg is attached to the free end of the wire. Another identical wire is attached to the bottom of the load and another load of 2.0 kg is attached to the lower end of this wire. Compute the longitudinal strain produced in both the wires, if the cross-sectional area of the wires is 10−4 m2. (Ysteel = 20 × 1010 N/m2)Strain in two stacked wiresElongation of Two Wires in Series Under Different Loads | Mechanical Properties of Solids 🟡 Similarupper wire 2.25 × 10−6, lower wire 1.0 × 10−6
Concept Two identical wires hang in series with loads at two levels. The key is that each wire carries only the weight below it: the upper wire supports both masses, the lower wire supports only the bottom mass. Strain = stress/Y = T/(AY); with the same A and Y for both wires, the strains are simply in the ratio of the tensions they carry. No elongation value is needed — strain is dimensionless.
Formula strain = T/(AY). Stress is T/A and Hooke's law gives strain = stress/Y, so strain is fixed once the tension in each wire is known.
Solution
Given two steel wires in series; loads 2.5 kg and 2.0 kg; A = 10⁻⁴ m²; Y = 20 × 10¹⁰ N/m²
Find the longitudinal strain in each wire
1. Tension in the upper wire (carries 2.5 kg + 2.0 kg): T1 = (2.5 + 2.0) × 10 = 45 N (g = 10 m s−2).
2. Tension in the lower wire (carries only 2.0 kg): T2 = 2.0 × 10 = 20 N.The lower wire never feels the upper load; cutting it would free only the 2.0 kg mass.
3. Common denominator AY = (10−4)(20 × 1010) = 2 × 107 N.
4. Upper strain: ε1 = T1/(AY) = 45 / (2 × 107) = 2.25 × 10−6.
5. Lower strain: ε2 = T2/(AY) = 20 / (2 × 107) = 1.0 × 10−6.Both are pure numbers — a 2.25 : 1 ratio, exactly the 45 : 20 tension ratio.
6. State the pair: the upper wire is strained 2.25 × 10−6, the lower wire 1.0 × 10−6.
⚡ JEE Trick In a stacked-wire problem, each segment supports only the load hanging beneath it — work from the bottom up. Because the wires are identical, strain just tracks tension, so you never need the area or length separately; the ratio of strains equals the ratio of weights supported.
Answer: upper wire strain = 2.25 × 10−6, lower wire strain = 1.0 × 10−6.
4A steel wire of length 7 m and cross section 1 mm2 is hung from a rigid support with a steel weight of volume 1000 c.c. hanging from the other end. Find the decrease in the length of the wire when the steel weight is completely immersed in water. (Ysteel = 2 × 1011 N/m2, density of water = 1 g/c.c)Buoyancy reduces elongationBuoyancy and Apparent Weight | Archimedes' Principle Physics | JEE NEET MHT CET 🟡 Similar≈ 0.343 mm (the wire shortens)
Concept In air the hanging weight stretches the wire by its full weight. Once the weight is lowered into water, buoyancy pushes up on it, so the tension in the wire drops by the buoyant force and the wire partly recovers — it shortens. The decrease in length is therefore the elongation that the buoyant force alone would have produced, ΔL = FbL/(AY). The weight's own density never enters; only the displaced water matters.
Formula Fb = Vρwg and ΔL = FbL/(AY). The change in tension equals the upthrust; feeding that into Young's modulus gives the change in length.
Solution
Given L = 7 m, A = 1 mm², weight volume 1000 cc fully immersed, Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m², ρ_water = 1 g/cc
Find the decrease in length of the wire on immersion
1. Convert the data: V = 1000 c.c. = 10−3 m3, L = 7 m, A = 1 mm2 = 10−6 m2, ρw = 1000 kg m−3.
2. Buoyant force (the reduction in tension): Fb = Vρwg = (10−3)(1000)(9.8) = 9.8 N.Upthrust equals the weight of displaced water, independent of what the hanging body is made of.
3. The wire's tension falls by Fb, so its length decreases by the elongation that Fb would cause.
4. Stiffness factor AY = (10−6)(2 × 1011) = 2 × 105 N.
5. Decrease in length: ΔL = FbL/(AY) = (9.8)(7)/(2 × 105) = 68.6 / (2 × 105) = 3.43 × 10−4 m.
6. Express the result: ΔL ≈ 0.343 mm, and the wire gets shorter because the load on it has eased.The wire does not go slack — it is still taut under the reduced weight — it simply contracts by this amount.
⚡ JEE Trick The change in length tracks the change in tension, which is just the buoyant force — you never need the mass or density of the suspended weight. Spotting that the answer uses only V, ρwater and the wire data turns a scary-looking problem into a two-line calculation.
Answer: the wire shortens by about 0.343 mm when the weight is immersed.

Since ΔL = FL/(AY), setting the two elongations equal forces a relation between the lengths and the moduli: the wire with the smaller Y must be correspondingly shorter to stretch the same amount. Given equal cross-sections; equal elongations; Y_Al = 7 × 10¹⁰ N/m², Y_brass = 9.1 × 10¹⁰ N/m²
Find the ratio of the lengths of the aluminium and brass wires

6A sphere of mass 1 kg is attached at one end of a steel wire of length 0.5 m and radius 1 mm. It is whirled in a vertical circle with an angular velocity of 120 r.p.m. What is the elongation of the wire when the sphere is at the lowest point in its path? (Ysteel = 20 × 1010 N/m2)Elongation at lowest point of vertical circleA 14.5 kg mass fastened to the end of a steel wire is whirled in a vertical circle 🟡 Similar≈ 0.0706 mm
Concept At the lowest point of a vertical circle the wire must do two jobs at once: hold up the weight and supply the centripetal force that keeps the sphere turning. So the tension is larger than the static weight, T = m(g + ω2L). That enlarged tension is what stretches the wire, ΔL = TL/(AY). The angular speed, given in r.p.m., must first be converted to radians per second.
Formula T = m(g + ω2L) at the lowest point, then ΔL = TL/(AY). Newton's second law radially: T − mg = mω2L, since centripetal acceleration is ω2L directed up at the bottom.
Solution
Given m = 1 kg, L = 0.5 m, r = 1 mm, 120 rpm, Y = 20 × 10¹⁰ N/m²
Find the elongation of the wire at the lowest point
1. Convert the spin: ω = 120 r.p.m. = 120 × 2π/60 = 4π = 12.57 rad s−1, so ω2 = 16π2 = 157.9.
2. Centripetal term: ω2L = 157.9 × 0.5 = 78.96 m s−2 (taking the radius as the wire length L = 0.5 m).
3. Tension at the lowest point: T = m(g + ω2L) = 1 × (9.8 + 78.96) = 88.76 N.The centripetal demand (79) dwarfs gravity (9.8), so the wire is stretched mainly by the rotation.
4. Cross-section: A = πr2 = π(10−3)2 = 3.142 × 10−6 m2, so AY = 3.142 × 10−6 × 2 × 1011 = 6.283 × 105 N.
5. Elongation: ΔL = TL/(AY) = (88.76)(0.5)/(6.283 × 105) = 44.38 / (6.283 × 105) = 7.06 × 10−5 m.
6. Convert: ΔL ≈ 0.0706 mm.
⚡ JEE Trick At the bottom of a vertical circle the tension is mg plus the centripetal mω2L, not just mg — forgetting the mω2L term is the standard error. Always convert r.p.m. to rad s−1 (multiply by 2π/60) before squaring; using 120 directly inflates ω2 by a factor of nearly 40.
Answer: the wire elongates by about 0.0706 mm at the lowest point.

1A uniform plank is resting over a smooth horizontal floor and is pulled by applying a horizontal force at its one end. Which of the following statements is correct? (a) Stress developed in the plank material is maximum at the end at which the force is applied and decreases linearly to zero at the other end. (b) A uniform tensile stress is developed in the plank material. (c) Since the plank is pulled at one end only, it starts to accelerate along the direction of the force; hence no stress is developed in the plank material. (d) None of these.Stress in an accelerating plankA uniform plank is resting over a smooth horizontal floor and is pulled at one end 🟢 ExactaStress is maximum at the pulled end and falls linearly to zero at the far end
Concept A plank on a frictionless floor, pulled at one end, accelerates as a whole with a = F/m. To give each bit of the plank that acceleration, the material behind a chosen cross-section must be dragged along by the internal force across that section. The further you are from the free end, the more mass lies ahead needing to be pulled, so the internal tension — and hence the stress — grows from zero at the free end to a maximum at the pulled end. It is definitely not uniform, and it is certainly not zero.
Formula T(x) = (m x / L) a = F x / L, measuring x from the free end. The section at x must accelerate the mass fraction (x/L)m sitting between it and the free end, so stress σ(x) = T(x)/A rises linearly with x.
Solution
1. Whole plank acceleration: a = F/m along the pull.
2. Cut the plank at distance x from the free end; the portion beyond (toward the free end) has mass (x/L)m.
3. That portion is pulled only by the tension T(x) at the cut, so T(x) = (x/L)m · a = F x/L.Newton's second law applied to the free-side piece; no friction means tension is the only horizontal force on it.
4. At the free end (x = 0): T = 0, so the stress there is zero.

Concept The two bars are glued along a plane tilted at angle θ to the bar's length, and are pulled apart by a force F along the length. The joint fails when the normal stress across the glued plane reaches σ0. Two geometric facts matter: the inclined contact area is larger than the bar's cross-section (S0/sinθ), and only the component of F perpendicular to the plane presses the bars together. Combining them gives the normal stress, which we cap at σ0.
Formula normal force Fn = F sinθ, contact area A = S0/sinθ, so normal stress σn = F sin2θ/S0. The plane makes angle θ with the axis, so its normal makes (90° − θ) with F; the perpendicular component of F is F sinθ.
Solution
1. The contact plane is inclined at θ to the length, so its area is A = S0/sinθ (larger than the cross-section S0).
2. Resolve F perpendicular to the plane: the normal makes angle (90° − θ) with the axis, so Fn = F cos(90° − θ) = F sinθ.Only this perpendicular component produces a normal (tensile) stress tending to peel the joint apart.
3. Normal stress on the joint: σn = Fn/A = (F sinθ)/(S0/sinθ).
4. Simplify: σn = F sin2θ/S0.
5. Failure condition: set σn = σ0, so F sin2θ/S0 = σ0.The adhesive yields exactly when the normal stress reaches its limit σ0.
6. Solve for the maximum force: Fmax = σ0S0/sin2θ.
⚡ JEE Trick An inclined joint brings two factors of sinθ: the area grows as 1/sinθ and the normal force component is F sinθ, so the normal stress carries sin2θ. Mishandling either — forgetting the enlarged area, or using cosθ for the component — is the usual error. As θ → 90° (a transverse cut) Fmax → σ0S0, the sensible limit.
Answer: Fmax = σ0S0/sin2θ.
