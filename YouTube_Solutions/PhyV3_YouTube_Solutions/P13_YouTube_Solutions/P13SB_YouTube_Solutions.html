1Two satellites A and B go around the earth in circular orbits at heights RA and RB respectively, from the surface of earth. Assume the earth to be a uniform sphere of radius RE. The ratio of the magnitudes of the velocity, vA/vB of the satellites is : (a) √(RB/RA) (b) (RA/RB)² (c) (RB + RE)/(RA + RE) (d) √[(RB + RE)/(RA + RE)] — (Level-7)orbital speed ratio of satellitesTwo artificial satellites A and B are at a distance `r_(A)` and `r_(B)` above the earth\'s surface.🟡 Similard√[(RB + RE)/(RA + RE)]
Concept For a satellite in a circular orbit, gravity alone supplies the centripetal force, so its speed depends only on the orbital radius measured from the earth's centre. The trap: RA and RB here are heights above the surface, so the orbit radii are (RE + RA) and (RE + RB), not RA and RB.
Formula mv²/r = GMm/r² ⇒ v = √(GM/r), with r = RE + h. The satellite mass m cancels, so v depends only on GM and r; a lower orbit is a faster orbit.
Solution
1. Orbit radius of A: rA = RE + RA; orbit radius of B: rB = RE + RB.Heights are measured from the surface, radii from the centre.
2. Force balance for A: m vA²/rA = GMm/rA² ⇒ vA = √(GM/rA) = √[GM/(RE + RA)].
3. Same for B: vB = √(GM/rB) = √[GM/(RE + RB)].
4. Divide: vA/vB = √(GM/rA)/√(GM/rB) = √(rB/rA).GM is common to both and cancels.
5. Put the radii back: vA/vB = √[(RB + RE)/(RA + RE)].
6. Sanity check: if A is lower (RA < RB) the ratio is > 1, i.e. the lower satellite is faster, as it must be.
 v = sqrt(GM/r),  r = R_E + h
 vA/vB = sqrt(rB/rA)
       = sqrt[(R_B + R_E)/(R_A + R_E)]
 -----------------------------------------
 option (d)                      <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Remember v ∝ r−1/2, so the ratio is the square root of the inverse radius ratio. Option (a) is the tempting distractor: it uses the heights as if they were radii and forgets RE. Option (c) forgets the square root (that would be the ratio of v², or of ω-like quantities), and (b) has the wrong power altogether.
Answer: (d) √[(RB + RE)/(RA + RE)].

3The gravitational field due to a mass distribution is given by E = K/x³ in x-direction. Taking the gravitational potential to be zero at infinity, its value at distance x is : (a) 2K/x² (b) K/(2x²) (c) K/x² (d) 3K/(2x²) — (Level-7)potential from given fieldThe gravitational field due to a mass distribution is given by `E=K/x^3` in X-direction. Talking...🟢 ExactbK/(2x²)
Concept Potential and field are linked by E = −dV/dx, so the potential is minus the integral of the field, taken from the zero-reference point (infinity) to the point x. The trap is a sign slip or forgetting the factor 2 that comes from integrating x−3.
Formula V(x) = −∫∞x E dx This follows from E = −dV/dx with V(∞) = 0 as the reference, exactly as the question specifies.
Solution
1. Given field (along +x): E(x) = K/x³; reference V(∞) = 0.
2. Write the definition: V(x) − V(∞) = −∫∞x (K/x³) dx.
3. Antiderivative: ∫ x−3 dx = x−2/(−2) = −1/(2x²).Power rule with n = −3 gives the factor 1/(n + 1) = −1/2.
4. Evaluate: V(x) = −K[−1/(2x²)]∞x = −K[−1/(2x²) − 0] = K/(2x²).
5. Check by differentiating back: −dV/dx = −d/dx[K/(2x²)] = −K·(−2)/(2x³) = K/x³ = E. ✓
6. Also V → 0 as x → ∞, so the reference condition is satisfied; the answer is K/(2x²), option (b).
7. Dimension check: E has units of N/kg = m/s², so K has units of m⁴/s²; then K/x² has units of m²/s² = J/kg, which is exactly the unit of gravitational potential. ✓All four options pass this test, so only the factor decides; that is why the integration must be done carefully.
 V(x) = -INT[inf..x] E dx
      = -INT[inf..x] K x^-3 dx
      = -K [ -1/(2x²) ] from inf to x
      =  K/(2x²)
 check: -dV/dx = K/x³ = E      ok
 -----------------------------------------
 V = K/(2x²)                     <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Fastest route: guess V = A/x², differentiate, and match −dV/dx = 2A/x³ = K/x³ ⇒ A = K/2. Option (c) K/x² is the classic slip of forgetting the 1/2 from the power rule; option (a) 2K/x² multiplies instead of divides by 2. Note the sign: the field given points along +x (away), so the potential here comes out positive, unlike the usual −GM/r for an attractive field.
Answer: (b) K/(2x²).
4The masses and radii of the earth and moon are M1, R1 and M2, R2 respectively. Their centres are at distance d apart. What is the minimum speed with which a particle of mass m should be projected from a point midway between the two centres so as to escape to infinity? — (Level-7)escape from midpoint of earth-moonThe masses and radii of the Earth and the Moon are `M_1, R_1 and M_2,R_2` respectively🟢 Exactv = 2√[G(M1 + M2)/d]
Concept To escape to infinity the particle needs total mechanical energy ≥ 0. At the midpoint it sits in the potential wells of both the earth and the moon, so both potential energies must be paid for. The radii R1, R2 are red herrings: the particle starts outside both bodies, so only the centre distances (d/2 each) matter.
Formula (1/2)mv² − GM1m/(d/2) − GM2m/(d/2) = 0 Minimum speed means the particle just reaches infinity with zero KE, where the PE is also zero.
Solution
1. Distance of the midpoint from each centre = d/2.
2. PE at the midpoint (potentials add as scalars): U = −GM1m/(d/2) − GM2m/(d/2) = −2Gm(M1 + M2)/d.Potential energy is a scalar, so no vector cancellation, unlike the forces, which partly cancel at the midpoint.
3. Energy at infinity (just escaping): KE = 0, PE = 0 ⇒ Efinal = 0.
4. Conservation: (1/2)mv² − 2Gm(M1 + M2)/d = 0.
5. Solve: v² = 4G(M1 + M2)/d ⇒ v = 2√[G(M1 + M2)/d].
6. The answer is independent of m, R1 and R2, and of the launch direction (energy is a scalar), as long as the path does not hit either body.
 r1 = r2 = d/2
 U  = -GM1m/(d/2) - GM2m/(d/2) = -2Gm(M1+M2)/d
 (1/2)mv² + U = 0
 v² = 4G(M1+M2)/d
 -----------------------------------------
 v = 2 sqrt[G(M1+M2)/d]          <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Escape speed always comes from v = √(−2U/m) at the launch point; just add up every body's −GM/r there. Trap 1: adding forces (they partly cancel at the midpoint) instead of potentials. Trap 2: using the surface radii R1, R2, which only matter for a launch from a surface. Trap 3: dropping the factor 2 from 1/(d/2) and writing √[2G(M1 + M2)/d].
Answer: vmin = 2√[G(M1 + M2)/d].

Concept A rod is not a point mass, so slice it into elements dm = (M/L)dx, each acting like a point mass at distance r = x0 − x from P. On the axis every element pulls P in the same direction (toward the rod, along −x), so the magnitudes simply add by integration. The trap is using GM/x0², which is only the far-field limit.
Formula dE = G dm/r² = G(M/L)dx/(x0 − x)², integrated from x = −L/2 to +L/2. Uniform rod ⇒ constant linear density λ = M/L; all contributions are collinear.
Solution
1. Element at position x, width dx: dm = (M/L)dx; its distance from P is r = x0 − x.
2. Its field at P: dE = G(M/L)dx/(x0 − x)², directed toward −x.
3. Integrate: E = (GM/L)∫−L/2L/2 dx/(x0 − x)² = (GM/L)[1/(x0 − x)]−L/2L/2.d/dx[1/(x0 − x)] = 1/(x0 − x)², so the antiderivative is 1/(x0 − x).
4. Limits: E = (GM/L)[1/(x0 − L/2) − 1/(x0 + L/2)] = (GM/L)·L/(x0² − L²/4).
5. Simplify: E = GM/(x0² − L²/4) = 4GM/(4x0² − L²), pointing toward the rod (−x direction).
6. Check: for x0 ≫ L, E → GM/x0², the point-mass result. ✓ As x0 → L/2 the field blows up, as expected at the end of a line mass.
 dm = (M/L)dx ,  r = x0 - x
 E  = (GM/L) INT[-L/2..L/2] dx/(x0-x)²
    = (GM/L)[1/(x0-L/2) - 1/(x0+L/2)]
    = (GM/L) . L/(x0² - L²/4)
 -----------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick Axial field of a rod = GM/(d1d2), where d1, d2 are distances from P to the near and far ends: (x0 − L/2)(x0 + L/2) = x0² − L²/4. One line, no integral. Trap: taking the field from the centre (GM/x0²) underestimates it, since the near half pulls harder than the far half.
Answer: E = GM/(x0² − L²/4), directed toward the rod (along −x).
6The rotation of the earth having radius R about its axis speeds upto a value such that a man at latitude angle 60° feels weightless. The duration of the day in such case will be : (a) π√(R/g) (b) 8π√(g/R) (c) 8π√(R/g) (d) 4π√(g/R) — (Level-7)weightlessness at latitude due to rotationThe rotation of the earth having radius R about its axis speeds up to a value such that a man at lat🟢 Exactaπ√(R/g)
Concept Because of earth's spin, the effective gravity at latitude λ is reduced: g' = g − ω²R cos²λ. A man "feels weightless" when g' = 0. The trap is to use the equator formula ω²R = g (λ = 0) or to put sin instead of cos for a latitude angle.
Formula g' = g − ω²R cos²λ = 0 ⇒ ω = √[g/(R cos²λ)], then T = 2π/ω. At latitude λ the circle of rotation has radius R cosλ, so the centrifugal term is ω²R cosλ, and its component along the radius is ω²R cos²λ.
Solution
1. Latitude λ = 60° ⇒ cosλ = 1/2, cos²λ = 1/4.
2. Weightless condition: g − ω²R cos²60° = 0 ⇒ g = ω²R/4.
3. Solve: ω² = 4g/R ⇒ ω = 2√(g/R).The required spin is twice the equatorial one, because only a quarter of ω²R acts radially at 60°.
4. Length of day: T = 2π/ω = 2π/[2√(g/R)] = π√(R/g).
5. Numbers for feel: √(R/g) = √(6.4×10⁶/9.8) ≈ 808 s ⇒ T ≈ π·808 ≈ 2540 s ≈ 42 minutes.
6. Compare the equator case: Teq = 2π√(R/g) ≈ 84 min; at 60° the day must be half as long, consistent with ω doubling.
7. Fine print: the centrifugal force ω²R cosλ is perpendicular to the axis, so at 60° it also has a tangential part ω²R cosλ sinλ that the radial-component formula ignores. The book (like the standard JEE treatment) takes weightlessness to mean the radial g' = 0, which gives the result above.This approximation is what every option set on this topic assumes.
 g' = g - w²R cos²(lat) = 0
 cos²60 = 1/4  =>  w² = 4g/R
 w = 2 sqrt(g/R)
 T = 2pi/w = 2pi/[2 sqrt(g/R)] = pi sqrt(R/g)
 -----------------------------------------
 T = pi sqrt(R/g) ~ 42 min       <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Check dimensions first: the period must go as √(R/g), which kills (b) and (d) instantly (√(g/R) is a frequency). Between (a) and (c): ω must be larger than the equatorial √(g/R), so T must be smaller than 2π√(R/g); only π√(R/g) fits. Option (c) 8π√(R/g) would make the day longer, a common slip from multiplying by 4 instead of dividing.
Answer: (a) π√(R/g).
