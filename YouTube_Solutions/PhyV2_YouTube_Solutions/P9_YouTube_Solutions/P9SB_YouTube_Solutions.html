1A simple pendulum of length 1 m has a wooden bob of mass 1 kg. It is struck by a bullet of mass 10−2 kg moving with a speed of 2×102 m s−1. The bullet gets embedded into the bob. The height to which the bob rises before swinging back is (use g = 10 m s−2) — (JEE Main 2024, Level-6)Ballistic pendulum, momentum→energyA simple pendulum of length 1m has a wooden bob of mass 1kg. It is struck by a bullet of mass 10...🟢 Exactb0.20 m
Concept This is a two-stage ballistic-pendulum problem, and the two stages use different conservation laws. Stage 1 — the bullet embedding in the bob — is a perfectly inelastic collision: it happens in a tiny fraction of a second, so the string's tension/gravity impulse during that instant is negligible and momentum alone is conserved (kinetic energy is NOT — that lost energy is what deforms the wood). Stage 2 — the combined bob+bullet swinging up — is a smooth, lossless swing: here energy alone is conserved.
Formula Stage 1: m u = (m+M) v — m, M in kg, u, v in m/s. Stage 2: ½(m+M)v² = (m+M)gh ⇒ h = v²/(2g) — h in m, g in m/s². Left side of stage 1 is kg·m/s on both sides ✓; in stage 2 the (m+M) cancels, so the rise h never needs the actual combined mass.
Solution
1. Data: bob M = 1 kg, bullet m = 0.01 kg, bullet speed u = 200 m/s, g = 10 m/s², string length L = 1 m (not needed directly).
2. Apply momentum conservation across the collision instant:
  m u = (m+M) v ⇒ (0.01)(200) = (1.01) v
3. Solve for v: v = 2/1.01 = 1.980 m/s.Note the combined mass is 1.01 kg, not 1 kg — dropping the bullet's own mass from the sum is the single most common slip in this problem.
4. Switch laws for stage 2: the swing afterwards is frictionless, so mechanical energy is conserved — all of the post-collision KE turns into gravitational PE at the top of the swing:
  h = v²/(2g) = (1.980)²/20 = 3.92/20
5. h = 0.196 m ≈ 0.20 m.Rounding 1.980 to 1.98 or even 2 m/s barely moves the third decimal — the answer is robust to that rounding.
6. Sanity check: h = 0.196 m is well under the string length L = 1 m, so the bob never approaches the horizontal or loops over the support — a physically consistent small swing.If h had come out larger than L the problem's premise (bob "swinging back", not going over the top) would break down.
7. Match to the options: 0.196 m rounds to 0.20 m, option (b).
BEFORE (stage 1)              AFTER (stage 2)
  u=200 m/s                    v=1.98 m/s swings up
  ●→ bullet  ○ bob(1kg)             ○●  (combined, 1.01 kg)
  ──────────┬──────                  ╲
            │ string 1m               ╲ h = v²/2g
            ▾                          ╲___
  m u = (m+M) v  →  v = 1.98 m/s     h = 0.196 m ≈ 0.20 m
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a collision is followed by a swing/rise/further motion, split it into momentum for the bang, energy for the swing — never conserve energy through the collision itself (that's exactly what a "perfectly inelastic" collision forbids) and never conserve momentum through the smooth glide afterward (gravity's impulse is no longer negligible over that longer time). Trap: computing (m+M) as just M = 1 kg; trap: plugging u = 200 m/s directly into h = u²/2g and skipping the collision entirely.
Answer: (b) 0.20 m (bob+bullet common speed 1.98 m/s after impact).
2A stationary particle breaks into two parts of masses mA and mB which move with velocities vA and vB respectively. The ratio of their kinetic energies (KB:KA) is: (a) vB:vA (b) mB:mA (c) mBvB:mAvA (d) 1:1 — (JEE Main 2024, Level-4)Explosion, momentum vs KE ratioA stationary particle breaks into two parts of masses mA and mB which move with velocities vA and vB🟢 ExactavB:vA
Concept A particle at rest has zero momentum, so when it explodes into two pieces the pieces must carry equal and opposite momenta — that single fact is the whole problem. Everything else is algebra: express both kinetic energies in terms of that common momentum magnitude and see what ratio survives. No masses or velocities need be assumed numerically; this is a pure-symbol manipulation.
Formula Momentum conservation (initial p = 0): mAvA = mBvB (magnitudes; directions opposite). Kinetic energy: K = ½mv² — m in kg, v in m/s, K in J. kg·(m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J on both sides ✓; the momentum equation is kg·m/s = kg·m/s ✓.
Solution
1. Write momentum conservation for the explosion: total momentum before = 0, so the two fragment momenta are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction:
  mAvA = mBvB
2. From this, isolate the mass ratio: mA/mB = vB/vA.This single relation is what lets every other ratio in the options collapse to a two-variable expression.
3. Write both kinetic energies: KA = ½mAvA², KB = ½mBvB².
4. Take the ratio: KB/KA = (mBvB²)/(mAvA²) = (mB/mA)·(vB/vA)².
5. Substitute mB/mA = vA/vB (the reciprocal of step 2's relation):
  KB/KA = (vA/vB)·(vB/vA)² = vB/vA.
6. So KB:KA = vB:vA — option (a).Sanity check with numbers: let mA=1, mB=2. Momentum equality forces vA=2, vB=1 (for equal momenta of 2). Then KA=2 J, KB=1 J ⇒ KB:KA=1:2, and vB:vA=1:2 — matches.
7. Reject (b): the same numeric check gives mB:mA = 2:1, the reciprocal of the true ratio. Reject (c): mBvB:mAvA = 1:1 always (equal momenta), which is really option (d) in disguise, not a KE ratio.
before:  ○ at rest, p = 0

after:   ◂── mB, vB          mA, vA ──▸
           (fragment B)      (fragment A)

  mA vA = mB vB   (equal & opposite momenta)
  KA = ½mA vA² ,  KB = ½mB vB²
  KB/KA = (mB/mA)(vB/vA)² = (vA/vB)(vB/vA)² = vB/vA
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an object at rest breaks into two pieces, immediately write "equal and opposite momenta" — it is the single conserved quantity and every KE-ratio question reduces to one line of substitution from it. Trap: option (b) is the mass ratio inverted, the answer you get if you forget to square the velocity ratio in step 4. Trap: option (c) is just a restatement of momentum equality (=1:1), not a KE ratio — pattern-matching on "looks like the answer" costs the mark here.
Answer: (a) vB:vA — equivalently KB:KA = mA:mB.
3A spherical body of mass 100 g is dropped from a height of 10 m from the ground. After hitting the ground, the body rebounds to a height of 5 m. The impulse of force imparted by the ground to the body is given by: (given g = 9.8 m s−2) — (JEE Main 2024, Level-4)Impulse = Δmomentum, free fall + rebound12 JEE Main 2024, Physics 30 Jan Morning | A spherical body of mass 100 g is dropped from a height🟢 Exactd2.39 kg m s−1
Concept Impulse equals the change in momentum, and here the tricky part is direction: the body arrives moving downward and leaves moving upward, so the two velocities must be added in magnitude (not subtracted) when you compute |Δp| along a single axis with a sign flip. Free fall and the rebound rise are two independent kinematics problems, each solved with v = √(2gh).
Formula Fall: v₁ = √(2gh₁). Rebound rise: v₂ = √(2gh₂) — h in m, g in m/s², v in m/s. Impulse: J = |Δp| = m(v₁ + v₂) (taking down as reference; the rebound velocity is opposite in sign, so subtracting a negative becomes addition) — m in kg, J in kg·m/s (= N·s). kg·(m/s) is the correct unit for both impulse and momentum, and √(m²/s²·) = m/s checks under the square root too.
Solution
1. Data: m = 100 g = 0.1 kg, h₁ = 10 m (fall), h₂ = 5 m (rebound rise), g = 9.8 m/s².
2. Speed just before impact (free fall through 10 m):
  v₁ = √(2 × 9.8 × 10) = √196 = 14 m/s (downward).
3. Speed just after impact (enough to rise 5 m):
  v₂ = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = √98 = 9.899 m/s (upward).v₂ < v₁ as it must be — the collision with the ground is inelastic (some KE is lost as heat/sound), so the rebound height is always less than the drop height.
4. Take downward as positive. Before: p₁ = +m v₁ = +0.1×14 = +1.4 kg·m/s. After: p₂ = −m v₂ = −0.1×9.899 = −0.9899 kg·m/s.The minus sign is not optional — missing it is exactly what turns 2.39 into the wrong answer 0.41.
5. Impulse (on the body, by the ground) = Δp = p₂ − p₁ = −0.9899 − 1.4 = −2.3899 kg·m/s; magnitude 2.39 kg·m/s.
6. Equivalently, and faster: J = m(v₁+v₂) = 0.1×(14+9.899) = 0.1×23.899 = 2.39 kg·m/s — same result via direct addition of the two speeds.
7. Match to the options: 2.39 kg m s⁻¹ is option (d), not (c) 23.9 (a ×10 slip from forgetting the 100 g → 0.1 kg conversion).
   before impact          after impact
     ● v1=14 m/s ↓          ● v2=9.9 m/s ↑
     │ h1=10m                │ h2=5m (rebound, lower)
     │                       │
═════▾═══════════════════════●═══════════ ground

  J = m(v1+v2) = 0.1(14+9.899) = 2.39 kg·m/s
⚡ JEE Trick For "drop-and-bounce" impulse questions, skip the sign bookkeeping entirely and just add the fall-speed and rebound-speed magnitudes, then multiply by mass — the reversal of direction is what turns subtraction into addition automatically. Trap: computing √(2gh) for both heights but then subtracting the two speeds (14−9.9=4.1, ×0.1=0.41 kg·m/s) — that would be correct only if the body bounced back down, not up. Trap: forgetting the g/kg unit conversions from the printed 100 g.
Answer: (d) 2.39 kg m s⁻¹ (impact speed 14 m/s, rebound speed 9.9 m/s).
4A body of mass 1000 kg is moving horizontally with a velocity 6 m s−1. If 200 kg extra mass is added, the final velocity (in m s−1) is: (a) 6 (b) 2 (c) 3 (d) 5 — (JEE Main 2024, Level-3)Perfectly inelastic mass additionA body of mass 1000 kg is moving horizontally with a velocity 6 m/s. If 200 kg extra mass is added,🟢 Exactd5
Concept "Extra mass is added" to a moving body, with no mention of it being given a push, means the added 200 kg has zero initial velocity and sticks to the body — this is a perfectly inelastic collision in disguise, just phrased as "mass addition" instead of "two bodies colliding". Momentum, not velocity, is what's conserved when mass changes hands.
Formula m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁+m₂)v — m in kg, u, v in m/s. Here u₂ = 0 (the extra mass is at rest before joining), so this reduces to m₁u₁ = (m₁+m₂)v. kg·m/s on both sides ✓; note this is NOT "velocity is conserved" — that common misreading gives the trap answer (a) 6.
Solution
1. Data: original mass m₁ = 1000 kg, original velocity u₁ = 6 m/s, added mass m₂ = 200 kg (at rest, u₂ = 0).
2. Initial momentum of the system = m₁u₁ + m₂(0) = 1000×6 + 0 = 6000 kg·m/s.The 200 kg contributes zero momentum before joining — it was not moving.
3. Total mass after the addition: M = m₁+m₂ = 1000+200 = 1200 kg.
4. Apply momentum conservation: M v = 6000 ⇒ v = 6000/1200
5. v = 5 m/s.The body slows down because the same momentum must now move a larger mass — exactly analogous to a bullet embedding in a block.
6. Cross-check with a simpler ratio form: v = u₁ × m₁/(m₁+m₂) = 6 × (1000/1200) = 6 × 5/6 = 5 m/s ✓.
7. Reject (a) 6: that assumes velocity stays unchanged, ignoring that momentum (not speed) is what's conserved when mass is added at rest.
  BEFORE                        AFTER
  ┌──────────┐  6 m/s          ┌──────────────┐  v
  │ 1000 kg  │ ──────▸    +    │   1200 kg    │ ──▸
  └──────────┘                 └──────────────┘
     +200 kg (at rest, u=0)

  1000(6) + 200(0) = 1200 v  ⇒  v = 6000/1200 = 5 m/s
⚡ JEE Trick Read "extra mass added" as "perfectly inelastic collision with a stationary mass" every time — then it's the ordinary m₁u₁=(m₁+m₂)v formula, nothing new. Trap: option (a) 6, from assuming velocity is unaffected by adding mass (true only if the added mass matched the body's velocity, which the question never says). Quick ratio shortcut: v = u × (old mass)/(new mass) always works for this exact scenario.
Answer: (d) 5 m/s.
5An artillery piece of mass M1 fires a shell of mass M2 horizontally. Instantaneously after the firing, the ratio of kinetic energy of the artillery and that of the shell is: (a) M1/(M1+M2) (b) M2/M1 (c) M2/(M1+M2) (d) M1/M2 — (JEE Main 2024, Level-3)Recoil, KE ratio from momentumAn artillery piece of mass M1 fires a shell of mass M2 horizontally. Instantaneously after the🟢 ExactbM2/M1
Concept This is the recoil-gun version of the "explosion from rest" family (same skeleton as Q2 in this Strong Box): the artillery + shell system starts at rest, so the recoiling gun and the fired shell carry equal and opposite momenta. Whichever body has the smaller mass gets the larger share of the kinetic energy for a fixed common momentum — a fact worth memorising outright.
Formula Momentum conservation: M₁v₁ = M₂v₂ (magnitudes). Kinetic energy in terms of the common momentum p: K = p²/(2M) — p in kg·m/s, M in kg, K in J. p²/M has units (kg·m/s)²/kg = kg·m²/s² = J ✓ — this form is the fast way to compare two KEs sharing the same p.
Solution
1. Before firing, the artillery+shell system is at rest: total momentum = 0.
2. After firing, momentum conservation demands the artillery's recoil momentum exactly cancels the shell's forward momentum:
  M₁v₁ = M₂v₂ = p (same magnitude for both).
3. Write each kinetic energy using K = p²/(2M) (valid since both share the same |p|):
  K_artillery = p²/(2M₁),   K_shell = p²/(2M₂).
4. Take the ratio — the p² and the 2 cancel:
  K_artillery/K_shell = (p²/2M₁)/(p²/2M₂) = M₂/M₁.
5. So the ratio asked for, (artillery : shell), is M₂/M₁ — option (b).Physically sensible: the light shell (small M₂) is fired at high speed and carries most of the energy, while the heavy artillery (large M₁) barely recoils and carries little — consistent with M₂/M₁ being the artillery's small share when M₁≫M₂.
6. Sanity-check with numbers: M₁=100 kg, M₂=1 kg. If v₂=100 m/s then p=100, v₁=1 m/s. K_artillery=½(100)(1)²=50 J, K_shell=½(1)(100)²=5000 J. Ratio = 50/5000 = 1/100 = M₂/M₁ = 1/100 ✓.
7. Reject (d) M₁/M₂ — that is the reciprocal, i.e. K_shell/K_artillery, the easy sign/order slip in this question.
  before:  [artillery + shell]  at rest,  p = 0

  after:   M1 (artillery) ◂── p        p ──▸ M2 (shell, fast)
           v1 = p/M1                  v2 = p/M2

  K = p²/2M  (same p for both)
  K_artillery / K_shell = (p²/2M1)/(p²/2M2) = M2/M1
⚡ JEE Trick For any "body at rest splits into two" or "gun fires bullet" KE-ratio question, jump straight to K ∝ 1/M (for equal momenta) — the answer is always (other body's mass)/(this body's mass), never the same-order ratio. Trap: option (d) M₁/M₂ is the shell-to-artillery ratio, exactly upside down from what's asked. Trap: option (a)/(c) with a (M₁+M₂) sum in the denominator look like reduced-mass formulas but don't apply here — there's no reduced mass in a two-body recoil-from-rest problem.
Answer: (b) M₂/M₁ (light shell carries most of the kinetic energy).
6A ball of mass 200 g rests on a vertical post of height 20 m. A bullet of mass 10 g, travelling in horizontal direction, hits the centre of the ball. After collision both travel independently. The ball hits the ground at a distance 30 m and the bullet at a distance of 120 m from the foot of the post. The value of initial velocity of the bullet will be (if g = 10 m s−2): (a) 120 ms−1 (b) 60 ms−1 (c) 400 ms−1 (d) 360 ms−1 — (JEE Main 2023, Level-6)Collision + projectile motion (2 bodies)A ball of mass 200 g rests on a vertical post of height 20 m A bullet of mass || jee mains 2024🟢 Exactd360 ms−1
Concept A three-stage problem stitched together: (1) both fragments undergo identical vertical free fall from the same height, so they share the same fall time; (2) each fragment's horizontal range, divided by that shared time, gives its horizontal speed right after collision; (3) those two post-collision speeds feed into a single horizontal momentum conservation equation to back out the bullet's original speed. The ball and bullet never interact with each other again after the strike, so the projectile-motion part is just two independent copies of the same drop.
Formula Fall time: h = ½gt² (same for both, same h). Horizontal speed after collision: v = R/t (R = range, in m). Momentum conservation (all horizontal, before/after the brief collision): m_bullet·u = m_bullet·v_bullet + m_ball·v_ball — m in kg, v in m/s. m/s = m/s on both sides of v=R/t ✓; kg·m/s balances in the momentum equation ✓.
Solution
1. Data: m_ball = 0.2 kg, m_bullet = 0.01 kg, post height h = 20 m, ball's range R_ball = 30 m, bullet's range R_bullet = 120 m, g = 10 m/s².
2. Fall time (same for both since both leave the post at the same height, same instant): h = ½gt² ⇒ 20 = ½(10)t² ⇒ t² = 4 ⇒ t = 2 s.
3. Ball's horizontal speed right after collision: v_ball = R_ball/t = 30/2 = 15 m/s.
4. Bullet's horizontal speed right after collision: v_bullet = R_bullet/t = 120/2 = 60 m/s.The bullet keeps moving much faster than the ball post-impact — physically sensible since a light fast bullet transfers only some of its momentum before continuing on.
5. Apply horizontal momentum conservation across the collision (vertical motion doesn't start until after, and gravity's impulse during the brief strike is negligible):
  m_bullet·u = m_bullet·v_bullet + m_ball·v_ball = (0.01)(60) + (0.2)(15)
6. Evaluate the right side: 0.6 + 3.0 = 3.6. So 0.01·u = 3.6 ⇒ u = 3.6/0.01
7. u = 360 m/s — option (d).This is much larger than either post-collision speed, as expected: the bullet had to be moving very fast to hand off 3 kg·m/s of momentum to a ball 20× its own mass while itself only slowing from 360 to 60 m/s.
         V m/s ●bullet(10g)→●ball(200g) rests on post, h=20m
                              │
        both fall together in time t = 2s (same h)
                              │
   ══════0══════════30═══════════════════120═══▸ (m)
            ball range 30m         bullet range 120m

   t=2s: v_ball=30/2=15, v_bullet=120/2=60
   0.01u = 0.01(60)+0.2(15) = 3.6  ⇒  u = 360 m/s

Trap: mixing up which range belongs to which mass (bullet range should exceed ball range since the bullet keeps most of its speed). Trap: forgetting to convert grams to kilograms, which shifts the answer by a factor of 1000.

7100 balls each of mass m moving with speed v simultaneously strike a wall normally and reflected back with same speed, in time t s. The total force exerted by the balls on the wall is: (a) 100mv/t (b) 200mv/t (c) 200mvt (d) mv/100t — (JEE Main 2023, Level-3)Impulse from repeated elastic bounces100 balls each of mass m moving with speed v simultaneously strike a wall normally and reflected.🟢 Exactb200mv/t
Concept A ball that bounces straight back off a wall with unchanged speed reverses the sign of its velocity — so its momentum change is double the incoming momentum, not zero (a very common wrong instinct, since speed is "the same"). Scale that single-ball impulse up to 100 balls, then use average force = total impulse ÷ time.
Formula Per-ball momentum change: Δp = m(−v) − m(v) = −2mv, magnitude 2mv — m in kg, v in m/s, Δp in kg·m/s. Total impulse for N balls: J = N(2mv). Average force: F = J/t — t in s, F in N. kg·m/s ÷ s = kg·m/s² = N ✓ — dimensionally this is an ordinary Newton's-second-law force.
Solution
1. Take the direction toward the wall as positive. One ball's velocity before impact = +v, after impact (reflected straight back) = −v.
2. Momentum change of one ball: Δp = m(−v) − m(v) = −2mv.The magnitude is 2mv, not 0 — "same speed" describes magnitude only, and the direction has fully reversed.
3. By Newton's third law, the ball exerts an equal and opposite impulse on the wall: magnitude 2mv per ball, directed into the wall.
4. All 100 balls strike simultaneously, so their impulses on the wall add directly: total impulse J = 100 × 2mv = 200mv."Simultaneously" is what lets you simply multiply by 100 instead of integrating a stream of arrivals spread over t.
5. This total impulse is delivered over the stated time t (the duration of the strike-and-rebound event): F_avg = J/t = 200mv/t.
6. Check units: [kg][m/s]/[s] = kg·m/s² = N ✓, and the answer scales correctly — doubling the number of balls or the speed should double the force, and it does.
7. Match to the options: 200mv/t is option (b).
   before:  ●● ●● ... (100 balls) ──▸ v      ║ wall
   after :  ●● ●● ... (100 balls) ◂── v      ║

   one ball:  Δp = m(-v) - m(v) = -2mv
   100 balls: J  = 100 (2mv) = 200mv
   force:     F  = J/t = 200mv/t
⚡ JEE Trick Any "bounces back with the same speed" line is a flag to double the momentum, never treat it as zero change — memorise Δp = 2mv per elastic bounce as a standing fact, then just multiply by however many particles/balls/molecules are involved (this is literally the kinetic theory of gas pressure derivation in miniature). Trap: option (a) 100mv/t comes from forgetting the factor of 2 on reversal; option (d) inverts the ball count.
Answer: (b) 200mv/t.
8A bullet of 10 g leaves the barrel of gun with a velocity of 600 m s−1. If the barrel of gun is 50 cm long and mass of gun is 3 kg, then value of impulse supplied to the gun will be: (a) 6 N s (b) 3 N s (c) 36 N s (d) 12 N s — (JEE Main 2023, Level-2)Impulse = bullet's momentum (Newton's 3rd law)A bullet 10 g leaves the barrel of gun with a velocity of 600 m/s. If the barrel of gun is 50 cm.🟢 Exacta6 N s
Concept The barrel length and the gun's mass are decoys — they'd matter if the question asked for the gun's recoil acceleration inside the barrel or its recoil velocity, but the question only asks for impulse. By Newton's third law, whatever impulse the exploding propellant delivers to the bullet, an exactly equal and opposite impulse is delivered to the gun — no extra data is needed once you recognise this.
Formula Impulse–momentum theorem for the bullet: J_bullet = Δp_bullet = m_bullet(v − 0) — m in kg, v in m/s, J in N·s = kg·m/s. Newton's third law: J_gun = −J_bullet, so |J_gun| = m_bullet·v. kg × m/s = kg·m/s = N·s on both sides ✓ — impulse and momentum share the same unit, which is exactly why this shortcut works.
Solution
1. Data: m_bullet = 10 g = 0.01 kg, muzzle velocity v = 600 m/s (bullet starts from rest inside the barrel). Barrel length 50 cm and gun mass 3 kg are extra, unused numbers.
2. Impulse delivered to the bullet by the expanding gas = change in the bullet's momentum:
  J_bullet = m_bullet(v − 0) = 0.01 × 600
3. J_bullet = 6 kg·m/s = 6 N·s.
4. By Newton's third law, the reaction impulse felt by the gun is equal in magnitude, opposite in direction: |J_gun| = 6 N·s.This is exactly why guns recoil — the gun receives the same total "kick" as the bullet, just spread over the same firing duration.
5. Cross-check via the gun's own recoil: momentum conservation gives m_gun·v_recoil = 6 ⇒ v_recoil = 6/3 = 2 m/s; impulse on gun = m_gun·v_recoil = 3×2 = 6 N·s — same answer, confirming the barrel length was never needed.
6. Note why the barrel length is irrelevant here: it would let you compute the bullet's acceleration inside the barrel (v² = 2as) or the firing time, but impulse only needs the momentum change, not how it was built up.
7. Match: 6 N s is option (a).
   gun (3 kg)         barrel 0.5m         bullet (0.01 kg)
   ┌────────┐═══════════════════▸ leaves at v = 600 m/s
   └────────┘
   ◂── recoil (extra info, not needed for J)

   J_bullet = m v = 0.01(600) = 6 N·s
   J_gun    = −J_bullet  (Newton's 3rd law)  ⇒  |J_gun| = 6 N·s
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever a question gives you "impulse on the gun/wall/support" right after describing a projectile's launch, skip straight to the projectile's own momentum change — action-reaction guarantees they're equal in magnitude. Trap: option (c) 36 N s comes from multiplying 6 N·s by the barrel length in cm (a meaningless unit mashup); trap: dragging in the gun's mass to compute a recoil velocity first when it's not required at all.
Answer: (a) 6 N s.

  v0=3 ──▸ ●1kg   ○5kg(rest)     ◂──2  ●1kg    ○5kg ──▸ 1 m/s

  p_total = 1(3) = 3 kg·m/s        (a) TRUE
  p_5kg   = 5(1) = 5 kg·m/s        (b) FALSE (printed 4)
  v_cm = 3/6 = 0.5;  KEcm=½(6)(0.5)²=0.75J   (c) TRUE
  KE_total = 4.5 J (elastic, conserved)      (d) FALSE (printed 4)
⚡ JEE Trick In any elastic-collision "which statement is true" question, first pin down the unknown initial speed using v₁' = [(m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂)]v₀, then treat momentum conservation and KE conservation as two independent checks you can cross-verify against each other — if your v₂' doesn't make total momentum come out consistent with (a), you've made an arithmetic slip. Trap: (b) and (d) are both "off by a little" numbers (5 vs 4, 4.5 vs 4) — exactly the kind of near-miss distractor designed to catch a rushed half-computation.
Answer: (a), (c) are correct; (b) and (d) are false (correct values are 5 kg·m/s and 4.5 J respectively).
