★ Core ideaElasticity is one statement — stress = modulus × strain — written for three kinds of deformation. Change of length uses Young's modulus Y, change of volume uses bulk modulus K, change of shape uses the rigidity modulus η. Pick the deformation first, then the matching modulus, and almost every numerical collapses to ΔL = FL/AY or ΔV = PV/K or φ = F/Aη.
💡 Method that never failsFor any loaded wire write ΔL = FL/(AY) and read off the proportionalities: at fixed material and load, ΔL ∝ L/A ∝ L/r². Ratio questions (two wires, composite wires, same load or same extension) are then one line — cancel Y, F and π and keep only the lengths and radii.
⚠ Three traps that cost marks(a) Bulk modulus and Poisson's ratio carry a minus sign in their definitions — drop it and K or σ comes out negative. (b) Work done in stretching is (1/2)F·ΔL, not F·ΔL — the force rises from zero. (c) Shear strain is an angle (radian), tensile strain is a length ratio — never equate φ with ΔL/L of the same body.
L-1

Elastic Behaviour of Solids, Stress and Strain

Elastic behaviour and basic definitions

  • When a solid is deformed, its atoms shift from their regular lattice sites and the interatomic distances change. On removing the force, the interatomic forces pull the atoms back, restoring the original shape — this is elastic behaviour (model: balls joined by springs).
  • Deforming force — a force that changes the relative positions of the molecules (length, volume or shape) without moving the body as a whole. For a body in equilibrium under two equal opposite forces, the force on any section is T = F.
  • Elasticity: the property of regaining original shape and size after the deforming force is removed. A perfectly elastic body recovers completely and instantly (an idealisation; rubber and some metals come close).
  • Plasticity: permanent deformation that is not recovered. A perfectly plastic body retains its new shape indefinitely (clay-like idealisation). Real materials show a mixture of both.
  • Cause of elasticity: interatomic / intermolecular bonds act as the restoring force. Stronger bonds, regular crystal structure, higher particle density and higher purity tend to raise elasticity; temperature and strain rate also affect it.
  • For a body of mass m pulled by force F at one end, the tension at a section a distance x from the free end is T = (m/L)x · (F/m) = Fx/L: T = 0 at the free end (x = 0), T = F/2 at the middle, T = F at the pulled end.

    • Stress and its types

    • Stress σ = restoring force per unit area of the deformed cross-section, σ = F/A (more precisely the limit ΔF/ΔA). SI unit N/m² (Pa). Numerically it equals the applied force per unit area in equilibrium.
    • Normal stress: force ⊥ to the surface. Tensile (elongates, pulls the two parts apart) and compressive (shortens, pushes them together).
    • Shearing / tangential stress: force parallel to the surface, τ = Fᵗ/A — changes shape, not volume.
    • Volumetric stress: uniform force from all directions (pressure), P = F/A — changes volume, not shape.
    Stressσ = F/A  ·  normal σ = Fₙ/A  ·  shear τ = Fᵗ/A  ·  volume P = F/A

    Strain and its types

    • Strain = change in configuration / original configuration; it is a pure number. For a rubber band stretched from 10 cm to 12 cm, ε = 2/10 = 0.2 (20%).
    • Longitudinal strain ε = ΔL/L — tensile (elongation) or compressive (shortening).
    • Volume strain εᵥ = ΔV/V — from uniform normal stress (pressure) on all faces.
    • Shear strain γ = θ = ΔL/L = tanφ — the angle through which a face is tilted when one face is held fixed and the opposite face slides.
    Strainlongitudinal ε = ΔL/L  ·  volume εᵥ = ΔV/V  ·  shear γ = φ ≈ tanφ = Δx/L
    💡 NoteTensile and compressive stress produce longitudinal strain; pressure from all sides produces volume strain; a force parallel to a face produces shear strain. Matching the stress type to the strain type is the whole of this lecture.

    Book Example 1 · L-1
    A load of 4.0 kg is suspended from a ceiling through a steel wire of radius 2.0 mm. Find the tensile stress developed in the wire when equilibrium is achieved. Take g = 3.1π m/s².
    Tension equals the weight: F = mg = 4.0 × 3.1π = 12.4π N.
    Area of cross-section: A = πr² = π(2.0 × 10⁻³)² = 4.0π × 10⁻⁶ m².
    Tensile stress σ = F/A = 12.4π / (4.0π × 10⁻⁶) = 3.1 / 10⁻⁶ = 3.1 × 10⁶ N/m².
    σ = 3.1 × 10⁶ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: choosing g = 3.1π cancels the π in the area, leaving clean arithmetic — a hint that the examiner wants you to simplify before substituting numbers.
    Book Example 2 · L-1
    A bar of cross-section A is subjected to equal and opposite tensile forces F at its ends. Consider a plane section whose normal makes angle θ with the axis of the bar. (a) tensile stress on this plane, (b) shearing stress on this plane, (c) θ for maximum tensile stress, (d) θ for maximum shearing stress.
    The inclined section has larger area A′ = A/cosθ; resolve F into a normal component Fₙ = F·cosθ and a tangential component Fᵗ = F·sinθ.
    (a) Tensile stress = Fₙ/A′ = (F cosθ)/(A/cosθ) = (F/A)cos²θ.
    (b) Shearing stress = Fᵗ/A′ = (F sinθ)/(A/cosθ) = (F/A)sinθcosθ = (F/2A)sin2θ.
    (c) Tensile stress (F/A)cos²θ is maximum when cosθ = 1, i.e. θ = 0° (section ⊥ to the axis).
    (d) Shearing stress (F/2A)sin2θ is maximum when sin2θ = 1, i.e. 2θ = 90° ⇒ θ = 45°.
    (a) (F/A)cos²θ   (b) (F/2A)sin2θ   (c) θ = 0°   (d) θ = 45°
    ⚡ JEE trick: a bar pulled axially fails along a 45° plane because that is where the shear stress peaks — the reason ductile metals neck and slip at 45°.
    Book Example 3 · L-1
    The formula for volumetric strain is: (a) ε = ΔL/L (b) ε = ΔV/V (c) ε = ΔP/P (d) None of these.
    Volumetric (volume) strain is the change in volume per unit original volume.
    εᵥ = (change in volume)/(original volume) = ΔV/V.
    (b) ε = ΔV/V
    ⚡ JEE trick: ΔL/L is longitudinal strain and ΔP/P is a pressure ratio, not a strain — only ΔV/V is dimensionless and tied to a volume change.
    L-2

    Elastic Limit, Hooke's Law, Young's Modulus and Elongation

    Elastic limit and Hooke's law

    • Elastic limit: the maximum stress a material can bear and still return to its original shape. Below it the body is elastic; beyond it it enters the plastic region and keeps a permanent deformation.
    • Hooke's law: for small deformations stress ∝ strain, so σ = Y·ε, and the modulus of elasticity Y = stress/strain. Y is a measure of stiffness (higher Y = stiffer); in some books it is written E.
    • The three elastic moduli are Young's modulus (axial), shear modulus (shape) and bulk modulus (volume).
    Hooke's lawσ = Y·ε ⇒ Y = stress / strain

    Young's modulus

    • Young's modulus = longitudinal stress / longitudinal strain. For a wire of length L, area A, extension ΔL under force F: Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/(A·ΔL).
    • For a mass M hung from a wire of radius r: Y = MgL/(πr²·ΔL), so the extension is ΔL = FL/(AY).
    • Rod as a spring: a rod behaves like a spring of stiffness k = YA/L, since F = (YA/L)·ΔL ⇒ F ∝ ΔL. For two rods in series k = k₁k₂/(k₁+k₂) with kᵢ = YᵢAᵢ/Lᵢ.
    Young's modulusY = FL/(A·ΔL)  ·  ΔL = FL/(AY)  ·  rod-spring k = YA/L
    ★ RememberSteel has a high Y (stiff, deforms little under load — used in bridges and cables); rubber has a low Y (flexible — used in tyres and shock absorbers). A perfectly rigid body is the Y → ∞ limit.

    Composite bars, stress–strain curve, self-weight and breaking

    • Composite bar (e.g. a copper rod inside a steel tube, ends rigidly joined): both parts have the same strain (ΔL₁ = ΔL₂), the loads add A₁f₁ + A₂f₂ = F, and the stress in each goes as its Young's modulus: f₁ = Y₁F/(A₁Y₁ + A₂Y₂), f₂ = Y₂F/(A₁Y₁ + A₂Y₂).
    • Stress–strain diagram: linear up to the proportional limit (slope = Y), elastic up to the elastic limit, then yield, plastic flow and finally the breaking / fracture point. A larger plastic region ⇒ more ductile; a short one ⇒ more brittle.
    • Elongation due to own weight: tension rises linearly from 0 at the bottom to mg at the top, so the effective load is mg/2 and ΔL = mgL/(2AY).
    • Breaking of a wire: maximum force Fᵐ = σᵐ·A; the greatest self-supporting length is Lᵐ = σᵐ/(ρg), independent of cross-section.
    Key L-2 resultscomposite: f = YF/(A₁Y₁+A₂Y₂)  ·  self-weight ΔL = mgL/(2AY)  ·  breaking length L = σᵐ/(ρg)
    💡 NoteSeries wires → same force, extensions add (use k = YA/L and k = k₁k₂/(k₁+k₂)). Composite / parallel bars → same extension, loads add. Deciding “same force” vs “same extension” first fixes the whole problem.
    Worked examples from the module
    Book Example 1 · L-2
    A load of 4.0 kg is suspended from a ceiling through a steel wire of length 20 m and radius 2.0 mm. The length increases by 0.031 mm as equilibrium is achieved. Find Young's modulus of steel. Take g = 3.1π m/s².
    Longitudinal stress σ = F/A = (4.0 × 3.1π)/(π(2.0 × 10⁻³)²) = 3.1 × 10⁶ N/m².
    Longitudinal strain ε = ΔL/L = (0.031 × 10⁻³)/20 = 1.55 × 10⁻⁶.
    Y = σ/ε = (3.1 × 10⁶)/(1.55 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: 2 × 10¹¹ Pa is the standard value for steel — worth memorising as a sanity check on any steel-wire answer.
    Book Example 2 · L-2
    Determine the elongation of a steel bar 1 m long and 1.5 cm² cross-section when subjected to a pull of 1.5 × 10⁴ N. Take Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    From Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L), the extension is ΔL = FL/(AY).
    A = 1.5 cm² = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m².
    ΔL = (1.5 × 10⁴ × 1.0)/(1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.0 × 10¹¹) = (1.5 × 10⁴)/(3.0 × 10⁷) = 0.5 × 10⁻³ m.
    ΔL = 0.5 × 10⁻³ m = 0.5 mm
    ⚡ JEE trick: keep ΔL = FL/AY as one unit-consistent block — convert cm² to m² first and the powers of ten line up cleanly.
    Book Example 3 · L-2
    A copper wire of length 2 m and a steel wire of length 1 m, both of diameter 2.0 mm, are connected end to end. When stretched by a load the net elongation is 0.8 mm. Obtain the load applied. Y_copper = 1.1 × 10¹¹, Y_steel = 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    Each wire acts as a spring of stiffness k = YA/L, with A = π(1.0 × 10⁻³)² = π × 10⁻⁶ m² (d = 2 mm ⇒ r = 1 mm).
    k₁ (copper) = Y₁A/L₁ = (1.1 × 10¹¹ × π × 10⁻⁶)/2 = 1.73 × 10⁵ N/m.
    k₂ (steel) = Y₂A/L₂ = (2.0 × 10¹¹ × π × 10⁻⁶)/1 = 6.28 × 10⁵ N/m.
    Series combination: k = k₁k₂/(k₁+k₂) = (1.73 × 6.28 × 10¹⁰)/((1.73 + 6.28) × 10⁵) = 1.36 × 10⁵ N/m.
    Load W = k·ΔL = 1.36 × 10⁵ × 0.8 × 10⁻³ = 108.8 N.
    W = 108.8 N
    ⚡ JEE trick: treating each wire as a spring and combining in series bypasses sharing the elongation between the two pieces by hand.
    Book Example 4 · L-2
    A copper rod 25 mm in diameter is encased in a steel tube of 30 mm inner and 35 mm external diameter. The ends are rigidly attached. The composite bar is 500 mm long and is subjected to an axial pull of 30 kN. Find the stresses induced in the rod and tube. Y_steel = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm², Y_copper = 1 × 10⁵ N/mm².
    Convert: Y₁ (copper) = 1 × 10¹¹ N/m², Y₂ (steel) = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m², F = 3 × 10⁴ N.
    Areas: A₁ = π(0.0125)² = 4.91 × 10⁻⁴ m²; A₂ = π[(0.0175)² − (0.015)²] = 2.55 × 10⁻⁴ m².
    Denominator A₁Y₁ + A₂Y₂ = 4.91×10⁻⁴·10¹¹ + 2.55×10⁻⁴·2×10¹¹ = 1.00 × 10⁸ N.
    Copper: f₁ = Y₁F/(A₁Y₁+A₂Y₂) = (10¹¹ × 3×10⁴)/(1.00×10⁸) = 2.996 × 10⁷ N/m².
    Steel: f₂ = Y₂F/(A₁Y₁+A₂Y₂) = (2×10¹¹ × 3×10⁴)/(1.00×10⁸) = 5.991 × 10⁷ N/m².
    f_copper = 2.996 × 10⁷ N/m², f_steel = 5.991 × 10⁷ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: because both parts share the strain, the stress ratio equals the modulus ratio, f₂/f₁ = Y₂/Y₁ = 2 — a one-line internal check.
    Book Example 5 · L-2
    The stress–strain graphs for materials A and B (same scale) are shown. (a) which has greater Young's modulus? (b) which is more ductile? (c) which is more brittle? (d) which is the stronger material?
    (a) Young's modulus is the slope of the linear part. Curve A is steeper ⇒ A has the greater Young's modulus.
    (b) A larger plastic region means more ductility. The plastic region of A is larger ⇒ A is more ductile.
    (c) Less ductile means more brittle ⇒ B is more brittle.
    (d) The one withstanding the greater breaking stress is stronger. A's breaking strength is higher ⇒ A is stronger.
    (a) A   (b) A   (c) B   (d) A
    ⚡ JEE trick: read the graph in three features — slope = Y, length of plastic plateau = ductility, height of the peak = strength.
    Book Example 6 · L-2
    Find the change in length of a wire of mass 1 kg hanging from a rigid support. The length of the wire is 1 m and the area of cross-section is 1.5 cm². Take Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    For a wire extending under its own weight, ΔL = mgL/(2AY) (effective load = mg/2 because tension varies linearly).
    A = 1.5 cm² = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m².
    ΔL = (1 × 10 × 1)/(2 × 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 2 × 10¹¹) = 10/(6 × 10⁷) = 1.67 × 10⁻⁷ m.
    ΔL = 1.67 × 10⁻⁷ m
    ⚡ JEE trick: the factor 2 in the denominator is the signature of a self-weight problem — forgetting it doubles the answer.
    Book Example 7 · L-2
    If the elastic limit of copper is 1.5 × 10⁸ N/m², determine the minimum diameter a copper wire can have under a load of 10.0 kg if its elastic limit is not to be exceeded.
    At the limit F/A_min = σᵐ, with A_min = πd²_min/4, so d_min = √(4F/πσᵐ).
    F = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N.
    d_min = √[(4 × 10 × 9.8)/(3.14 × 1.5 × 10⁸)] = √(392/4.71 × 10⁸) = √(8.32 × 10⁻⁷) = 9.1 × 10⁻⁴ m.
    d_min = 9.1 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.91 mm
    ⚡ JEE trick: “minimum diameter / maximum load” means setting the stress exactly at the limiting stress — the inequality becomes an equality.
    Book Example 8 · L-2
    Find the greatest length of steel wire that can hang vertically without breaking. Breaking stress of steel = 8.0 × 10⁸ N/m², density of steel = 8 × 10³ kg/m³, g = 10 m/s².
    The wire breaks when its own weight produces the breaking stress: σᵐ = (weight)/A = (A·L·ρ)g/A = ρgL.
    So the greatest length is L = σᵐ/(ρg).
    L = (8.0 × 10⁸)/(8 × 10³ × 10) = (8.0 × 10⁸)/(8 × 10⁴) = 10⁴ m.
    L = 10⁴ m (10 km)
    ⚡ JEE trick: the area cancels — the maximum self-supporting length depends only on breaking stress and density, never on the wire's thickness.
    L-3

    Elastic Potential Energy and Interatomic Force Constant

    Energy stored in a deformed body

    • Stretching a wire stores elastic potential energy equal to the work done against the restoring force. With F = (AY/L)x, the work to extend from 0 to ΔL is W = ∫(AY/L)x·dx = (AY/2L)·ΔL².
    • Rewriting with stress and strain: U = (1/2) × stress × strain × volume, and the energy density (energy per unit volume) is u = (1/2)·stress·strain = (1/2)Yε² = σ²/(2Y).
    • Equivalently the work done in stretching a wire is W = (1/2) × load × extension — the factor (1/2) because the load rises from zero to its final value.
    Elastic PEU = (AY/2L)·ΔL² = (1/2)·stress·strain·volume  ·  u = (1/2)Yε² = σ²/(2Y)
    Work in stretchingW = (1/2) × F × ΔL

    Interatomic force constant

    • Atoms behave like springs: the interatomic force is proportional to the change in spacing, f = k·Δr, where k is the interatomic force constant and Δr the change in interatomic distance.
    • Linking to the macroscopic wire (equilibrium spacing r₀, each chain of atoms contributing) gives k = Y·r₀ — a direct bridge from the bulk Young's modulus to the stiffness of a single bond.
    Interatomic force constantf = k·Δr  ·  k = Y·r₀
    ★ RememberThe three equivalent forms of energy density — (1/2)stress·strain, (1/2)Yε² and σ²/(2Y) — let you start from whichever of stress, strain or Y the problem actually gives.
    Worked examples from the module
    Book Example 1 · L-3
    A 40 kg boy whose legs are 4 cm² in area and 50 cm long falls through a height of 2 m without breaking his leg bones. If the bones can withstand a stress of 0.9 × 10⁸ N/m², calculate Young's modulus for the bone.
    Loss of PE = elastic energy stored in both legs: mgh = 2 × [(1/2) × stress × strain × volume] = σ²·(A·L)/Y.
    Here the two legs give the factor 2 and the (1/2) from the energy cancel, so mgh = 2 × [σ²/(2Y)]·(A·L).
    40 × 9.8 × 2 = 2 × [(0.9 × 10⁸)²/Y] × (4 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.50).
    784 = 2 × (8.1 × 10¹⁵/Y) × (2 × 10⁻⁴) = (16.2 × 10¹¹)/Y.
    Y = (16.2 × 10¹¹)/784 = 2.05 × 10⁹ N/m².
    Y = 2.05 × 10⁹ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: equate the gravitational PE lost to the elastic energy stored (σ²/2Y per unit volume) — the standard “impact without fracture” model.
    Book Example 2 · L-3
    A steel wire 4.0 m long is stretched through 2.0 mm. The cross-sectional area is 2.0 mm² and Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m². Find (a) the energy density of the wire, (b) the elastic potential energy stored.
    (a) Strain ε = ΔL/L = (2.0 × 10⁻³)/4.0 = 5 × 10⁻⁴; energy density u = (1/2)Yε².
    u = (1/2) × 2.0 × 10¹¹ × (5 × 10⁻⁴)² = (1/2) × 2.0 × 10¹¹ × 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.5 × 10⁴ J/m³.
    (b) Volume = A × L = (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) × 4.0 = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³.
    U = u × volume = 2.5 × 10⁴ × 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.20 J.
    (a) u = 2.5 × 10⁴ J/m³   (b) U = 0.20 J
    ⚡ JEE trick: compute the energy density first, then multiply by the wire's volume — cleaner than plugging into (AY/2L)ΔL² directly.
    Book Example 3 · L-3
    If the interatomic distance between the atoms of a material is 10⁻¹⁰ m, find the interatomic force constant of the material. Given Young's modulus of the solid is 1.1 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    The force constant is related to the modulus and the equilibrium spacing by k = Y·r₀.
    k = (1.1 × 10¹¹) × (10⁻¹⁰) = 11 N/m.
    k = 11 N/m
    ⚡ JEE trick: k = Yr₀ turns a macroscopic modulus into a single-bond spring constant — of the order of a few N/m, as expected for atomic bonds.
    L-4

    Modulus of Rigidity, Bulk Modulus, Poisson's Ratio and Moduli Relations

    Modulus of rigidity (shear modulus)

    • When a tangential force acts on a face while the opposite face is fixed, the body is sheared (shape changes, volume does not). The shear strain is the angle φ (= KK′/KN = tanφ ≈ φ).
    • Modulus of rigidity η = shearing stress / shearing strain = (F/A)/φ = F/(Aφ). Higher η = stiffer against shape change; only solids have a non-zero η.
    Rigidity modulusη = (F/A)/φ = F/(Aφ)

    Bulk modulus and compressibility

    • Bulk modulus K = −(volume stress)/(volume strain) = −P/(ΔV/V) = −ΔP/(ΔV/V). The minus sign keeps K positive (increasing pressure decreases volume). A higher K means the material is less compressible.
    • Compressibility is the reciprocal of the bulk modulus, C = 1/K — a measure of how easily the volume changes per unit pressure. Gases are highly compressible (small K, large C); solids and liquids are not.
    Bulk modulusK = −ΔP/(ΔV/V)  ·  compressibility C = 1/K  ·  ΔP = K·(−ΔV/V)

    Poisson's ratio

    • When a rod is stretched along its length it contracts laterally (and expands laterally when compressed). Poisson's ratio σ = −(lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) = −(Δr/r)/(ΔL/L).
    • It is dimensionless and theoretically lies between −1 and +0.5. σ = +0.5 means the volume stays constant during deformation; σ = −1 means the material contracts completely in the lateral direction when stretched.
    Poisson's ratioσ = −(Δr/r)/(ΔL/L)  ·  range: −1 ≤ σ ≤ 0.5

    Relations among Y, K, η and σ

    • The four elastic constants are not independent; for an isotropic solid any two fix the rest:
    Moduli inter-relationsY = 3K(1 − 2σ)  ·  Y = 2η(1 + σ)  ·  9/Y = 1/K + 3/η  (so Y = 9Kη/(3K + η))  ·  σ = (Y/2η) − 1
    ⚠ Book misprintOn page 296 the module first prints the Young–rigidity relation as Y = 2G(1 + 2σ) and then immediately as Y = 2G(1 + σ). The correct relation is Y = 2η(1 + σ) — the “2σ” in the first line is a typo; the derivation (which leads to σ = Y/2η − 1) uses the correct Y = 2η(1 + σ).
    ★ RememberFrom Y = 3K(1 − 2σ), σ → 0.5 forces Y → 0 against shear-free compression, and from Y = 2η(1 + σ), σ = −1 forces Y → 0 — exactly the −1 to +0.5 limits of Poisson's ratio.
    Worked examples from the module
    Book Example 1 · L-4
    A rubber cube of 5 cm side has one face fixed rigidly while its opposite face is sheared by 3 mm parallel to the fixed face by a tangential force of 15 N. Calculate the modulus of rigidity (in N/m²).
    Shear strain φ = displacement/height = (3 × 10⁻³)/(0.05) = 0.06 rad.
    Area of the sheared face A = (0.05)² = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m².
    η = F/(Aφ) = 15/(2.5 × 10⁻³ × 0.06) = 15/(1.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 10⁵ N/m².
    η = 10⁵ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: shear strain is a pure angle (displacement ÷ the perpendicular distance to the fixed face) — never divide by the length along the force.
    Book Example 2 · L-4
    The bulk modulus of water is 2.3 × 10⁹ N/m². (a) Find its compressibility in atm⁻¹. (b) How much pressure in atmospheres is needed to compress a sample of water by 0.1%?
    Express K in atm: K = (2.3 × 10⁹)/(1.01 × 10⁵) = 2.27 × 10⁴ atm.
    (a) Compressibility C = 1/K = 1/(2.27 × 10⁴) = 4.4 × 10⁻⁵ atm⁻¹.
    (b) Volume strain −ΔV/V = 0.1% = 1.0 × 10⁻³; required pressure ΔP = K × (−ΔV/V).
    ΔP = (2.27 × 10⁴) × (1.0 × 10⁻³) = 22.7 atm.
    (a) C = 4.4 × 10⁻⁵ atm⁻¹   (b) ΔP = 22.7 atm
    ⚡ JEE trick: converting K into atm first makes both parts fall out instantly — compressibility is just its reciprocal.
    Book Example 3 · L-4
    One end of a nylon rope of length 4.5 m and diameter 6 mm is fixed to a tree-limb. A monkey weighing 100 N jumps to catch the free end and stays there. Find the elongation of the rope and the corresponding change in diameter. Y_nylon = 4.8 × 10¹¹ N/m², Poisson's ratio of nylon = 0.2.
    Area A = πr² = π(3 × 10⁻³)² = π × 9 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.83 × 10⁻⁵ m².
    Elongation ΔL = TL/(AY) = (100 × 4.5)/(2.83 × 10⁻⁵ × 4.8 × 10¹¹) = 450/(1.36 × 10⁷) = 3.32 × 10⁻⁵ m.
    Poisson's ratio: σ = (Δd/d)/(ΔL/L) = (Δd · L)/(ΔL · d), so Δd = σ·ΔL·d/L.
    Δd = (0.2 × 3.32 × 10⁻⁵ × 6 × 10⁻³)/4.5 = (3.98 × 10⁻⁸)/4.5 = 8.8 × 10⁻⁹ m.
    ΔL = 3.32 × 10⁻⁵ m, Δd = 8.8 × 10⁻⁹ m (diameter decreases)
    ⚠ Book misprintThe module prints the elongation as 3.32 × 10⁻⁶ m. Re-working ΔL = TL/(AY) = 450/(1.36 × 10⁷) gives 3.32 × 10⁻⁵ m — a factor-of-ten slip. The book's own next line (Δd = 8.8 × 10⁻⁹ m) is in fact consistent with the correct 10⁻⁵ elongation, confirming the typo is only in the elongation exponent.
    ⚡ JEE trick: lateral contraction Δd = σ·ΔL·d/L follows straight from the Poisson definition — keep the longitudinal strain ΔL/L as one block.
    Book Example 4 · L-4
    If the shear modulus and bulk modulus of a material are 0.5 × 10¹¹ N/m² and 0.6 × 10¹¹ N/m² respectively, find the Young's modulus of that material.
    Use the relation Y = 9Kη/(η + 3K) (equivalently 9/Y = 1/K + 3/η), with η = 0.5 × 10¹¹, K = 0.6 × 10¹¹.
    Numerator 9Kη = 9 × (0.6 × 10¹¹)(0.5 × 10¹¹) = 2.7 × 10²².
    Denominator η + 3K = 0.5 × 10¹¹ + 1.8 × 10¹¹ = 2.3 × 10¹¹.
    Y = (2.7 × 10²²)/(2.3 × 10¹¹) = 1.2 × 10¹¹ N/m².
    Y = 1.2 × 10¹¹ N/m²
    ⚡ JEE trick: whenever both η and K are given, Y = 9Kη/(3K + η) is the one-step route — no need to find σ first.

    Strong Box · PYQ 1 · JEE Advanced 2020 Paper 2
    A cubical solid aluminium block has an edge length of 1 m on the surface of the earth. It is kept on the floor of a 5 km deep ocean. Taking the average density of water and the acceleration due to gravity to be 10³ kg/m³ and 10 m/s² respectively, the change in the edge length of the block (in mm) is ____ (bulk modulus of aluminium = 7 × 10¹⁰ N/m²).
    Pressure at depth h: P = ρgh = 10³ × 10 × 5000 = 5 × 10⁷ Pa.
    Volume strain: ΔV/V = P/K = (5 × 10⁷)/(7 × 10¹⁰) = 7.14 × 10⁻⁴.
    For a cube, linear strain = (1/3) volume strain: Δa/a = (1/3)(7.14 × 10⁻⁴) = 2.38 × 10⁻⁴.
    Δa = 2.38 × 10⁻⁴ × 1 m = 2.38 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.238 mm ≈ 0.24 mm.
    Δa ≈ 0.24 mm (contraction)
    ⚡ JEE trick: for a cube under hydrostatic pressure, the edge (linear) strain is one-third of the volume strain — apply Δa/a = (1/3)(P/K).
    Strong Box · PYQ 2 · JEE Advanced 2013 Paper 1
    One end of a horizontal thick copper wire of length 2L and radius 2R is welded to an end of another horizontal thin copper wire of length L and radius R. When the arrangement is stretched by applying forces at two ends, the ratio of the elongation in the thin wire to that in the thick wire is: (a) 0.25 (b) 0.50 (c) 2.00 (d) 4.00
    The wires are in series, so the same force F passes through both. Elongation ΔL = FL/(AY) with the same Y (both copper).
    Thin wire: length L, area A = πR² ⇒ ΔL_thin = FL/(πR²Y).
    Thick wire: length 2L, area π(2R)² = 4πR² ⇒ ΔL_thick = F(2L)/(4πR²Y) = FL/(2πR²Y).
    Ratio = ΔL_thin/ΔL_thick = [FL/(πR²Y)] ÷ [FL/(2πR²Y)] = 2.
    (c) 2.00
    ⚡ JEE trick: with the same force, ΔL ∝ L/R² — the thin wire's half the length but one-quarter the area wins by a net factor of 2.
    Strong Box · PYQ 3 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 1
    Two wires A and B made of different materials of lengths 60 cm and 54 cm, and areas of cross-section 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ m² and 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² respectively, are stretched by the same magnitude under a given load. The ratio of the Young's modulus of A to that of B is x : 3. The value of x is ___ : (a) 2 (b) 1 (c) 4 (d) 5
    Same load F and same elongation ΔL: from ΔL = FL/(AY), equal ΔL gives L_A/(A_A Y_A) = L_B/(A_B Y_B).
    So Y_A/Y_B = (L_A · A_B)/(A_A · L_B) = (60 × 4.5 × 10⁻⁵)/(3.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 54).
    = (60 × 4.5)/(3.0 × 54) = 270/162 = 5/3.
    Hence Y_A : Y_B = 5 : 3, so x = 5.
    (d) 5
    ⚡ JEE trick: “same elongation, same load” collapses to Y ∝ L/A — just cross-multiply the lengths and areas.
    Strong Box · PYQ 4 · JEE Main 2026 Shift 1
    The strain–stress plot for materials A, B, C and D is shown (strain on the y-axis, stress on the x-axis). Which material has the largest Young's modulus? (a) D (b) B (c) C (d) A
    Young's modulus Y = stress/strain. On a strain-vs-stress plot the slope is strain/stress = 1/Y.
    So the line with the smallest slope (the one closest to the stress / x-axis) has the largest Young's modulus.
    In the figure the lines from steepest to flattest are A, B, D, C; curve C lies closest to the stress axis.
    (c) C
    ⚡ JEE trick: beware the swapped axes — on a normal stress-vs-strain graph the steepest line has the largest Y, but with strain on the vertical axis it is the flattest line.
    Strong Box · PYQ 5 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 2
    A massless spring gets elongated by amount x₁ under a tension of 5 N. Its elongation is x₂ under a tension of 7 N. For the elongation of (5x₁ − 2x₂), the tension in the spring will be: (a) 39 N (b) 15 N (c) 11 N (d) 20 N
    For a spring F = kx, so x₁ = 5/k and x₂ = 7/k.
    5x₁ − 2x₂ = 5(5/k) − 2(7/k) = (25 − 14)/k = 11/k.
    Tension for this elongation: F = k × (11/k) = 11 N.
    (c) 11 N
    ⚡ JEE trick: elongation is linear in tension, so any linear combination a·x₁ + b·x₂ corresponds to tension a·(5) + b·(7) — here 5×5 − 2×7 = 11 N.
    Strong Box · PYQ 6 · JEE Main 2025 Shift 2
    The volume contraction of a solid copper cube of edge length 10 cm, when subjected to a hydraulic pressure of 7 × 10⁶ Pa, would be ___ mm³. (Bulk modulus of copper = 1.4 × 10¹¹ N/m².)
    Volume strain: ΔV/V = P/K = (7 × 10⁶)/(1.4 × 10¹¹) = 5 × 10⁻⁵.
    Volume of cube: V = (0.10)³ = 10⁻³ m³ = 10⁶ mm³.
    ΔV = V × (P/K) = 10⁶ mm³ × 5 × 10⁻⁵ = 50 mm³ (= 5 × 10⁻⁸ m³).
    ΔV = 50 mm³ (= 5 × 10⁻⁸ m³)
    ⚡ JEE trick: work the volume strain P/K first, then multiply by the volume in the units the answer is asked for (here 10⁶ mm³).
    Strong Box · PYQ 7 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 2
    A wire of cross-sectional area A, modulus of elasticity 2 × 10¹¹ N/m² and length 2 m is stretched between two vertical rigid supports. When a mass of 2 kg is suspended at the middle it sags lower from its original position making angle θ = 1/100 radian at the supports. The value of A is ___ × 10⁻⁴ m² (x « L, g = 10 m/s²).
    At the middle, two tensions balance the weight: 2T sinθ = mg, so T = mg/(2sinθ) ≈ mg/(2θ) = (2 × 10)/(2 × 0.01) = 1000 N.
    Each half (length L = 1 m) stretches from L to L/cosθ, so strain = (secθ − 1) ≈ θ²/2 = (0.01)²/2 = 5 × 10⁻⁵.
    Also T = (stress)A = Y·(strain)·A, so 1000 = (2 × 10¹¹)(5 × 10⁻⁵)A = 10⁷·A.
    A = 1000/10⁷ = 10⁻⁴ m².
    A = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m² (value = 1)
    ⚡ JEE trick: a sagging horizontal wire has small-angle strain θ²/2, while the tension comes from mg/(2θ) — two different uses of the same small θ.
    Strong Box · PYQ 8 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 2
    Two blocks of mass 2 kg and 4 kg are connected by a metal wire going over a smooth pulley. The radius of the wire is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ m and Young's modulus of the metal is 2.0 × 10¹¹ N/m². The longitudinal strain developed in the wire is 1/(απ). The value of α is ___ (g = 10 m/s²).
    Atwood acceleration: a = (m₂ − m₁)g/(m₁ + m₂) = (4 − 2)(10)/6 = 10/3 m/s².
    Wire tension: T = m₁(g + a) = 2(10 + 10/3) = 2 × 40/3 = 80/3 N (check: m₂(g − a) = 4 × 20/3 = 80/3 ✓).
    Area A = πr² = π(4 × 10⁻⁵)² = 16π × 10⁻¹⁰ m².
    Strain = T/(AY) = (80/3)/(16π × 10⁻¹⁰ × 2 × 10¹¹) = (80/3)/(320π) = 1/(12π).
    Comparing with 1/(απ): α = 12.
    α = 12
    ⚡ JEE trick: find the Atwood tension first (not just mg), then strain = T/AY — the π from the circular area is what leaves the clean 1/(12π).
    Strong Box · PYQ 9 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 1
    Each of three blocks P, Q and R has a mass of 3 kg. Each of the wires A and B has cross-sectional area 0.005 cm² and Young's modulus 2 × 10¹¹ N/m². Neglecting friction, the longitudinal strain on wire B is ___ × 10⁻⁴ (g = 10 m/s²). [P and Q on a table joined by wire A; wire B runs over a pulley to a hanging block R.]
    The whole system (P + Q on the table, R hanging) accelerates: a = m_R·g/(m_P + m_Q + m_R) = (3 × 10)/9 = 10/3 m/s².
    Wire B carries only the hanging block R: for R, m_R·g − T_B = m_R·a ⇒ T_B = m_R(g − a) = 3(10 − 10/3) = 20 N.
    Area A = 0.005 cm² = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m².
    Strain = T_B/(A·Y) = 20/(5 × 10⁻⁷ × 2 × 10¹¹) = 20/(10⁵) = 2 × 10⁻⁴.
    Strain on B = 2 × 10⁻⁴ (value = 2)
    ⚡ JEE trick: the tension in B is set by the hanging block alone (m_R(g − a)), not by the whole weight — find a first, then the local tension.
    Strong Box · PYQ 10 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 1
    The density and breaking stress of a wire are 6 × 10⁴ kg/m³ and 1.2 × 10⁸ N/m² respectively. The wire is suspended from a rigid support on a planet where the acceleration due to gravity is one-third of its value on the earth's surface. The maximum length of the wire that can be hung without breaking is ___ m (g = 10 m/s²).
    A hanging wire breaks when its own weight gives the breaking stress: σᵐ = ρ L g′, so L = σᵐ/(ρ g′).
    Planet gravity: g′ = g/3 = 10/3 m/s².
    L = (1.2 × 10⁸)/(6 × 10⁴ × 10/3) = (1.2 × 10⁸)/(2 × 10⁵) = 600 m.
    L = 600 m
    ⚡ JEE trick: maximum self-supporting length L = σᵐ/(ρg) — weaker gravity (g/3) lets the wire hang three times longer.
    Strong Box · PYQ 11 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 2
    Two metallic wires P and Q have the same volume and are made of the same material. Their areas of cross-section are in the ratio 4 : 1. A force F₁ applied to P produces an extension Δl; the force required to produce the same extension in Q is F₂. The value of F₁/F₂ is ___.
    Same volume V = A·L, so L ∝ 1/A. With A_P : A_Q = 4 : 1, L_P : L_Q = 1 : 4, i.e. L_Q = 4L_P.
    Extension Δl = FL/(AY). For P: Δl = F₁L_P/(A_P Y). For Q: Δl = F₂L_Q/(A_Q Y).
    Equal Δl: F₁L_P/A_P = F₂L_Q/A_Q ⇒ F₁/F₂ = (L_Q/A_Q)(A_P/L_P) = (4L_P/A_P)(A_P/L_P) × (A_P/A_Q)⁻¹…
    Substitute A_P = 4A_Q, L_Q = 4L_P: F₁/F₂ = (L_Q·A_P)/(A_Q·L_P) = (4L_P · 4A_Q)/(A_Q · L_P) = 16.
    F₁/F₂ = 16
    ⚡ JEE trick: at fixed volume the extension for a given force scales as L/A = (V/A)/A = V/A², so the force needed scales as A² — (4)² = 16.
    Strong Box · PYQ 12 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 2
    One end of a metal wire is fixed to a ceiling and a load of 2 kg hangs from the other end. A similar wire is attached to the bottom of the load and another load of 1 kg hangs from this lower wire. The ratio of the longitudinal strain of the upper wire to that of the lower wire is (A = 0.005 cm², Y = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m², g = 10 m/s²).
    Upper wire carries both loads: T_upper = (2 + 1)g = 3 × 10 = 30 N.
    Lower wire carries only the 1 kg load: T_lower = 1 × 10 = 10 N.
    Same area and same Y, so strain = T/(AY) ⇒ strain ratio = T_upper/T_lower = 30/10 = 3.
    Ratio = 3 : 1
    ⚡ JEE trick: identical wires share the same A and Y, so the strain ratio is just the tension ratio — the data A and Y never need substituting.
    Strong Box · PYQ 13 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 2
    A wire of length L and radius r is clamped at one end. If its other end is pulled by a force F, its length increases by l. If the radius of the wire and the applied force are both reduced to half of their original values keeping the original length constant, the increase in length will become: (a) 3 times (b) 3/2 times (c) 4 times (d) 2 times
    Extension l = FL/(πr²Y).
    New force F′ = F/2, new radius r′ = r/2, so new area = π(r/2)² = πr²/4.
    New extension l′ = (F/2)L/(πr²/4 · Y) = (F/2)(4)L/(πr²Y) = 2·FL/(πr²Y) = 2l.
    (d) 2 times
    ⚡ JEE trick: halving the force halves l, but halving the radius quarters the area and quadruples l — net factor 4 × (1/2) = 2.
    Strong Box · PYQ 14 · JEE Main 2024 Shift 1
    An elastic spring under a tension of 3 N has a length a. Its length is b under a tension of 2 N. For its length (3a − 2b), the value of the tension will be ___ N.
    A spring of natural length L₀ and constant k: length = L₀ + F/k. So a = L₀ + 3/k and b = L₀ + 2/k.
    3a − 2b = 3(L₀ + 3/k) − 2(L₀ + 2/k) = L₀ + 9/k − 4/k = L₀ + 5/k.
    This matches length = L₀ + F/k with F/k = 5/k, so F = 5 N.
    Tension = 5 N
    ⚡ JEE trick: keep the natural length explicit (length = L₀ + F/k) — the combination 3a − 2b is chosen so the L₀ survives with coefficient 1, leaving a genuine spring length.
    Strong Box · PYQ 15 · JEE Main 2023 Shift 1
    A thin rod of length 1 m and area of cross-section 3 × 10⁻⁶ m² is suspended vertically from one end. The rod is cooled from 210°C to 160°C. After cooling, a mass M is attached at the lower end so that the length of the rod again becomes 1 m. Young's modulus and coefficient of linear expansion are 2 × 10¹¹ N/m² and 2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. The value of M is ___ kg (g = 10 m/s²).
    Cooling by Δθ = 50 contracts the rod by α·Δθ·L; the mass M must stretch it back by the same amount, so thermal strain = elastic strain.
    αΔθ = (Mg/A)/Y ⇒ Mg = Y·α·Δθ·A.
    Mg = (2 × 10¹¹)(2 × 10⁻⁵)(50)(3 × 10⁻⁶) = (4 × 10⁶)(50)(3 × 10⁻⁶) = 600 N.
    M = 600/g = 600/10 = 60 kg.
    M = 60 kg
    ⚡ JEE trick: equate thermal strain αΔθ to the elastic strain Mg/(AY) — the length cancels, so the 1 m is only there to confirm the rod returns to its original length.
    Strong Box · PYQ 16 · JEE Main 2023 Shift 1
    Two wires each of radius 0.2 cm and negligible mass, one of steel (1.6 m, upper) and one of brass (1 m, lower), are loaded as shown: a 2 kg block between them and a 1.14 kg block at the bottom. The elongation of the steel wire is ___ × 10⁻⁶ m. (Y_steel = 2 × 10¹¹ N/m², g = 10 m/s².)
    The steel wire carries the total load below it: (2 + 1.14) = 3.14 kg ⇒ T = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 N = 10π N.
    Area A = πr² = π(0.2 × 10⁻²)² = π × 4 × 10⁻⁶ m².
    ΔL = TL/(AY) = (10π × 1.6)/(π × 4 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10¹¹) = 16π/(8π × 10⁵) = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m.
    So ΔL = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m = 20 × 10⁻⁶ m.
    ΔL(steel) = 20 × 10⁻⁶ m
    ⚡ JEE trick: the 31.4 N load is 10π N, so the π from the area cancels — the examiner picked 3.14 kg deliberately to clean up the arithmetic.
    Strong Box · PYQ 17 · JEE Main 2023 Shift 2
    Young's moduli of the materials of wires A and B are in the ratio 1 : 4, while their areas of cross-section are in the ratio 1 : 3. If the same load is applied to both wires (same length), the elongations produced in A and B will be in the ratio: (a) 12 : 1 (b) 1 : 36 (c) 36 : 1 (d) 1 : 12
    Elongation ΔL = FL/(AY) with the same F and L, so ΔL ∝ 1/(AY).
    ΔL_A/ΔL_B = (A_B Y_B)/(A_A Y_A) = (A_B/A_A)(Y_B/Y_A).
    A_A : A_B = 1 : 3 ⇒ A_B/A_A = 3; Y_A : Y_B = 1 : 4 ⇒ Y_B/Y_A = 4.
    ΔL_A/ΔL_B = 3 × 4 = 12, i.e. 12 : 1.
    (a) 12 : 1
    ⚡ JEE trick: for the same load and length, elongation ∝ 1/(AY) — the softer (smaller Y) and thinner (smaller A) wire A stretches 12 times more.
