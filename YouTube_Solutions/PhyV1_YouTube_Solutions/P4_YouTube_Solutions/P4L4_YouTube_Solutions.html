1What is the value of velocity of an object at maximum height when projected vertically upwards? (a) Equal to initial velocity (b) Equal to g (c) Zero (d) Equal to final velocity on descentVelocity at maximum heightA body is thrown upward and reaches its maximum height. At that position its velocity is zero🟢 ExactcZero
Concept When a body is thrown straight up, gravity acts as a constant downward acceleration of 9.8 m s⁻² that steadily eats away its upward speed. The body keeps climbing only while some upward velocity remains; the very instant that velocity falls to zero it stops rising — and that instant defines the maximum height. So at the top the velocity is exactly zero. It is a momentary halt, not a permanent one: gravity is still acting at full strength, so the body immediately begins to fall back down.
Formula Take upward as positive, a = −g. Velocity at any time v = u − g·t; the top is defined by v = 0. Equivalently v² = u² − 2gh hits v = 0 exactly when h = u²/2g. "Maximum height" and "velocity = 0" are two names for the same instant — that is the whole idea being tested here.
Solution
1. Set up: launch speed u (upward, +), acceleration a = −g throughout the entire flight.
2. From v = u − g·t the speed decreases linearly; the highest point is reached the moment v just becomes 0.
3. Test (a) "equal to initial velocity": the body regains speed u only when it returns to the launch level on the way down, not at the top — reject.Speed u at the summit would mean gravity did nothing during the climb.
4. Test (b) "equal to g": g = 9.8 m s⁻² is an acceleration, not a velocity — dimensionally impossible for a velocity — reject.
5. Test (d) "equal to final velocity on descent": by symmetry that descent speed at launch level is u, again not zero — reject.
6. Only (c) survives: at maximum height v = 0.

4A stone released from a certain height 'h' reaches the ground in time 'T'. When will it be at (a) 1/2 h (b) 3/4 h (c) 5/6 h below the point of release?Free fall — time to reach a fractional heightA body is released from the top of a tower of height h. It takes t sec to reach the ground. Where will be the ball after t/2 s🟡 Similar (inverse form of the same h = ½gt² relation)(√(1/2))T, (√(3/4))T, (√(5/6))T
Concept For a body released from rest, distance fallen grows as the square of the time: s ∝ t². This single fact answers the whole question. Because neither h nor T is given a number, the smart move is to work in ratios — divide the "distance fallen at time t" equation by the "total distance at time T" equation so that ½g cancels and only t/T survives. The square law also delivers the key insight students find counter-intuitive: falling half the height takes far more than half the time (0.707 T), because the body is still slow during the early part of the drop and only picks up speed later.
Formula Released from rest ⇒ u = 0, so s = ½·g·t² (m), and for the full drop h = ½·g·T² (m). Dividing: s ÷ h = t² ÷ T² ⇒ t = T·√(s ÷ h). The ratio s/h is a pure number (m ÷ m), so its square root is a pure number too, and t comes out in the same unit as T — seconds ✓. Assumptions: dropped from rest (u = 0), air resistance neglected, g constant, distances measured downward from the release point.
Solution
1. Write the two facts with the same constant ½g: at time T the stone has fallen h, so h = ½g·T² (m); at any earlier time t it has fallen s, so s = ½g·t² (m).
2. Divide the second by the first — the ½g (units m s⁻²) cancels completely: s/h = (½g·t²)/(½g·T²) = t²/T².This is why we never need a number for g or h; the ratio kills both.
3. Take the square root of both sides: t = T·√(s/h) (s). Now just substitute each fraction.
4. Case (a), s = h/2: s/h = 1/2, so t = T·√(1/2) = T ÷ √2 = 0.707 T (s).√0.5 = 0.7071 — more than half of T, because the stone is still slow over the first half of the drop.
5. Case (b), s = 3h/4: s/h = 3/4, so t = T·√(3)/2 = T × 1.732 ÷ 2 = 0.866 T (s).√0.75 = 0.8660 — three-quarters of the height is reached at 86.6% of the total time.
6. Case (c), s = 5h/6: s/h = 5/6 = 0.8333, so t = T·√(0.8333) = 0.913 T (s).√0.8333 = 0.9129 — the last sixth of the drop takes under 9% of the time, as the stone is fastest at the end.
7. Sanity check the ordering: 0.707 T < 0.866 T < 0.913 T < T — the deeper the point, the later it is reached, and every time is less than T ✓.

Concept The "velocity of projection" is the velocity the instant the ball leaves the origin, i.e. at t = 0. The resultant speed is then the vector sum of those two components. The −5t² term in y also secretly reveals the gravity: matching ½gt² gives g = 10 m s⁻², confirming this is a normal projectile.
Formula Component velocities vx = dx/dt, vy = dy/dt; resultant speed v = √(vx² + vy²). Given x = 6t and y = 8t − 5t² (metres, t in s). Differentiating a·t gives a constant a; differentiating −5t² gives −10t, which vanishes at t = 0.

3. Evaluate at the launch instant t = 0: vx = 6 m s⁻¹, vy = 8 − 10(0) = 8 m s⁻¹.

Concept The same object is launched from the top of a tower three ways — thrown up (time t₁), thrown down with equal speed (time t₂), and simply released (time t) — all covering the same height h to the ground. Writing one displacement equation for each and eliminating both the unknown launch speed u and the height h leaves a strikingly clean result: the release time is the geometric mean of the other two, t = √(t₁t₂). It is a symmetry gem worth memorising.
Formula Down positive, origin at the tower top, drop height h. Thrown up: h = −u·t₁ + ½g·t₁²; thrown down: h = u·t₂ + ½g·t₂²; released: h = ½g·t². Eliminating u and h gives t = √(t₁·t₂). Assumptions: same launch speed u up and down, air resistance neglected, g constant.
Solution
1. Add the up and down equations: 2h = ½g(t₁² + t₂²) − u(t₁ − t₂).
2. Subtract them: 0 = ½g(t₁² − t₂²) − u(t₁ + t₂) ⇒ u = ½g(t₁ − t₂).This pins u to the two times, ready to substitute out.
3. Put u back into step 1: 2h = ½g(t₁² + t₂²) − ½g(t₁ − t₂)² = ½g[2t₁t₂] ⇒ h = ½g·t₁t₂.
4. But for release from rest h = ½g·t², so ½g·t² = ½g·t₁t₂ ⇒ t² = t₁t₂ ⇒ t = √(t₁t₂).
5. Substitute the data t₁ = t₂ = 4 s: t = √(4 × 4) = √16 = 4 s.
6. Sanity: t₁ = t₂ forces u = ½g(t₁ − t₂) = 0 — the "up" and "down" throws had zero speed, i.e. they were also drops, so of course t = t₁ = t₂ = 4 s ✓.

Concept This problem gives two speeds and the separation between two points, and asks for a speed — with no mention of time anywhere. Treat point A as the "start" and point B as the "end" of one leg of the journey: the stone enters that leg at speed v, climbs 30 m against gravity, and leaves it at v/2. Formula vB² = vA² − 2·g·s, taking upward as positive so a = −g = −9.8 m s⁻². Here vA = v (m s⁻¹), vB = v/2 (m s⁻¹), s = +30 m (B is above A). Concept Acceleration due to gravity always points downward, toward the Earth. Its algebraic sign is not a physical property but a bookkeeping choice that depends on which direction we call positive. 2. Gravity pulls the body toward the ground — the downward direction — which is the negative direction on this axis.
3. Therefore while the object moves upward, g must be entered with a minus sign: a = −g.A positive u and a negative a give a decreasing speed — physically correct for a rising body.
4. Check the alternatives: options claiming "positive" would make the body speed up as it rises, contradicting observation — reject.
5. Note the symmetry: on the way down, if we keep the same up-positive axis, g is still −g, but now velocity is also negative, so the falling body speeds up — again consistent.

1. Test (a): "Area under the a–t graph from 0 to t equals −gt, the change in velocity." The graph is a rectangle of height −g (m s⁻²) and width t (s), so its area = (−g)(t) = −g·t (m s⁻¹).
2. Confirm this really is Δv: from v = u − g·t we get v − u = −g·t ✓ — exactly the area. Both the value and the interpretation in the statement are right. (a) is CORRECT.Area below the t-axis counts as negative, which is why the velocity decreases — the stone loses 9.8 m s⁻¹ of upward velocity every second.
3. Test (b): "Time to reach maximum height equals the intercept where the a–t line meets zero." The a–t line is the horizontal line a = −g. A horizontal line at height −9.8 m s⁻² is parallel to the t-axis and never touches it — there is no such intercept.
4. So the statement is meaningless as stated: the apex is at ta = u/g, but that instant is where the v–t graph crosses zero, not the a–t graph. (b) is INCORRECT ✗.This is the classic confusion between the two graphs. At the apex the velocity is zero while the acceleration is still −9.8 m s⁻² — if a really did hit zero there, the stone would hang motionless in mid-air.
5. Test (c): "Slope of the a–t graph equals jerk; here it is zero for all t." Jerk is defined as da/dt (m s⁻³). Since a = −g is a constant, da/dt = 0 m s⁻³ for every t.
6. Geometrically the same thing: a horizontal line has zero slope. Both halves of the statement — the definition and the value — check out. (c) is CORRECT ✓.Zero jerk is precisely what "uniformly accelerated motion" means: the acceleration itself never changes.
7. Test (d): "Area of the a–t graph from launch to landing is −2u." The full flight lasts T = 2u ÷ g (s), so the rectangle's area = (−g) × T = (−g)(2u ÷ g) = −2u (m s⁻¹) — the g cancels.
8. Verify it is the true Δv over the flight: the stone leaves at +u m s⁻¹ and lands at −u m s⁻¹ (same speed, opposite direction), so Δv = (−u) − (+u) = −2u ✓. (d) is CORRECT ✓.A lovely consistency check: the graph's area independently reproduces the up/down speed symmetry.
9. Collect the verdicts: (a), (c) and (d) are correct; (b) is false.
