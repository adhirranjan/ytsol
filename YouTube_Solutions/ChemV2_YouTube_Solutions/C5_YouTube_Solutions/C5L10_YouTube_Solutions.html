1Hydrogen bonding occurs when hydrogen is bonded to which element? (Level-1) (a) Chlorine (b) Fluorine (c) Bromine (d) IodineCondition for H-bondingHydrogen bonding — when it forms, and its two types🟡 SimilarbFluorine
Concept A hydrogen bond is not a normal covalent bond — it is an electrostatic attraction that only switches on when hydrogen carries a large enough partial positive charge. That happens only if H is covalently attached to a small, highly electronegative atom, and in practice the list is exactly three: F, O, N. Two conditions must hold together: high electronegativity (to pull the shared pair away and expose the bare proton) and small size (to concentrate the resulting charge). Among the four halogens offered, only fluorine satisfies both.
Key relation H—Xδ− … H—Xδ− forms when EN(X) is high AND size(X) is small. Pauling electronegativities: F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0, Cl 3.0, Br 2.8, I 2.5. Notice Cl ties with N on electronegativity but is far bigger, so its charge density is too low — that is why HCl does not hydrogen bond.
Solution
1. Write the criterion the book gives: "If a hydrogen atom is bonded to a highly electronegative element such as fluorine, nitrogen, oxygen, a slight positive charge develops on the H-atom."
2. Score the four options on electronegativity and size:
  • (a) Chlorine — EN 3.0 is respectable, but the atom is large (99 pm), so charge density is low ⇒ no true H-bond.
  • (b) Fluorine — EN 4.0, the highest of all elements, and the smallest halogen (64 pm) ⇒ strongest H-bond known.
  • (c) Bromine — EN 2.8, still bigger ⇒ no.
  • (d) Iodine — EN 2.5, biggest ⇒ definitely no.
3. The evidence is in the boiling points printed in the theory: HF 19.4 °C but HCl −85 °C and HBr −67.1 °C.HF is a liquid near room temperature while its heavier relatives are gases — a 100-degree anomaly that only H-bonding explains.
 H—X    EN(X)   size(X)/pm   charge on H    H-bond ?   b.p./°C
 H—F     4.0        64        large δ+       YES        +19.4   <-- answer (b)
 H—Cl    3.0        99        small δ+       no         −85
 H—Br    2.8       114        smaller        no         −67.1
 H—I     2.5       133        smallest       no         −35
 rule    H-bond needs BOTH high EN and small size  →  F, O, N only
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the three-letter code FON (F, O, N) — every hydrogen-bonding question in this chapter is settled by asking "is H attached to F, O or N?". Trap: chlorine has the same electronegativity as nitrogen (3.0), so option (a) looks defensible; the tie-breaker is size, and that single word is what the examiner is testing.
Answer: (b) Fluorine.
2Which compound has the highest boiling point due to hydrogen bonding? (Level-2) (a) H₂S (b) HBr (c) H₂O (d) PH₃H-bonding and boiling pointBoiling points of the hydrides — where H-bonding breaks the trend🟡 SimilarcH₂O
Concept Boiling means tearing molecules apart from one another, so the boiling point measures the strength of the intermolecular force, not the strength of the bonds inside the molecule. Ordinary van der Waals forces grow with molecular mass, giving a smooth upward trend down a group. A hydrogen bond is roughly ten times stronger than a van der Waals interaction, so the one hydride in a group that can hydrogen bond jumps far above the trend line. In this list only H₂O has H attached to an F/O/N atom.
Key relation b.p. ∝ (strength of intermolecular force) × (number of such interactions per molecule). Water wins on both counts: O is small and very electronegative (strong bonds), and each H₂O has 2 donor H's + 2 lone pairs = up to 4 H-bonds per molecule.
Solution
1. Check each candidate for the FON rule:
  • (a) H₂S — H on sulphur; S is large and EN 2.5 ⇒ no H-bond, only dipole–dipole. b.p. −59.6 °C.
  • (b) HBr — H on bromine, EN 2.8 ⇒ no H-bond. b.p. −67.1 °C.
  • (c) H₂O — H on oxygen, EN 3.5 and tiny ⇒ extensive H-bonding. b.p. +100 °C.
  • (d) PH₃ — H on phosphorus, EN 2.1 ⇒ no H-bond. b.p. −85 °C.
2. Compare magnitudes: water boils about 160 degrees above H₂S even though H₂S is the heavier molecule (34 vs 18 g mol⁻¹).If mass alone governed the boiling point, H₂S would boil higher — the reversal is the fingerprint of hydrogen bonding.
3. So option (c) is the only one that can hydrogen bond, and it is the only one that is a liquid at room temperature.
 compound   M/g mol⁻¹   H on ?   H-bond   b.p./°C
 PH₃          34         P        no       −85
 H₂S          34         S        no       −59.6
 HBr          81         Br       no       −67.1
 H₂O          18         O        YES      +100     <-- answer (c)
 note: H₂O is the LIGHTEST yet boils HIGHEST ⇒ force, not mass, decides
⚡ JEE Trick In any "highest boiling point" list, first cross out everything that cannot hydrogen bond; if exactly one survivor remains you are done without any numbers. Trap: option (b) HBr has by far the largest molar mass and tempts you into a "heavier boils higher" answer — that rule only applies within a set of non-hydrogen-bonded molecules.
Answer: (c) H₂O.
3Which covalent compound dissolves in water due to hydrogen bonding with water molecules? (Level-3) (a) Carbon tetrachloride (b) Ethanol (c) Benzene (d) Carbon disulfideSolubility via H-bondingWhy ethanol is completely miscible with water — hydrogen bonding explained🟢 ExactbEthanol
Concept Water dissolves a covalent substance only if the solute can replace the water–water hydrogen bonds it breaks with new solute–water hydrogen bonds. Dissolving costs energy (you must prise apart the H-bonded water network); the cost is repaid only if the incoming molecule carries an —OH, —NH or C=O group that can itself H-bond. A molecule made only of C, H and halogen or sulphur has nothing to offer and stays out. This is the molecular meaning of "like dissolves like".
Key relation Solubility requires |ΔH(solute–water H-bonds formed)| ≳ |ΔH(water–water H-bonds broken)|. The book states it directly: "Covalent compounds do not dissolve in water. However, the compounds which can form hydrogen bonds with water are dissolved in it — ethanol, ammonia, amines, lower aldehydes and ketones."
Solution
1. Look for an H attached to F/O/N, or a lone pair on O/N able to accept an H-bond from water.
2. Test each option:
  • (a) CCl₄ — tetrahedral, symmetric, μ = 0; no O/N/F, no polar H ⇒ insoluble.
  • (b) C₂H₅OH — the —OH group both donates (its H) and accepts (O lone pairs) hydrogen bonds ⇒ completely miscible with water.
  • (c) C₆H₆ — pure hydrocarbon, non-polar ⇒ insoluble (floats as a separate layer).
  • (d) CS₂ — linear S=C=S, symmetric, μ = 0 ⇒ insoluble.
3. Draw the interaction that makes ethanol special: R—O—H … O(H)—H and H—O(H) … O(R)—H, i.e. two-way bonding to the solvent.Ethane, C₂H₆, is the control experiment — replace the OH by H and solubility collapses, proving the OH group is what matters.
 compound   formula     H-bond donor   H-bond acceptor   soluble in water?
 CCl₄       CCl₄            no              no                no
 ethanol    C₂H₅OH         YES (O—H)      YES (O lp)          YES  <-- answer (b)
 benzene    C₆H₆            no              no                no
 CS₂        S=C=S           no              no (weak)         no
 rule: need an —OH / —NH / C=O to plug into the water network
⚡ JEE Trick Scan the formula for O or N carrying (or next to) hydrogen — if it is absent, the compound is insoluble, no matter how "polar" it looks. Trap: CS₂ and CCl₄ contain very electronegative-looking atoms, but the molecules are symmetric, so their bond dipoles cancel and they cannot bond to water at all.
Answer: (b) Ethanol.
4Ice floats on water because it: (Level-4) (a) Has higher density than water (b) Forms an open cage-like H-bonded structure (c) Expands on melting (d) Has shorter O—H bondsStructure of iceWhy ice floats and why water expands on freezing🟢 ExactbForms an open cage-like H-bonded structure
Concept Almost every substance contracts on freezing, because the molecules pack more efficiently in the solid. Water is the famous exception. In ice each oxygen is tetrahedrally linked to four neighbours through hydrogen bonds — two it donates and two it accepts — and a tetrahedral network is geometrically open: it contains large hexagonal holes. Those holes are empty space, so a given volume of ice contains fewer molecules than the same volume of liquid water, and density = mass/volume falls.
Key relation ρ = m / V. Freezing forces the H-bonds to their ideal directions, so V increases by ≈ 9 % and ρ(ice) = 0.917 g cm⁻³ < ρ(water) = 1.000 g cm⁻³. By Archimedes, anything less dense than the liquid floats — ice sits with about 9/10 of its volume submerged.
Solution
1. In liquid water the H-bond network is disordered and constantly breaking, so molecules can slip into the gaps and pack closely.
2. On freezing, every molecule commits to 4 hydrogen bonds in fixed tetrahedral directions, building the "cage" drawn in the theory notes.The cages are the hexagonal channels that also give snowflakes their six-fold symmetry.
3. Fixed directions ⇒ trapped empty space ⇒ volume expands ⇒ density falls ⇒ ice floats. That is option (b).
4. Eliminate the rest: (a) is the exact opposite — higher density would sink; (c) is backwards, ice contracts on melting (which is why the volume of water decreases from 0 °C to 4 °C); (d) O—H bond length is an intramolecular quantity and has nothing to do with packing.
 phase    H-bonds per molecule   arrangement        density/g cm⁻³
 water    ≈3.4 (fluctuating)     close, disordered      1.000
 ice       4 (fixed)             open tetrahedral       0.917   <-- answer (b)
          cages ⇒ V ↑ ≈ 9 %  ⇒  ρ ↓  ⇒  floats (≈ 9/10 submerged)
 melting:  cages collapse  ⇒  V ↓  (ice CONTRACTS on melting, not expands)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the pair of numbers 0.917 and 1.000 and the phrase "open cage, 4 H-bonds, tetrahedral" — they answer every ice question, including "why does water have maximum density at 4 °C?" (the last cages break just above 0 °C). Trap: option (c) "expands on melting" reads plausibly, but expansion happens on freezing; melting collapses the cages and shrinks the volume.
Answer: (b) Forms an open cage-like H-bonded structure.
5HF shows hydrogen bonding because: (Level-5) (a) HF has the longest H—X bond (b) Fluorine is highly electronegative (c) HF has very weak polarity (d) Fluorine has the largest atomic sizeOrigin of H-bond in HFWhy HCl shows no hydrogen bonding although Cl is electronegative🟡 SimilarbFluorine is highly electronegative
Concept The hydrogen bond is born from bond polarity. Fluorine, with the highest electronegativity of any element (4.0), drags the shared electron pair strongly towards itself. Because hydrogen has no inner shell to hide behind, what is left on the H end is very nearly a bare proton — an intense, unshielded positive charge that can reach out and grip a lone pair on the fluorine of a neighbouring molecule. High electronegativity is therefore the cause; the large dipole moment and the high boiling point are the consequences.
Key relation Hδ+—Fδ− ⋯ Hδ+—Fδ− ⋯ H—F with δ ∝ ΔEN = EN(F) − EN(H) = 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9. Compare HCl: ΔEN = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, less than half, and Cl is 50 % larger — the charge is both smaller and more spread out, so no H-bond forms.
Solution
1. State the requirement: a large δ+ on hydrogen, which needs a partner atom of high electronegativity and small size.
2. Fluorine is the champion on both counts (EN 4.0; covalent radius 64 pm), so HF is the most polar hydrogen halide ⇒ option (b).
3. Kill the distractors one by one:
  • (a) False — H—F is the shortest H—X bond (92 pm), since F is the smallest halogen; the order is HI > HBr > HCl > HF.
  • (c) False — HF has the strongest polarity of the halogen acids, not the weakest; this option contradicts the very mechanism.
  • (d) False — F has the smallest atomic size in group 17; large size would destroy H-bonding, as it does in HCl, HBr and HI.
4. Confirm with data: HF boils at 19.4 °C, HCl at −85 °C — a 104-degree gap created purely by fluorine's electronegativity.
 H—X   EN(X)   ΔEN vs H   d(H—X)/pm   H-bonding   b.p./°C
 H—F    4.0      1.9          92         YES        +19.4   <-- answer (b)
 H—Cl   3.0      0.9         127         no         −85
 H—Br   2.8      0.7         141         no         −67.1
 H—I    2.5      0.4         161         no         −35
 cause: high EN(F) ⇒ big δ+ on H ⇒ H⋯F attraction ⇒ molecules associate
⚡ JEE Trick Options (a) and (d) both claim fluorine is big or long — fluorine is the smallest reactive non-metal, so any option describing it as large is an instant reject. That single observation kills half the choices before you think about chemistry at all. Trap: option (c) inverts the true statement; examiners love "weak polarity" for the most polar molecule in the set.

1Display electron distribution around the oxygen atom in water molecule and state shape of the molecule, also write H—O—H bond angle.Shape of H₂O · VSEPRH₂O molecular geometry, shape and bond angle (104.5°)🟢 Exact—
Concept To describe a molecule's shape you must separate two different pictures: the electron-pair geometry (how all pairs, bonding and lone, arrange themselves) and the molecular shape (where the atoms actually sit). VSEPR says all pairs repel and spread as far apart as possible; the count of pairs is captured by the steric number. Lone pairs are held by one nucleus only, so they are fatter and push harder than bond pairs — which is why real angles fall below the ideal value.
Key relation steric number = (number of σ bonds) + (number of lone pairs); SN 4 ⇒ sp³ hybridisation ⇒ tetrahedral electron geometry (ideal 109°28′). Repulsion order: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp. Two lone pairs squeeze the two O—H bonds together, so the angle drops from 109.5° to the observed 104.5°.
Solution
1. Electron count on oxygen: O is 1s²2s²2p⁴, so it brings 6 valence electrons. Two of them are shared with the two hydrogens.
2. Electron distribution: that gives 2 bond pairs (the two O—H bonds) and 2 lone pairs left on oxygen — a total of 4 electron pairs, i.e. 8 electrons, a complete octet.
3. Steric number: SN = 2 σ bonds + 2 lone pairs = 4 ⇒ oxygen is sp³ hybridised and the four pairs point to the corners of a tetrahedron.
4. Shape: we name the shape from the atoms only. With two corners occupied by invisible lone pairs, the three atoms H—O—H form a bent (angular, V-shaped) molecule.
5. Bond angle: the ideal tetrahedral angle is 109°28′, but the two lone pairs repel more strongly, compressing it to H—O—H = 104.5° (often quoted 104°27′ or 104°31′).Compare NH₃ with one lone pair: 107°; and CH₄ with none: 109.5°. Each extra lone pair costs roughly 2.5°.
6. Consequence: because the molecule is bent, the two O—H bond dipoles do not cancel, so water has a resultant dipole moment of 1.84 D — the reason it is a polar solvent and hydrogen bonds so well.

 shared with 2 H        2   ⇒  2 BOND PAIRS
 remaining              4   ⇒  2 LONE PAIRS
 steric number  SN = 2 bp + 2 lp = 4      ⇒  sp³ hybrid, tetrahedral e⁻ geometry
 shape (atoms only)     BENT / ANGULAR / V-shaped
 ideal angle            109°28′
 lp–lp and lp–bp repulsion  ⇒  compressed
 OBSERVED  H—O—H  =     104.5°            <-- required answer
 comparison   CH₄ 109.5° (0 lp) > NH₃ 107° (1 lp) > H₂O 104.5° (2 lp)
 net dipole   μ = 1.84 D  (bond dipoles do not cancel in a bent shape)
⚡ JEE Trick Compute the steric number first — SN = σ bonds + lone pairs — then read hybridisation straight off (2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d²). Memorise the descending trio 109.5° / 107° / 104.5° for CH₄ / NH₃ / H₂O; it is asked in almost every shape question. Trap: writing "tetrahedral" as the shape of water — tetrahedral describes the electron pairs; the molecule itself is bent.
Key point: oxygen carries 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs (SN 4, sp³); the molecule is bent / angular (V-shaped) and the H—O—H bond angle is 104.5°, reduced from 109°28′ by lone-pair repulsion.
2Define: (a) Bond enthalpy (b) Bond lengthBond parameters — definitionsBond energy, bond dissociation enthalpy and mean bond enthalpy🟡 Similar—
Concept Bond enthalpy and bond length are the two headline bond parameters, and they measure opposite faces of the same thing: how tightly two nuclei are held. Enthalpy is the energy cost of separating them; length is the distance at which the attraction and repulsion balance. They are inversely related — the shorter the bond, the more strongly the nuclei share the electron cloud, and the more energy it takes to break.
Definitions Bond enthalpy — the amount of energy required to break one mole of bonds of a particular type in the gaseous state, to give gaseous atoms. Units kJ mol⁻¹; always positive (endothermic). e.g. H₂(g) → 2H(g), ΔH = +435.8 kJ mol⁻¹. For polyatomic molecules such as H₂O the four successive steps differ, so we quote the mean bond enthalpy. Bond length — the equilibrium distance between the centres of the nuclei of two bonded atoms. Units Å (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m) or pm (1 pm = 10⁻¹² m); measured by X-ray/electron diffraction or spectroscopy.
Solution
1. (a) Bond enthalpy / bond dissociation enthalpy. Write the defining equation with everything gaseous, because only then is the number free of solvation and lattice effects.A—B(g) → A(g) + B(g), ΔH = bond enthalpy. Breaking a bond always absorbs energy, so the sign is +.
2. Note the "mean" refinement: in H₂O, breaking the first O—H needs 502 kJ mol⁻¹ and the second 427 kJ mol⁻¹, so the mean O—H bond enthalpy is (502 + 427)/2 = 464.5 kJ mol⁻¹.
3. (b) Bond length. It is an equilibrium (average) internuclear distance, since the atoms vibrate continuously.
4. Add the additivity rule the chapter uses: for a covalent molecule AB, d = rA + rB, the sum of the covalent radii; for an ionic compound, d = r+ + r−.
5. For a homonuclear diatomic the covalent radius is exactly half the bond length: rCl = d(Cl—Cl)/2.
6. Link the two definitions: bond enthalpy ↑ as bond length ↓ (C≡C 837 kJ mol⁻¹ at 120 pm vs C—C 347 kJ mol⁻¹ at 154 pm).

                  in the GASEOUS state → gaseous atoms                      O—H  464 (mean)
                  A—B(g) → A(g) + B(g),  ΔH = +ve                           C—C  347

                  of the two bonded atoms                                   Cl—Cl 198 pm

⚡ JEE Trick Three words earn the mark for bond enthalpy: "one mole", "gaseous state", "to break"; drop any one and the definition is incomplete. For bond length the key word is "equilibrium" (or "average") internuclear distance. Trap: quoting bond enthalpy as negative — formation releases energy, but the definition is written for breaking, so it is positive.
Key point: Bond enthalpy = energy needed to break one mole of a given bond in the gaseous state (kJ mol⁻¹, positive; use the mean value for polyatomics). Bond length = equilibrium internuclear distance between two bonded atoms (Å/pm), equal to the sum of the covalent radii, rA + rB.
3Explain the term : Resonance energy.Resonance energyWhat is resonance? Define resonance energy — Class 11 chemical bonding🟢 Exact—
Concept When a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule, we draw several canonical (resonance) structures that differ only in the placement of electrons, and the real molecule is a resonance hybrid of them. The hybrid is never merely an average — delocalising the electrons over more nuclei always lowers the energy. The size of that stabilisation is the resonance energy, and it is measured, not guessed: you compare the real molecule's enthalpy with the value calculated for the most stable single canonical form.
Definition Resonance energy = E(most stable canonical structure) − E(actual resonance hybrid) Because the hybrid lies lower, the difference is positive and is quoted as a stabilisation, e.g. benzene ≈ 150 kJ mol⁻¹, and it is also called delocalisation energy.
Solution
1. Define resonance first: the representation of a molecule by two or more Lewis structures that differ only in the positions of electrons, not of nuclei.
2. State that the true structure is the resonance hybrid, which cannot be drawn as any one canonical form and is more stable than every one of them.
3. Define the quantity: the difference in energy between the resonance hybrid and the most stable canonical structure is the resonance energy (delocalisation energy).
4. Give the standard illustration with numbers — benzene:Predicted heat of hydrogenation for a hypothetical cyclohexatriene = 3 × 120 = 360 kJ mol⁻¹, but the measured value for benzene is only 208 kJ mol⁻¹. Resonance energy = 360 − 208 = 152 kJ mol⁻¹.
5. Add the structural evidence: in benzene all six C—C bonds are 139 pm, between a single bond (154 pm) and a double bond (134 pm) — proof that the electrons are delocalised, not fixed.
6. Note the rule of thumb: the greater the number of (equivalent) canonical forms, the greater the resonance energy and the greater the stability — CO₃²⁻ (3 equivalent forms) is very stable for this reason.

⚡ JEE Trick Say the sentence "energy difference between the resonance hybrid and the most stable canonical structure" — those exact words score the mark, and adding the benzene figure (≈150 kJ mol⁻¹) secures it. Trap: describing the hybrid as a rapid oscillation between canonical forms; the molecule is a single, unchanging structure, and the canonical forms have no independent existence.
Key point: Resonance energy (delocalisation energy) is the difference in energy between the actual resonance hybrid and the most stable canonical structure; it measures the extra stability gained by electron delocalisation — about 152 kJ mol⁻¹ for benzene.
4Write the formula to calculate bond order of a molecule.Bond order formulaHow to calculate bond order — (Nb − Na)/2 with worked examples🟢 Exact—
Concept Bond order counts how many net electron pairs are holding two nuclei together. In molecular orbital theory the atomic orbitals combine into bonding MOs (which pull the nuclei together) and antibonding MOs (which push them apart), and an electron in an antibonding orbital cancels the effect of one in a bonding orbital. So you fill the MO diagram by the Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules, count the two populations, take the difference, and halve it because it takes a pair to make one bond.
Formula Bond order = (Nb − Na) / 2 where Nb = number of electrons in bonding molecular orbitals and Na = number of electrons in antibonding molecular orbitals. A positive bond order means the species exists; zero (as in He₂) means it does not. For resonance systems the alternative form is bond order = (total bonds between the two atoms across all canonical forms) ÷ (number of canonical forms).
Solution
1. Write the MO filling order for second-row diatomics (up to N₂): σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < π2px = π2py < σ2pz < π*2px = π*2py < σ*2pz. For O₂ and F₂, σ2pz drops below the π orbitals.
2. Fill in the total number of electrons, then count Nb and Na.
3. Apply BO = (Nb − Na)/2.
4. Worked example — N₂ (14 e⁻): Nb = 10, Na = 4 ⇒ BO = (10 − 4)/2 = 3, a triple bond, diamagnetic.
5. Worked example — O₂ (16 e⁻): Nb = 10, Na = 6 ⇒ BO = (10 − 6)/2 = 2, and the two π* electrons are unpaired, so O₂ is paramagnetic — MO theory's greatest success.
6. State the consequences: higher bond order ⇒ shorter bond, higher bond enthalpy, greater stability.
 FORMULA        bond order = (N_b − N_a) / 2

 species   total e⁻   N_b   N_a   (N_b−N_a)/2   bond    magnetic
 H₂          2         2     0    (2−0)/2 = 1   single  diamagnetic
 He₂         4         2     2    (2−2)/2 = 0   none    does not exist
 N₂         14        10     4    (10−4)/2 = 3  triple  diamagnetic
 O₂         16        10     6    (10−6)/2 = 2  double  PARAMAGNETIC (2 unpaired in π*)
 O₂⁺        15        10     5    (10−5)/2 = 2.5        stronger, shorter than O₂
 F₂         18        10     8    (10−8)/2 = 1  single  diamagnetic
 trend: bond order ↑  ⇒  bond length ↓  ⇒  bond enthalpy ↑     <-- required formula
⚡ JEE Trick For homonuclear diatomics of the second period there is a two-second shortcut: bond order = |8 − n|/2 for 2 ≤ n ≤ 6 valence electrons and (n − 8)/2 pattern beyond — but the safe habit is to fill the diagram and count. Always add the magnetic verdict: any unpaired electron ⇒ paramagnetic, which is how O₂ questions are really scored. Trap: forgetting to divide by 2, which turns N₂'s bond order into 6.
Key point: Bond order = (Nb − Na)/2, where Nb and Na are the numbers of electrons in bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals respectively (e.g. N₂ = (10 − 4)/2 = 3).

1Hydrogen bonding occurs when hydrogen is bonded to: (a) Carbon (b) Hydrogen (c) Fluorine, Oxygen, or Nitrogen (d) ChlorineThe F–O–N ruleHydrogen bonding — condition, intermolecular and intramolecular types🟡 SimilarcFluorine, Oxygen, or Nitrogen
Concept Only three elements are electronegative enough and small enough to strip hydrogen bare: fluorine, oxygen and nitrogen. The two requirements act together. Electronegativity sets how much charge is pulled off the hydrogen; small size decides how concentrated that charge is, and how closely the partner's lone pair can approach. Chlorine passes the first test (EN 3.0, equal to nitrogen) but fails the second badly, which is exactly why HCl is a gas at −85 °C while NH₃ is a liquid below −33 °C.
Key relation X—H ⋯ Y requires X, Y ∈ {F, O, N}, with charge density ∝ EN(X) / size(X). Both X (the donor, which supplies the polarised H) and Y (the acceptor, which supplies the lone pair) must come from this set — that is why H₂O⋯H₂O, HF⋯HF and NH₃⋯H₂O all work.
Solution

Concept A hydrogen bond is not a new kind of bond — it is an electrostatic consequence of an already existing polar covalent bond. When H is joined to F, O or N, the shared pair is pulled hard towards the electronegative partner, so hydrogen is left almost bare: it has no inner shell to screen its nucleus, so its δ+ is unusually concentrated. That naked δ+ proton can then reach across to a lone pair on the F/O/N of a neighbouring molecule. The chain of cause and effect is therefore electron displacement → δ+ on H → electrostatic attraction to a lone pair, and option (a) states exactly the first, causal step.
Definition Xδ−—Hδ+ ⋯ Yδ− with X, Y ∈ {F, O, N}. Two conditions must both hold, and both trace back to the same displacement: the donor atom X must be highly electronegative (to create the δ+), and the acceptor Y must be small with a lone pair (so the δ+ can approach closely). Typical strength ≈ 10–40 kJ mol⁻¹, roughly one-tenth of the covalent H—X bond it sits on.
Solution
1. Start from the covalent bond H—F. ΔEN = 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9, so the bonding pair is displaced towards F; the molecule becomes Hδ+—Fδ−.This is the "displacement of electrons towards the more electronegative atom" named in option (a) — the wording is lifted straight from the definition in the theory.
2. Hydrogen is now a proton with essentially no electron cloud of its own (it had only one electron, and that is now largely on F). Its charge density is enormous compared with any other δ+ atom.
3. This δ+ hydrogen is attracted to a lone pair on the F of the next molecule ⇒ the hydrogen bond H—F ⋯ H—F. So the cause is step 1 ⇒ option (a).
4. Eliminate the distractors:
  • (b) Reverses the direction of the shift. Electrons never move towards hydrogen in a H-bonded system — H is the least electronegative partner here (EN 2.1 against 4.0, 3.5, 3.0). If electrons moved to H it would carry δ− and could not attract a lone pair at all.
  • (c) Describes a bond between the hydrogens of two molecules. That is impossible: both are δ+ and would repel. The bond runs from the H of one molecule to the electronegative atom of the other.
  • (d) "An attractive force which binds hydrogen atoms together" is again H-to-H, and it is a restatement, not a cause — it says that there is attraction without saying why. The stem asks "formed due to", i.e. for the mechanism.

Concept Almost every substance contracts on freezing, because the molecules lose kinetic energy and pack more tightly. Water is the famous exception, and hydrogen bonding is the reason. A hydrogen bond is directional: it must point straight at a lone pair. To satisfy all four of its hydrogen bonds simultaneously (two as donor, two as acceptor), each water molecule in ice must sit at the centre of a tetrahedron. That geometry leaves large hexagonal holes, so ice is a scaffold with empty rooms — fewer molecules per millilitre than liquid water, hence lower density, hence it floats.
Key relation ρ = m / V; ρ(ice, 0 °C) = 0.917 g cm⁻³ against ρ(water, 0 °C) = 0.9998 g cm⁻³, so ice is about 9 % less dense and roughly one-ninth of a floating berg stands above the surface. A bond with a modest ΔEN but a very long bond can beat a bond with a large ΔEN and a short one. LiI is the extreme case: ΔEN = 2.5 − 1.0 = 1.5 and a bond length of 239 pm, so it towers over everything else in the list.

Concept This is the classic paradox of the chapter: fluorine is the most electronegative element, so the individual F—H⋯F bond is the strongest hydrogen bond known (≈29–40 kJ mol⁻¹ against ≈21 for O—H⋯O), yet water boils 80 degrees higher. The resolution is bookkeeping: boiling point depends on the total cohesive energy per molecule, which is strength multiplied by number. HF has one donor hydrogen and three acceptor lone pairs — badly mismatched — so it can only form chains averaging about two hydrogen bonds per molecule. Water has two donors and two acceptors, a perfect match, so it forms a three-dimensional network of four. Four medium bonds beat two strong ones.
Key relation cohesion ≈ n × EH-bond; H₂O: 4 × 21 ≈ 84, HF: 2 × 29 ≈ 58 (arbitrary units, per molecule before halving for sharing). This is why ΔHvap(H₂O) = 40.7 kJ mol⁻¹ but ΔHvap(HF) = only 25.2 kJ mol⁻¹, and hence b.p. 100 °C versus 19.4 °C.

10[ONE OR MORE THAN ONE OPTION MAY BE CORRECT] How does the hydrogen bond affect the boiling point of water and solubility of compound? (a) Hydrogen bonding increases the boiling point of water (b) Water dissolves compounds that can form hydrogen bonds with it (c) Alcohols dissolve in water due to hydrogen bonding with water (d) Hydrogen bonding decreases the boiling point of waterEffects of H-bondingHydrogen bonding and its effect on boiling point and solubility🟡 Similarabc(a), (b) and (c) are correct; (d) is wrong
Concept Intermolecular hydrogen bonding does one thing — it holds molecules together — and both effects asked about follow from that. Boiling means separating molecules completely, so extra attraction means more energy is needed and the boiling point rises. Dissolving means mixing two substances, which succeeds when the new solute–solvent attractions repay the solvent–solvent attractions that had to be broken; a solute that can hydrogen bond with water repays them exactly, so it dissolves. Options (a), (b) and (c) are three faces of this single idea, while (d) states its opposite and is therefore false.
Key relation stronger intermolecular attraction ⇒ higher b.p. and "like dissolves like" ⇒ H-bonding solutes dissolve in H-bonding solvents. The only case where hydrogen bonding lowers a boiling point is intramolecular bonding, as in o-nitrophenol, because the bond is used up inside one molecule and no association occurs. The stem here concerns water, which is intermolecularly bonded.
Solution
1. (a) Hydrogen bonding increases the boiling point of water — true. Each molecule is held by four H-bonds and all must be broken to vaporise, giving ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ mol⁻¹ and a boiling point of +100 °C instead of the ≈−80 °C the mass trend predicts. ✓
2. (b) Water dissolves compounds that can form hydrogen bonds with it — true. This is the chapter's solubility rule: ammonia, amines, alcohols, sugars, carboxylic acids and lower aldehydes and ketones all dissolve, while hydrocarbons and CCl₄ do not. ✓
3. (c) Alcohols dissolve in water due to hydrogen bonding with water — true, and it is the standard example of (b). The —OH group both donates (through its polarised O—H) and accepts (through oxygen's lone pairs), so ethanol slots into the water network and is completely miscible; ethane, differing only by the missing oxygen, is insoluble. ✓
4. (d) Hydrogen bonding decreases the boiling point of water — false, and it directly contradicts (a). Two mutually exclusive options cannot both be right, and (a) is the one supported by every measurement.The lowering effect belongs to intramolecular hydrogen bonding — o-nitrophenol boils at 214 °C against p-nitrophenol's 279 °C — but water forms no intramolecular H-bond, having only one oxygen. ✗
