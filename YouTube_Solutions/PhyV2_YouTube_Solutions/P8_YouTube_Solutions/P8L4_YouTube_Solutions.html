1The total work done on a particle is equal to the change in its kinetic energy: (a) always (b) only if the forces acting on it are conservative (c) only if gravitational force alone acts on it (d) only if elastic force alone acts on it — (Level-1)Validity of work–energy theoremThe total work done on a particle is equal to the change in its kinetic energy (a) always (b) only if🟢 Exactaalways
Concept The work–energy theorem is not an energy-conservation statement — it is Newton's second law integrated once over displacement. Because nothing in that integration cares whether a force is conservative, dissipative, internal or external, the theorem holds for every force. The trap is to confuse it with ΔKE + ΔPE = 0, which really does need conservative forces only.
Formula Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf² − ½mvi² — W in joules (J), m in kg, v in m/s. Derivation: Fnet = m dv/dt (N), multiply by dx (m) and use dx/dt = v ⇒ Fnet dx = m v dv. Both sides are N·m = J, so the identity is dimensionally exact and is valid whatever the physical origin of Fnet.
Solution
1. Start with Newton's second law for the resultant force: Fnet = m a = m dv/dt (N)."Resultant" means the vector sum of all forces — friction, tension, spring, gravity, applied — with no restriction on their type.
2. Multiply both sides by the displacement element dx (m) and use the chain rule dv/dt = (dv/dx)(dx/dt) = v dv/dx:
  Fnet dx = m v dv
3. Integrate from the initial to the final state:
  ∫ Fnet dx = ∫vivf m v dv = ½mvf² − ½mvi²
4. The left side is by definition the total work Wnet done by all forces (J). Hence Wnet = ΔKE, always.No step used "the force is conservative" or "the force is gravity", so options (b), (c), (d) are unnecessary restrictions.
5. Cross-check with a dissipative case: a 2 kg block sliding at 3 m/s stopped by friction. Wfriction = 0 − ½(2)(3)² = −9 J, and indeed the friction force times distance equals −9 J. Friction is non-conservative, yet the theorem still balances.

   ─────────────────────          ──────────────
    F_net = m dv/dt
    ×dx →  F_net dx = m v dv
    ∫    →  W_net    = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i²  = ΔKE

   conservative ✓   non-conservative ✓

Trap: option (b) — students remember "energy is conserved only for conservative forces" and wrongly attach that condition to the theorem. The theorem does not conserve anything; it only bookkeeps what the net work did to the speed.
Answer: (a) always.

Formula a = F/m — a in m/s², F in N, m in kg. s = ut + ½at² — s in m, u in m/s, t in s. W = F s — J. ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu² — J. N × m = J and kg × (m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J, so the two routes must land on the same unit — a good self-check.

(Take g = 10 m/s²) — (Level-3)Atwood system, energy balanceA 1 kg mass on a floor is connected to a 2 kg mass by a string passing over a pulley.🟢 Exact4.47 m s⁻¹
Concept Two blocks joined by an inextensible string over a light pulley move with the same speed at every instant, so the whole system can be treated as one energy account. The 2 kg block falls 3 m (loses PE) while the 1 kg block rises 3 m (gains PE); the net PE released goes entirely into the kinetic energy of both blocks (3 kg of moving mass), because the string tension is internal and does zero net work.
Formula Energy balance: (m₂ − m₁) g h = ½ (m₁ + m₂) v² — masses in kg, g = 10 m/s², h in m, v in m/s, both sides in J. kg × m/s² × m = kg·m²/s² = J on the left, and kg × (m/s)² = J on the right ✓. Individually: ΔPE = −m₂gh + m₁gh and ΔKE = ½(m₁+m₂)v².
Solution
1. Constraint: one inextensible string over one pulley ⇒ when the 2 kg block descends h = 3 m, the 1 kg block rises exactly h = 3 m, and |v₁| = |v₂| = v.
2. Potential energy lost by the descending 2 kg block:
  m₂ g h = 2 × 10 × 3 = 60 J
3. Potential energy gained by the rising 1 kg block:
  m₁ g h = 1 × 10 × 3 = 30 J
4. Net loss of potential energy of the system:
  |ΔPE| = 60 − 30 = 30 JOnly the difference is available, which is why the effective driving weight is (m₂ − m₁)g = 10 N, not 20 N.
5. Gain in kinetic energy — both blocks are moving, so the moving mass is m₁ + m₂ = 3 kg:

4A rain drop of radius 2 mm falls from a height of 500 m above the ground. It falls with decreasing acceleration (due to viscous resistance of the air) until at half its original height it attains its maximum (terminal) speed, and moves with uniform speed thereafter. What is the work done by the gravitational force on the drop in the first and second half of its journey? What is the work done by the resistive force in the entire journey if its speed on reaching the ground is 10 m s⁻¹? — (Level-4)Gravity vs resistive work, terminal speedRain drop of radius 2 mm falls from 500 m — NCERT WEP solution🟢 Exact0.082 J (each half) ; resistive ≈ 0.1658 J in magnitude (i.e. −0.166 J)
Concept Gravity is a constant force, so the work it does depends only on the vertical drop — the fact that the drop decelerates, then coasts at terminal speed, is completely irrelevant to Wgravity. That makes the two halves give equal work. The resistive force is messy and unknown, so you never compute it directly: you get it from the work–energy theorem as the leftover, Wres = ΔKE − Wgravity.
Formula Mass of a water sphere: m = ρ (4/3)πr³ — ρ = 1000 kg/m³, r in m, m in kg. Wgravity = m g h (J). Wgravity + Wres = ΔKE = ½mv² − 0 (J). kg·m/s²·m = J on every term ✓. The drop starts from rest, so KEi = 0.
Solution
1. Radius r = 2 mm = 2 × 10⁻³ m. Volume:
  V = (4/3)π(2×10⁻³)³ = (4/3)π(8×10⁻⁹) = 3.351×10⁻⁸ m³
2. Mass (water, ρ = 1000 kg/m³):
  m = 1000 × 3.351×10⁻⁸ = 3.351×10⁻⁵ kg (about 0.0335 g)
3. Work by gravity over the first 250 m (g = 9.8 m/s²):
  W₁ = mgh = 3.351×10⁻⁵ × 9.8 × 250 = 0.0821 J ≈ 0.082 J
4. Work by gravity over the second 250 m: the drop now moves at constant terminal speed, but gravity is unchanged, so
  W₂ = mgh = 0.082 J — identical to W₁.This is the whole point of the question: constant force × equal drop = equal work, regardless of how the speed behaves.
5. Total work by gravity over 500 m: Wg = 0.082 + 0.082 = 0.1642 J (with g = 9.8); with the exam value g = 10 m/s² it is 3.351×10⁻⁵ × 10 × 500 = 0.1676 J.
6. Kinetic energy on landing (v = 10 m/s, started from rest):
  ΔKE = ½ m v² = ½ × 3.351×10⁻⁵ × 100 = 1.676×10⁻³ J
7. Work–energy theorem for the whole journey:
  Wres = ΔKE − Wg = 0.001676 − 0.1676 = −0.1659 J ≈ −0.1658 J (g = 10 m/s²)The key's 0.1658 J is this magnitude computed with g = 10 m/s²; with g = 9.8 m/s² the same step gives −0.1625 J. Both agree that the drag removes almost all of the ~0.166 J that gravity supplied — only about 1 % survives as kinetic energy.

Concept Tilting a rigid box changes only one thing energetically: the height of its centre of mass. Lying on the L×B face the vertical dimension is H, so the CM sits at H/2; standing on the B×H face the vertical dimension is L, so the CM sits at L/2. The subtlety that makes this a Level-5 item is that to get from one state to the other the box must pivot about an edge, and on the way the CM has to pass through its highest point — directly above that edge.
Formula Wmin = m g ΔhCM — W in J, m in kg, g = 10 m/s², Δh in m. Initial hi = H/2; final hf = L/2. Peak while pivoting: hpeak = ½√(L² + H²) — the CM's distance from the pivot edge. kg × m/s² × m = J ✓; √(m²+m²) = m, so hpeak is a length as it must be.
Solution
1. Initial orientation — resting on the largest face L×B = 1.5 m × 1.0 m. The vertical edge is then H = 0.6 m, so
  hi = H/2 = 0.6/2 = 0.30 m
2. Final orientation — resting on the face B×H = 1.0 m × 0.6 m. The vertical edge is now L = 1.5 m, so
  hf = L/2 = 1.5/2 = 0.75 m

1A drop of liquid of mass 2 g falls from a height of 2 km. It hits the ground with speed 25 m/s. What is the work done by (i) the gravitational force? (ii) the air resistance force? (g = 10 m/s²) — (Level : Board)Wgravity and Wair from ΔKEBody dropped from height h reaches ground with v — find work done by air friction🟡 SimilarWmg = 40 J, Wair = −39.375 J
Concept Exactly the pattern of the raindrop problem, in Board form. Gravity is uniform, so its work is mgh and nothing else. The air-drag force varies with speed in a way you are not told, so you must obtain its work indirectly from the work–energy theorem — this is the standard "the theorem is a measuring instrument for unknown forces" move.
Formula Wgravity = m g h — m in kg, g = 10 m/s², h in m, W in J. Wgravity + Wair = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu² — v, u in m/s. 2 g must be converted to 2×10⁻³ kg and 2 km to 2×10³ m before substituting, otherwise the joule is not a joule.
Solution
1. Convert to SI: m = 2 g = 2×10⁻³ kg, h = 2 km = 2000 m, v = 25 m/s, u = 0 (it "falls", i.e. starts from rest).
2. (i) Work done by gravity — force mg (N) acting through the full drop h (m), both downward, so cos θ = +1:
  Wmg = m g h = (2×10⁻³)(10)(2000) = +40 JPositive, because the force and the displacement point the same way.
3. Kinetic energy at impact:
  KEf = ½ m v² = ½ × 2×10⁻³ × 25² = ½ × 2×10⁻³ × 625 = 0.625 J
4. Change in kinetic energy over the fall: ΔKE = 0.625 − 0 = 0.625 J.
5. (ii) Apply the work–energy theorem to the whole 2 km:
  Wmg + Wair = ΔKE ⇒ 40 + Wair = 0.625
6. Wair = 0.625 − 40 = −39.375 JNegative, as drag must be — it opposes the motion. The size tells the physical story: the air removed 39.375 J of the 40 J gravity supplied, i.e. 98.4 %, which is exactly why real raindrops land gently.

1What does the Work-Energy Theorem state? (a) Work done by external forces equals potential energy (b) Work done by all forces equals total displacement (c) Work done by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy (d) Work done by friction equals the heat generated — (Level-1)Statement of the theoremUnderstanding the Work–Energy Theorem with derivations (JEE)🟡 SimilarcWork done by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy
Concept A definition question, but the three wrong options are each a real misconception worth naming. The theorem connects work to kinetic energy only — potential energy never appears, displacement is not an energy, and the friction-heat statement is a separate (and only approximately true) energy-accounting rule.
Formula Wnet = ΣWall forces = ΔKE = ½mvf² − ½mvi² — W in J, m in kg, v in m/s. Both sides are energies (J). Option (b) would equate joules to metres, which is dimensionally impossible — you can eliminate it on units alone.
Solution
1. Test (a): "external forces = potential energy". Wrong on two counts — the theorem needs all forces (internal ones too), and its right-hand side is kinetic, not potential, energy.The book's own text on p. 144 writes ΣWext + ΣWint = ΔKE, explicitly including internal work.
2. Test (b): "equals total displacement". Dimensionally absurd — [W] = ML²T⁻² but [s] = L. Reject immediately.
3. Test (c): Wnet = ΔKE. This is precisely the line the lecture derives from vf² = vi² + 2aΔx: multiply by ½m to get ½mvf² = ½mvi² + (ma)Δx = ½mvi² + FΔx. ✓
4. Test (d): "friction work = heat generated". Only the relative-sliding friction work becomes heat, and this is a thermodynamic statement, not the work–energy theorem. Reject.
5. Hence the correct statement is (c).
   v_f² = v_i² + 2aΔx          | multiply by ½m
   ─────────────────────────────
   ½mv_f² = ½mv_i² + (ma)Δx
          = ½mv_i² + F Δx
   ⇒  F Δx = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i²
   ⇒  W_net = ΔKE      ← the Work–Energy Theorem   (option c)
⚡ JEE Trick In any definition MCQ, run a dimension sweep first — here option (b) dies instantly (J ≠ m), leaving three. Then ask "does the sentence mention kinetic energy and all forces?" — only (c) does. Trap: (a) reads plausibly because of the familiar Wext = ΔEmech relation, but that one carries potential energy and only holds when a PE function has already been defined.
Answer: (c) Work done by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy.
2In a non-inertial frame, what additional force must be considered? (a) Tension (b) Friction (c) Pseudo force (d) Magnetic force — (Level-1)Pseudo force in accelerating framesNon-inertial frame of reference and pseudo force (JEE/NEET)🟡 SimilarcPseudo force
Concept Newton's second law in the raw form ΣF = ma is only true in an inertial (unaccelerated) frame. If you insist on using it inside a frame that is itself accelerating with a₀, you must add a fictitious force −m a₀ to every body. Tension, friction and magnetic forces are real interaction forces — they are present or absent regardless of your choice of frame.
Formula In a frame accelerating with a₀ (m/s²): ΣFreal + Fpseudo = m arel with Fpseudo = − m a₀ — F in N, m in kg. Work version (book p. 145): Wext + Wint + Wpseudo + Wother = ΔKE (J). kg × m/s² = N, so the pseudo force is a genuine newton-valued term in the equation even though no agent exerts it.
Solution
1. Take the lecture's own example: block A rides on block B, and B accelerates to the right with a₀ (m/s²) relative to the ground.
2. Ground (inertial) view: the only horizontal force on A is friction fs, and fs = m a₀.
3. Frame-of-B (non-inertial) view: A is seen at rest, so the forces on it must sum to zero. Friction fs still acts forward, so a backward force of size m a₀ must be added — that is the pseudo force, drawn as Fpseudo pointing left in the book's figure.
4. Check the other options: tension needs a string, friction needs contact, magnetic force needs charge and a field — each is a real interaction that either exists or does not, independent of the observer's acceleration. None of them is created by moving to a non-inertial frame.
5. Hence the extra term is the pseudo (inertial/fictitious) force ⇒ option (c).

  F tA = mA vA  and  F tB = mB vB
