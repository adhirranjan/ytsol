1Moment of inertia is calculated about — (a) The center of gravity only  (b) A fixed point only  (c) A given axis of rotation  (d) A random reference line — (Level-1)Definition of I · role of the axisSystems of Particles & Rotational Motion 01 | Moment of inertia | JEE🟡 SimilarcA given axis of rotation
Concept Mass is a property of a body alone; moment of inertia is not. I is a property of a body together with a chosen line. The defining sum needs ri, and ri is the perpendicular distance of the ith particle from that line. A single point cannot supply such a distance, because through a point run infinitely many directions and each gives a different perpendicular. Only a line fixes one perpendicular per particle, so a number for I is meaningless until the axis is named.
Formula I = Σmiri², ri ⊥ axis. The square is what makes far mass count far more than near mass, so shifting the axis changes I even when nothing about the body changes.
Solution
1. Read the definition literally: I = Σmiri² with ri measured perpendicular to a line, not to a point. This one word settles the whole question.
2. Kill option (a) with a counter-example: a ring of mass M, radius R has one centre of gravity, yet I = MR² about the central perpendicular axis and I = MR²/2 about a diameter — same CG, two answers.
3. Kill option (b): about a fixed point one can define Σmi|ri|², but that scalar never appears in τ = Iα or KE = ½Iω², which are the equations I exists to serve.
4. Kill option (d): "random reference line" is one word wrong — the line must be the axis the body actually turns about, otherwise Iα is not the torque the body feels.
5. Option (c) is exactly the definition: I is quoted for a stated axis of rotation, e.g. Irod, centre = ML²/12 while Irod, end = ML²/3.
6. The axis need not pass through the CG at all — the parallel-axis theorem I = Icm + Md² exists precisely to move to axes that miss it. Hence (c).

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is (a): most textbook tables list I about axes through the centre of mass, so students start believing the CG is compulsory. It is only the most convenient choice. Remember the slogan "I belongs to a body and an axis" — whenever a question hands you an unfamiliar axis, expect a parallel- or perpendicular-axis step rather than a table lookup.
Answer: (c) A given axis of rotation.
2Find the moment of inertia of the system about the Z-axis as shown in figure. A massless ring of radius R is centred at the origin in the x–y plane; a particle of mass m sits on the ring at 53° above the negative x-direction, a second particle of mass m sits on the ring at 37° above the positive x-direction, and a particle of mass 2m sits on the ring at the lowest point (on the −y axis). — (Level-2)Particles on a ring · central perpendicular axisMoment of Inertia of a system of masses🟡 Similar4mR²
Concept The Z-axis here is the line through the centre of the ring, perpendicular to the plane holding all three particles. For such an axis the perpendicular distance of a particle is just its distance from the centre measured in the plane. Every particle is glued to the ring, so that distance is R for all of them — whatever angle it sits at. The angles 53° and 37° are therefore decoration: they would matter only if the axis were the x- or y-axis, where the perpendicular distance becomes a component.
Formula Iz = Σmiri² with ri = R for a particle on a ring of radius R. For an axis normal to the plane at the centre, r is the full radial distance, not a projection.
Solution
1. Fix the geometry: the ring is massless, so it contributes nothing; only the three beads carry mass m, m and 2m.
2. Perpendicular distance of bead 1 from the Z-axis: r1 = √(x²+y²) = √((R cos53°)²+(R sin53°)²) = R. cos²θ+sin²θ = 1 wipes the angle out.
3. The same algebra gives r2 = R for the 37° bead and r3 = R for the 2m bead at the bottom.
4. Sum: Iz = m R² + m R² + 2m R² = (1+1+2)mR² = 4mR².
5. Sanity check by mass: Iz = MtotalR² with Mtotal = 4m — exactly the ring result I = MR², as it must be, since all mass sits at one radius.
6. Contrast: about the x-axis only y matters, so Ix = mR²sin²53° + mR²sin²37° + 2mR² = (0.64+0.36+2)mR² = 3mR² ≠ 4mR². This is where 53°/37° would have earned their keep.

3Three particles are fixed at the vertices of equilateral ΔABC of side a as shown in figure: mass 2m at A, mass m at B and mass m at C, with D the mid-point of side AB. Find the moment of inertia of the system about the axis passing through D and perpendicular to the plane of the triangle. — (Level-3)Equilateral triangle · axis at mid-point of a sideThree point masses each of mass m at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side a🟡 Similar3ma²/2
Concept The axis pierces the plane of the triangle at D, so for each particle the perpendicular distance to the axis is simply its distance from D measured inside the plane. Two of the three distances are trivial: A and B are the ends of the side that D bisects, so both are a/2 away. The third, DC, is the median from C to AB, and in an equilateral triangle the median to a side is also its altitude — height √3a/2. No parallel-axis theorem is needed; direct summation is faster.
Formula I = Σmiri², with h = √3 a/2 for the altitude of an equilateral triangle of side a. Because the axis is perpendicular to the plane, r is the in-plane distance from the foot D.
Solution
1. Put D at the origin with AB along x: A = (−a/2, 0), B = (a/2, 0), C = (0, √3a/2). D is the mid-point of AB, so A and B are symmetric about it.
2. Particle at A (mass 2m): IA = 2m(a/2)² = 2m·a²/4 = ma²/2.
3. Particle at B (mass m): IB = m(a/2)² = ma²/4.
4. Particle at C (mass m): DC = √3a/2, so IC = m(√3a/2)² = 3ma²/4. Squaring kills the surd, which is why the answer stays rational.
5. Add: I = ma²/2 + ma²/4 + 3ma²/4 = (2 + 1 + 3)ma²/4 = 6ma²/4 = 3ma²/2 = 1.5 ma².
6. Check the size: the total mass is 4m and the farthest particle is at 0.866a, so I must lie below 4m(0.866a)² = 3ma² — and 1.5ma² comfortably does. An upper bound like this catches most arithmetic slips instantly.

4Find the moment of inertia of the system about the given axis as shown in fig. A massless rod is hinged on a vertical axis and makes 30° with it; a particle of mass m lies on the rod 1 m from the axis and a particle of mass 2m lies a further 1 m along the rod (2 m from the axis). Take m = 1 kg. — (Level-4)Rod inclined to the axis · r = L sinθMoment of Inertia Part 2: System of Point Masses🟡 Similar2.25 kg m²
Concept Moment of inertia never uses the distance measured along the rod; it uses the distance measured perpendicular to the axis. When a particle sits a slant length L from the point where the rod meets the axis, and the rod is inclined at θ to that axis, the particle sweeps a circle of radius L sinθ as the assembly spins. Dropping the sine is the single commonest error in this figure, and it inflates the answer by 1/sin²30° = 4.
Formula r = L sinθ and I = Σmi(Li sinθ)² = sin²θ ΣmiLi². θ is common to both particles, so sin²θ can be pulled out as a single factor.
Solution
1. Slant distances from the axis point: L1 = 1 m for mass m, and L2 = 1 + 1 = 2 m for mass 2m. The figure marks two consecutive 1 m segments, so the far particle is 2 m along the rod, not 1 m.
2. Inclination θ = 30°, so sinθ = ½ and every slant length is halved: r1 = 1×½ = 0.5 m, r2 = 2×½ = 1.0 m.
3. Near particle: I1 = m r1² = (1)(0.5)² = 0.25 kg m².
4. Far particle: I2 = 2m r2² = (2)(1.0)² = 2.00 kg m². Twice the mass at twice the radius gives eight times the contribution — the r² weighting dominates.
5. Total: I = 0.25 + 2.00 = 2.25 kg m², i.e. 9/4 in symbols — I = sin²30°[m(1)²+2m(2)²] = ¼(9m) = 2.25m.
6. Trap check: forgetting sin30° gives m(1)²+2m(2)² = 9 kg m², exactly 4× too big; using cos30° instead gives 6.75 kg m². Neither is the perpendicular distance. sin, because the perpendicular offset is opposite to the angle at the axis.

5Find the moment of inertia of the system about the given axis as shown in figure. Four particles each of mass m sit at the vertices of a square of side a whose diagonals lie along the x- and y-axes (vertices on the axes, centre at the origin O). The axis of rotation passes through the origin O, lies in the x–z plane and makes an angle of 30° with the +x axis. — (Level-5)Oblique axis · d = r sinθ in 3-DFour point masses each of mass m arranged in the X-Y plane — moment of inertia🟡 Similar9ma²/8 ⚠
Concept All four particles lie in the x–y plane, but the axis is tilted out of it into the x–z plane. For a tilted axis the perpendicular distance of a particle is no longer a coordinate; it is d = r sinα, where r is the distance from O and α is the angle between the particle's position vector and the axis direction. Equivalently d² = r² − (r·n̂)². Particles lying on the y-axis are already perpendicular to an axis confined to the x–z plane, so they lose nothing; particles on the x-axis are foreshortened by sin30°.
Formula d² = r² − (r·n̂)² with n̂ = (cos30°, 0, sin30°). This is the exact perpendicular-distance identity; it needs no parallel-axis theorem because the axis passes through O.
Solution
1. Locate the particles. The diagonals lie on the axes, so the half-diagonal is a√2/2 = a/√2: the vertices are (±a/√2, 0, 0) and (0, ±a/√2, 0), each at r² = a²/2 from O. Side a means diagonal a√2; halving it gives a/√2, not a.
2. Axis direction n̂ = (cos30°, 0, sin30°) = (√3/2, 0, ½).
3. Particles on the x-axis: r·n̂ = ±(a/√2)(√3/2), so d² = a²/2 − 3a²/8 = a²/8 each, contributing 2m(a²/8) = ma²/4.
4. Particles on the y-axis: r·n̂ = 0 because n̂ has no y-component, so d² = a²/2 each, contributing 2m(a²/2) = ma². An axis in the x–z plane is perpendicular to the whole y-axis, so these two are at full radius.
5. Total: I = ma²/4 + ma² = 5ma²/4 = 1.25 ma². Cross-check with the tensor form: Ixx = ma², Izz = 2ma², Ixz = 0, so I = cos²30°·ma² + sin²30°·2ma² = 3ma²/4 + ma²/2 = 5ma²/4. Same number by an independent route.
6. Discrepancy: the ICAD key prints 9ma²/8 = 1.125 ma². Both of my routes give 5ma²/4 = 1.25 ma², so the key looks defective here — the letter has been kept above and flagged ⚠.

1What does the moment of inertia of a body represent?  (a) Its ability to move linearly  (b) Its resistance to rotational motion  (c) Its mass  (d) Its velocity — (Level-1)Physical meaning of IRotational Motion 04 || Moment Of Inertia – Introduction🟡 SimilarbIts resistance to rotational motion
Concept Rotational dynamics is built by copying translational dynamics symbol for symbol: force becomes torque, acceleration becomes angular acceleration, and the constant that links them becomes the moment of inertia. In F = ma the mass measures how stubbornly a body resists a change in its linear velocity. In τ = Iα the moment of inertia plays exactly that role for spin. So I is rotational inertia — the reluctance of a body to be spun up or slowed down.
Formula τ = Iα and KErot = ½Iω², mirroring F = ma and KE = ½mv². I sits in exactly the slot mass occupies, which is what "rotational inertia" means.
Solution
1. Write the rotational analogue of Newton's second law: τ = Iα, so α = τ/I. For the same torque, a bigger I gives a smaller angular acceleration — that is resistance, literally.
2. Eliminate (a): linear motion is governed by mass alone; a body's I does not appear anywhere in F = ma.
3. Eliminate (c): I is not the mass. Two bodies of identical mass can have wildly different I — a 1 kg ring of radius R has I = R², a 1 kg disc of the same radius has I = R²/2.
4. Eliminate (d): velocity is a state of motion; I is a fixed geometric-and-mass property of the body about a chosen axis, unchanged whether the body spins fast, slow or not at all.
5. Confirm (b) energetically: to reach angular speed ω you must supply ½Iω². Larger I means more work for the same ω, i.e. harder to set rotating and equally harder to stop.
6. Hence (b) resistance to rotational motion.

 KE = (1/2) m v^2       KE = (1/2) I omega^2

⚡ JEE Trick Option (c) tempts because I is measured in kg m² and grows with mass, so it "feels like" mass. The distinction to hold on to: mass is where matter is, moment of inertia is where that matter sits relative to the axis. Move the same mass outward and I rises without a gram being added — which is why a skater pulling her arms in speeds up.
Answer: (b) Its resistance to rotational motion.
2Which of the following factors directly affects the moment of inertia of a particle?  (a) Force applied  (b) Shape of the object  (c) Perpendicular distance from the axis  (d) Time — (Level-1)What I depends onHow to find Moment of Inertia (for point sources of mass)🟡 SimilarcPerpendicular distance from the axis
Concept The question says particle, and a particle has only two attributes that can enter a moment of inertia: its mass and its position. Once an axis is named, the position collapses to a single number — the perpendicular distance r to that axis. So the whole of I for one particle is I = mr², and only m and r can change it. Everything else offered is either a cause of motion, a property a point cannot have, or an independent variable that never enters the definition.
Formula I = m r² for a single particle. Two symbols on the right-hand side means exactly two things can affect I.
Solution
1. Start from the definition for one particle, I = mr², and read off the only inputs: mass m and perpendicular distance r.
2. Eliminate (a): force does not appear. Applying a torque changes α, not I — in τ = Iα the I is fixed and the α responds. Confusing cause with property is the standard trap in this row.
3. Eliminate (b): a particle is a point and has no shape. Shape matters only for an extended body, and even then it matters only through how it spreads mass in r.
4. Eliminate (d): I is a static property; leave the particle alone for an hour and I is unchanged. Time enters θ, ω, α — never I.
5. Confirm (c) quantitatively: double r and I = mr² quadruples, while doubling m only doubles it. Distance is the more powerful of the two inputs because it is squared.
6. Hence (c) perpendicular distance from the axis. Note the word "perpendicular" — the slant distance along a rod is not r.

3The moment of inertia of a system of particles is:  (a) The sum of their masses  (b) The product of total mass and total radius  (c) The sum of each particle's mr²  (d) Independent of the distance from axis — (Level-1)I for a system · Σmiri²Moment of Inertia | System of Particles | NEET JEE Physics🟡 SimilarcThe sum of each particle's mr²
Concept Moment of inertia is additive, particle by particle, because rotational kinetic energy is. Every particle of a rigid body shares the same ω but has its own r, so its share of the energy is ½mi(riω)². Summing and pulling out the common ½ω² leaves Σmiri² standing exactly where ½mv² keeps its mass. That sum is the definition of I for a system, and it makes clear why each particle enters with its own distance squared, not with some common radius.
Formula I = Σmiri² = m1r1² + m2r2² + … Each term carries its own r because each particle sits on its own circle about the axis.
Solution
1. Build it from energy: KE = Σ½mivi² with vi = riω, so KE = ½ω²Σmiri² ≡ ½Iω². Reading off, I = Σmiri². ω is common to all particles of a rigid body; v is not.
2. Eliminate (a): Σmi is the total mass, in kg. I is in kg m² — the units alone rule it out.
3. Eliminate (b): M×R is neither dimensionally right (kg m) nor conceptually right; there is no single "total radius" for scattered particles.
4. Eliminate (d): flatly contradicts step 1, where r appears squared in every term. Push the particles outward and I grows.
5. Confirm (c) numerically: 1 kg at 2 m and 3 kg at 1 m give I = 1(2)² + 3(1)² = 4 + 3 = 7 kg m² — a term-by-term sum, not a lumped product.
6. Hence (c). For a continuous body the same statement becomes I = ∫r²dm.
 KE  = sum (1/2) m_i v_i^2,   v_i = r_i * omega
     = (1/2) omega^2 * sum m_i r_i^2
     = (1/2) I omega^2      ->  I = sum m_i r_i^2

⚡ JEE Trick Option (b) survives in memory because M k² (radius of gyration) really is written as a mass times a length squared — but k is a derived quantity defined after I is known, k = √(I/M), and it is squared. Any option offering mass×radius to the first power is dimensionally dead on arrival; checking kg m² disposes of (a) and (b) in three seconds.
Answer: (c) The sum of each particle's mr².
4If all particles in a system are closer to the axis, the moment of inertia will:  (a) Increase  (b) Decrease  (c) Stay the same  (d) Become zero — (Level-1)Monotonic dependence of I on rMoment of Inertia, System of Particles – Physics🟡 SimilarbDecrease
Concept Every term in I = Σmiri² is non-negative and increases strictly with ri. So I is a monotonically increasing function of each particle's distance: reduce every ri while keeping the masses fixed and no term can grow, while at least one must shrink. The sum therefore falls. This is not a special case — it is the whole reason a figure skater spins faster with arms tucked in and a diver tucks to somersault quickly.
Formula I = Σmiri², and with angular momentum conserved L = Iω = constant. The second relation converts the drop in I into the observable rise in ω.
Solution
1. Take any particle: ri → ri′ with ri′ < ri. Then miri′² < miri², since masses are positive.
2. Sum over all particles: every term drops or stays, so I′ < I. The system's moment of inertia decreases.
3. Numerical illustration: two 2 kg masses at 3 m give I = 2(3)²×2 = 36 kg m²; move both to 1 m and I = 2(1)²×2 = 4 kg m² — a ninefold drop for a threefold move. The square makes the effect dramatic, not gradual.
4. Eliminate (a): increase would require r to grow, the opposite of the stem.
5. Eliminate (c): only a rearrangement that leaves every ri unchanged (a pure rotation about the axis) keeps I fixed — not what is described. Eliminate (d): I hits zero only if every particle lands exactly on the axis, which "closer" does not guarantee.
6. Hence (b): with L = Iω conserved, the skater's ω rises exactly as I falls.

Also resist option (c) — it is right only for the special rearrangement that preserves each r, e.g. rotating the whole system about the same axis.

5The moment of inertia depends on:  (a) Position of axis only  (b) Shape of the object only  (c) Mass distribution and distance from axis  (d) Velocity of object — (Level-1)Complete dependence list for ISystems of Particles & Rotational Motion 01 | Moment of inertia | JEE🟡 SimilarcMass distribution and distance from axis
Concept Read I = Σmiri² as a recipe with two ingredients: how much mass there is and where it sits. The mi encode the mass distribution; the ri encode the geometry relative to the chosen axis. Both are needed — the same mass placed differently gives a different I, and the same geometry filled with different masses does too. Options that say "only" are therefore incomplete by construction, and an option naming a state of motion is a category error.
Formula I = Σmiri², continuous form I = ∫r²dm. dm supplies the distribution, r² supplies the axis-dependent geometry — neither factor can be dropped.
Solution
1. Split the definition: I is a weighted sum where the weights are masses and the weighting function is the squared distance from the axis. Two ingredients, both essential.
2. Eliminate (a): axis position alone is not enough. A ring and a disc of the same M and R, about the same central axis, give MR² and MR²/2 — the mass distribution decided the difference.
3. Eliminate (b): shape alone is not enough either. Two identically shaped discs, one aluminium and one lead, have very different I. Shape fixes the r's but says nothing about the m's.
4. Eliminate (d): I is defined before the body ever moves; a stationary flywheel has a perfectly definite I. Velocity enters L = Iω and KE = ½Iω² as a separate factor.
5. Confirm (c): it names both ingredients. Strictly, the axis is implicit in "distance from axis", so (c) is the complete statement — mass distribution plus geometry about the stated axis.
6. Hence (c). A compact way to remember it: I = how much mass × how far, squared.

Keep the ring-versus-disc pair in your head as a ready counter-example — it kills "shape only" and "axis only" in one stroke.
Answer: (c) Mass distribution and distance from axis.

The moment of inertia of the system about axis AB is X × 10−3 kg m². Find X. — (Level-2)Two particles · unit conversion to SIMoment of Inertia Part 2: System of Point Masses🟡 SimilarX = 3
Concept The two particles lie in a plane perpendicular to AB, so AB pierces that plane and the distances quoted (20 cm, 10 cm) are already the perpendicular distances to the axis — no projection is needed. The only real work is unit hygiene: grams must become kilograms and centimetres must become metres before squaring, because the centimetre error is amplified by a factor of 104. The masses sitting on opposite sides of AB is irrelevant; r² is blind to sign.
Formula I = m1r1² + m2r2² in SI. 1 gm = 10−3 kg and 1 cm = 10−2 m, so 1 gm cm² = 10−7 kg m².
Solution
1. Convert masses: m1 = 50 gm = 0.05 kg, m2 = 100 gm = 0.10 kg.
2. Convert distances: r1 = 20 cm = 0.20 m, r2 = 10 cm = 0.10 m. Convert first, square second — squaring centimetres and converting later is where most marks are lost.
3. First particle: I1 = m1r1² = (0.05)(0.20)² = (0.05)(0.04) = 2.0 × 10−3 kg m².
4. Second particle: I2 = m2r2² = (0.10)(0.10)² = (0.10)(0.01) = 1.0 × 10−3 kg m². Twice the mass but half the distance loses out: half the r means a quarter of the r².
5. Total: I = 2.0 × 10−3 + 1.0 × 10−3 = 3.0 × 10−3 kg m².
6. Compare with the given form X × 10−3 kg m²: X = 3. Cross-check in cgs: 50(20)² + 100(10)² = 20000 + 10000 = 3×104 gm cm² = 3×104×10−7 = 3×10−3 kg m². Same.
 particle   m (kg)   r (m)    r^2      m r^2 (kg m^2)
 --------   ------   -----   ------   ---------------
   50 gm     0.05     0.20    0.04       2.0e-3
  100 gm     0.10     0.10    0.01       1.0e-3
 ---------------------------------------------------
 I = 3.0e-3 kg m^2   ->   X = 3
⚡ JEE Trick The distractor is the instinct that the heavier particle must dominate. It does not: r is squared, so the 50 gm at 20 cm contributes twice as much as the 100 gm at 10 cm. Whenever a numerical-answer question hands you a prefix like 10−3, work the whole sum in SI and read X off at the end — converting the final answer back into the demanded power is where sign-of-exponent slips creep in.
Answer: X = 3 (I = 3 × 10−3 kg m²).
2Two particles of masses 1 kg each are put at (3, 4) m and (1, 2) m. Find the moment of inertia of the system about the z−axis in kg m². — (Level-2)Coordinates · Iz = Σm(x²+y²)Four point masses each of mass m arranged in the X-Y plane — moment of inertia🟡 Similar30 kg m²
Concept The z-axis runs perpendicular to the x–y plane through the origin, so a particle at (x, y) traces a circle of radius √(x²+y²) about it. Its contribution is therefore m(x²+y²) — and because the square root is immediately squared again, you never need to evaluate it. This is the fastest of the three coordinate cases: about the x-axis only y matters, about the y-axis only x matters, and about the z-axis both do.
Formula Iz = Σmi(xi² + yi²). r² = x²+y² by Pythagoras, so the surd cancels before any arithmetic starts.
Solution
1. First particle at (3, 4): r1² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 m², so I1 = (1)(25) = 25 kg m². The familiar 3-4-5 triangle: r1 = 5 m, but 25 is what we actually need.
2. Second particle at (1, 2): r2² = 1² + 2² = 1 + 4 = 5 m², so I2 = (1)(5) = 5 kg m².
3. Add: Iz = 25 + 5 = 30 kg m².
4. Dimension sanity: both masses are 1 kg, so Iz is numerically just the sum of the squared distances, 25 + 5 = 30.
5. Cross-check by the perpendicular-axis idea: Ix = Σmy² = 16 + 4 = 20 and Iy = Σmx² = 9 + 1 = 10, and indeed Ix + Iy = 30 = Iz. The perpendicular-axis theorem holds because all the mass lies in the z = 0 plane.
6. Trap check: adding the distances rather than their squares gives 5 + √5 ≈ 7.24, and taking √(25+5) gives 5.48 — neither has the units kg m².
 particle   (x, y)    x^2   y^2   r^2 = x^2+y^2   m r^2
 --------  --------   ---   ---   -------------  -----
    1      (3, 4)      9    16         25          25
    2      (1, 2)      1     4          5           5
 ------------------------------------------------------
 I_z = 30 kg m^2   check: I_x + I_y = 20 + 10 = 30
⚡ JEE Trick Do not compute r and then square it — go straight to r² = x²+y². It saves a step and removes the temptation to round √5. The free cross-check is the perpendicular-axis theorem: for coplanar particles Iz must equal Ix+Iy, and this same triangle-of-points setup is reused for the x- and y-axis versions in Level-3.
Answer: 30 kg m².

4Two particles of mass 1 kg and 2 kg are fixed at the ends A and B of massless rod AB of length 1 m respectively. Find M.O.I. (in kg-m²) about the axis passing through A and perpendicular to the rod. — (Level-2)Dumb-bell · axis at one endMoment of Inertia of a system of masses🟡 Similar2 kg m²
Concept The rod is massless, so the system is just two point masses. The axis is perpendicular to the rod and pinned at A, which means the 1 kg at A sits on the axis and sweeps no circle at all. Only the 2 kg at the far end matters, and being perpendicular to the rod, the axis makes the full rod length its perpendicular distance. This is the cleanest illustration of the fact that mass on the axis is inertially invisible for rotation about it.
Formula I = Σmiri², with r measured perpendicular to the axis. Axis ⊥ rod means the projection factor sin90° = 1, so r equals the distance along the rod.
Solution
1. Set A at the origin, B at 1 m along the rod. Masses: mA = 1 kg at A, mB = 2 kg at B.
2. Particle at A: rA = 0, so IA = (1)(0)² = 0 kg m². A mass lying on the rotation axis has zero speed at every instant, so it stores no rotational kinetic energy.
3. Particle at B: rB = 1 m, so IB = (2)(1)² = 2 kg m².
4. Total: I = 0 + 2 = 2 kg m².
5. Cross-check via the parallel-axis theorem. The centre of mass sits at xcm = (1×0 + 2×1)/3 = 2/3 m from A, and Icm = 1(2/3)² + 2(1/3)² = 4/9 + 2/9 = 2/3 kg m². Then IA = Icm + Md² = 2/3 + 3(2/3)² = 2/3 + 4/3 = 2 kg m². Two independent routes agreeing is the cheapest error check available.
6. Trap check: swapping the masses (2 kg at A, 1 kg at B) would give 1 kg m², and putting the axis at the mid-point would give 0.75 kg m² — neither matches the stated geometry.
   axis
     |
     A---------------------B
   1 kg      1 m         2 kg
   r = 0                 r = 1 m
   I = 1*(0)^2 + 2*(1)^2 = 0 + 2 = 2 kg m^2
   check: I_cm = 2/3, M = 3, d = 2/3
          I_A  = 2/3 + 3*(2/3)^2 = 2 kg m^2
⚡ JEE Trick Notice how much cheaper the direct sum is than the parallel-axis route here — use the theorem only when the axis misses every particle. The classic slip is to include the on-axis mass "because it is heavy"; it contributes exactly zero. Equally, watch which end the axis is on: the answer changes from 2 to 1 kg m² if the masses are read the wrong way round.
Answer: 2 kg m².

3. Add: I = m x² + m x² = 2mx², which is option (b).

Concept The centre of mass of a two-particle dumb-bell lies on the line joining them, closer to the heavier mass, and divides the separation in the inverse ratio of the masses. Once the two distances are known, I is a two-term sum. There is also a shortcut worth owning: for exactly two particles the answer collapses to I = μd², where μ = m1m2/(m1+m2) is the reduced mass and d the full separation — one line instead of four.

Solution

5The M.O.I. of three particle system about the axis passing through centroid of triangle ABC and perpendicular to plane of paper. Concept The centroid of an equilateral triangle is also its circumcentre, so all three vertices lie on one circle about it. The axis pierces the plane at that centre, so every particle has the same perpendicular distance — the circumradius. The whole problem reduces to knowing that the circumradius of an equilateral triangle of side a is a/√3, which follows from the centroid cutting each median in the ratio 2 : 1 measured from the vertex.
Formula median h = √3a/2, circumradius R = (2/3)h = a/√3, then I = 3mR². Equal masses at equal radii means the sum is just 3 × one term.
Solution
1. Altitude (= median) of an equilateral triangle of side a: h = √(a² − (a/2)²) = √3a/2.
2. The centroid divides each median 2 : 1 from the vertex, so the vertex-to-centroid distance is R = (2/3)(√3a/2) = a/√3 ≈ 0.577a. Two thirds of the median, not half of it — the 2:1 split is the step everyone shortens by mistake.
3. Square it once and for all: R² = a²/3.
4. Each particle contributes mR² = ma²/3, and by symmetry all three are identical.
5. Total: I = 3 × ma²/3 = ma², option (d).

The moment of inertia of the system about the axis passing through the centre of the ring and perpendicular to the plane of the ring is  (a) 10mr²  (b) 8mr²  (c) 6mr²  (d) depends upon orientation of masses w.r.t. ring — (Level-4)Beads on a ring · orientation-independent IFour particles at four corners of a square — moment of inertia🟡 Similara10mr²
Concept Every bead is stuck to the rim, so each one is exactly r from the centre. The axis passes through that centre perpendicular to the plane, which means the perpendicular distance of a bead from the axis is its full radial distance — r, regardless of the angle at which it sits. Since r is common, it factors out of the sum and only the total mass survives. Sliding the beads around the rim changes nothing about I for this axis, which is why option (d) is a trap and not a hedge.
Formula I = Σmiri² = r²Σmi when every particle is at the same radius. A common r comes out of the sum, leaving the total mass times r² — the thin-ring result MR².
Solution
1. Note the ring is massless: it only positions the beads, contributing no inertia of its own.
2. Each bead lies on the circumference, so r1 = r2 = r3 = r4 = r. For a central perpendicular axis, distance-from-axis and distance-from-centre are the same number.
3. Factor the common radius: I = r²(m + 2m + 3m + 4m) = r²(10m) = 10mr².
4. Recognise the structure: the answer is Mtotalr² with Mtotal = 10m, which is exactly the standard thin-ring formula I = MR².
5. Reject (d): the angular positions never enter, because I depends on r² and every r is identical. Orientation would matter only for an axis lying in the plane of the ring, such as a diameter. About a diameter you would need Σmir²sin²θi, and then the angles are everything.
6. Decode (b) and (c): 8mr² and 6mr² correspond to dropping one of the masses (say the 2m or the 4m) from the sum — arithmetic slips, not physics.

 ----   ----   -----------   -------
  1      m          r         m r^2
  2     2m          r        2m r^2
  3     3m          r        3m r^2
  4     4m          r        4m r^2
 --------------------------------------

⚡ JEE Trick Option (d) is written to sound sophisticated — "surely where you put them matters". For an axis through the centre and perpendicular to the ring it does not, and recognising that is the whole question. Rule of thumb: whenever all mass sits at one radius from a central perpendicular axis, jump straight to I = Mtotalr² and skip the term-by-term sum entirely.
Answer: (a) 10mr².

Solution

4. D(0, a): displacement (−a, a/2), so r² = a² + a²/4 = 5a²/4 as well, contributing 5ma²/4. A and D are mirror images in the line BC's perpendicular bisector, so they must match.

Concept The four particles sit at the corners of a square of side a, and the ring through them is its circumcircle. For an axis through the centre perpendicular to the plane, every particle is at the circumradius, so the sum collapses to 4mR². The one geometric fact needed is that a square's diagonal is a√2, so its circumradius — half the diagonal — is a/√2. The ring's radius is therefore not a; confusing the two is the entire trap.
Formula diagonal = a√2, circumradius R = a/√2, so I = 4mR². All four particles share one radius, so the common R² factors straight out of Σmiri².
Solution
1. Take the square ABCD of side a. Its diagonal AC = √(a²+a²) = a√2. Pythagoras on two adjacent sides — the diagonals of a square are equal and bisect each other.
2. The centre O is the intersection of the diagonals, so R = OA = (a√2)/2 = a/√2 ≈ 0.707a.
3. Square it: R² = a²/2. Each particle contributes m(a²/2).
4. All four are at the same R, so I = 4m(a²/2) = 2ma², option (b).
5. Cross-check by coordinates: A(0,0), B(a,0), C(a,a), D(0,a) with O = (a/2, a/2). Each r² = (a/2)²+(a/2)² = a²/2, so I = 4m(a²/2) = 2ma². Same number.
6. Decode (d): 4ma² is what you get from R = a, i.e. from believing the ring's radius equals the square's side. The other options do not correspond to any consistent radius here. The factor between the two answers is exactly the 2 from (√2)².
