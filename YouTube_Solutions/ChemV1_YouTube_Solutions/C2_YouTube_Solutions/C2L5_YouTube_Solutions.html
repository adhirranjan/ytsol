1Total energy of the electron in the ground state of a hydrogen atom (Level-1)Energy of electronEnergy of electron-nth Bohr's orbit-Quick formula - solved problems-IIT JEE NEET-AdiChemistry🟢 Exactc−13.6 eV
Concept In Bohr's model the electron of a hydrogen atom is bound to the nucleus, and a bound particle always has negative total energy — a free electron at rest infinitely far away is taken as the zero. The single number that fixes every orbit's energy is −13.6 eV, the energy of the lowest (ground) state, n = 1. The whole of Bohr energetics is just this constant scaled by Z²/n².
Formula En = −13.6 × Z²/n² eV: En = total energy of the electron in the nth orbit (eV); Z = nuclear charge (for hydrogen Z = 1); n = principal quantum number. The 13.6 eV bundles all the physical constants; the minus sign encodes that energy must be supplied to free the electron. Ground state means n = 1.
Solution
1. Identify the numbers for hydrogen ground state: Z = 1, n = 1."Ground state" is the lowest orbit, n = 1; hydrogen has one proton so Z = 1.
2. Substitute into En = −13.6 Z²/n²: E₁ = −13.6 × (1)²/(1)².
3. Evaluate the fraction: (1)²/(1)² = 1/1 = 1.
4. So E₁ = −13.6 × 1 = −13.6 eV, which is option (c).
 quantity   value
 Z          1
 n          1
 E₁ = −13.6 × 1²/1² = −13.6 eV
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the hydrogen ladder: E₁ = −13.6, E₂ = −3.4, E₃ = −1.51, E₄ = −0.85 eV — each is −13.6/n². The ground state is the most negative (deepest, most tightly bound). Trap: a positive answer is impossible for a bound electron; sign errors here cost easy marks.
Answer: (c) −13.6 eV.
2Energy of an electron in the third orbit of He⁺ (Level-2)Hydrogen-like energyA-HLIKE🟡 Similara−6.04 eV
Concept He⁺ is a hydrogen-like species — a single electron orbiting a nucleus — so Bohr's formula applies unchanged, only with the correct nuclear charge Z. Helium has Z = 2, and because the energy scales as Z² the He⁺ levels are four times deeper than the corresponding hydrogen levels. Feeding Z = 2 and n = 3 into En = −13.6 Z²/n² gives the answer directly.
Formula En = −13.6 × Z²/n² eV, with Z = atomic number and n = orbit number. Single-electron ions obey the same law as H; the ×Z² factor reflects the stronger pull of a more highly charged nucleus. For He⁺, Z = 2; "third orbit" means n = 3.
Solution
1. List the data: He⁺ ⇒ Z = 2; third orbit ⇒ n = 3.
2. Square them: Z² = 2² = 4; n² = 3² = 9.
3. Substitute: E₃ = −13.6 × 4/9.
4. Multiply the numerator: −13.6 × 4 = −54.4, so E₃ = −54.4/9.
5. Divide: −54.4 ÷ 9 = −6.04 eV — option (a).
 Z=2, n=3
 E₃ = −13.6 × (2²/3²) = −13.6 × 4/9
    = −54.4 / 9 = −6.04 eV
⚡ JEE Trick For any H-like ion, En(ion) = Z² × En(H). Here E₃(H) = −1.51 eV, so E₃(He⁺) = 4 × (−1.51) = −6.04 eV — no long division needed. Trap: use Z for the ion (He⁺ has Z = 2), not the neutral-atom electron count.
Answer: (a) −6.04 eV.
3Separation energy to remove electron completely from 2nd excited state of H (Level-3)Separation energyA-IONEXC🟡 Similara+1.51 eV
Concept Separation energy is the energy needed to lift the electron from whatever level it is in all the way to n = ∞ (a free electron, E = 0). Since the electron sits at a negative energy En, freeing it means adding exactly |En|, so separation energy = −En = +13.6 Z²/n². The only care point is decoding "2nd excited state": ground = n = 1, first excited = n = 2, so second excited = n = 3.
Formula separation energy = 0 − En = −En = +13.6 Z²/n² eV. Free state has E = 0; the electron starts at En < 0, so the energy supplied equals the gap 0 − En, always positive. For H, Z = 1.
Solution
1. Decode the state: ground n = 1, 1st excited n = 2, 2nd excited n = 3."Excited state" numbering starts counting above the ground state.
2. Energy of that level: E₃ = −13.6 × 1²/3² = −13.6/9 = −1.51 eV.
3. Separation energy = 0 − E₃ = 0 − (−1.51) = +1.51 eV.Removing the electron to infinity means climbing from −1.51 eV up to 0, a rise of 1.51 eV.
4. This matches option (a), +1.51 eV.
 state         n   E_n = −13.6/n²   separation = −E_n
 2nd excited   3   −1.51 eV         +1.51 eV
⚡ JEE Trick Separation energy from level n (for H) = 13.6/n² eV, and it is just the magnitude of that orbit's energy. Trap: "2nd excited state" = n = 3, not n = 2 — off-by-one here turns 1.51 into the wrong 3.4 eV.
Answer: (a) +1.51 eV.
4Why ionization, separation & excitation energy are always positive (Level-4)Energy sign conventionAtomic Structure 11|Quantisation of Energy| Ionisation Energy| Separation Energy|Class11 NEET-JEE🟢 ExactbEnergy must always be supplied to increase the energy of a bound electron
Concept Ionization, separation and excitation all move the electron to a higher energy level (larger n, or n = ∞). Because a bound electron sits at negative energy and every higher level is less negative (closer to 0), each of these processes raises the electron's energy. Raising energy means energy must flow into the atom, so all three are absorbed (endothermic) quantities and carry a positive sign.
Formula energy supplied = Efinal − Einitial > 0 whenever Efinal > Einitial. All three processes end at a higher (less negative) level, so the difference is always positive. Ionization/separation go to Efinal = 0; excitation goes to a higher bound level.
Solution
1. Note the sign of levels: bound states have En < 0, and E rises (becomes less negative) as n increases toward 0 at n = ∞.
2. Excitation: nlow → nhigh, so E goes up ⇒ energy absorbed ⇒ positive.
3. Separation / ionization: n → ∞ where E = 0, the highest reachable value ⇒ energy absorbed ⇒ positive.
4. In every case the electron is pushed uphill in energy, which can only happen if energy is supplied — exactly statement (b).
 process       E_initial   E_final     ΔE = supplied
 excitation    −13.6/n₁²   −13.6/n₂²   > 0 (n₂>n₁)
 separation    −13.6/n²     0          > 0
 ionization    −13.6        0          +13.6 eV
⚡ JEE Trick Anything that moves the electron up (excite, separate, ionize) costs energy ⇒ positive; anything that lets it fall (emission, de-excitation) releases energy ⇒ negative. Trap: don't confuse the electron's energy (negative) with the energy you must add (positive) — they have opposite signs.
Answer: (b) Energy must always be supplied to increase the energy of a bound electron.
5Which Bohr-orbit relations (KE=−TE, PE=2TE, PE=−2KE) are always correct (Level-5)KE/PE/TE relationsPotential Energy, Kinetic Energy and Total Energy of the Electron in Bohr's Orbit for JEE and NEET🟢 ExactdI, II and III
Concept For any electron held by an inverse-square (Coulomb) force, the virial theorem ties its three energies together in fixed ratios. Taking the total energy TE as the reference, the kinetic energy is exactly its opposite (KE = −TE) and the potential energy is exactly twice it (PE = 2·TE). Every other relation between the three follows by simple substitution, so all three listed statements are consequences of the same rule.
Formula TE = −KE, PE = 2·TE, PE = −2·KE, and TE = KE + PE. These come from the virial theorem for a 1/r potential: the magnitudes lock into the ratio KE : TE : PE = 1 : −1 : −2. Energies in eV.
Solution
1. Start from the virial result KE = −TE (statement I).For a Coulomb field the kinetic energy equals minus the total energy — a standard result.
2. Use TE = KE + PE and replace KE by −TE: TE = (−TE) + PE.
3. Add TE to both sides: 2·TE = PE, i.e. PE = 2·TE (statement II) ✓.
4. Substitute TE = −KE into PE = 2·TE: PE = 2(−KE) = −2·KE (statement III) ✓.
5. All three (I, II, III) are always true — option (d).
 take TE = E
 KE = −E              (I)   ✓
 PE = 2E = 2·TE       (II)  ✓
 PE = 2E = −2·KE      (III) ✓   since KE = −E
 check: KE+PE = −E+2E = E = TE ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the ratio KE : TE : PE = 1 : −1 : −2. Once you know TE, everything follows: KE = |TE|, PE = 2·TE. Trap: sign slips — PE and TE are negative, KE is positive; a relation that makes KE negative is automatically wrong.
Answer: (d) I, II and III.

1Explain: (a) atomic number (b) mass number (c) nuclideAtomic & mass numberExplain with examples (i) Atomic number (ii) Mass number (iii) Isotopes, and (iv) Isobars. Give any🟢 Exact—
Concept Three counting labels pin down exactly what a nucleus contains. The atomic number Z fixes the element (it is the proton count), the mass number A fixes how heavy that particular atom is (protons + neutrons), and a nuclide is the name for one specific atom once both Z and A are stated. Together they let us write any species unambiguously as ZAX.
Key relation A = Z + N, where Z = number of protons (= atomic number), N = number of neutrons, A = mass number, written ZAX. Protons set the identity and charge; adding the neutron count gives the total nucleon (mass) number.
Solution
1. Atomic number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus; it equals the number of electrons in a neutral atom and defines the element.Change Z and you change the element itself.
2. Mass number (A) = total number of nucleons = protons + neutrons = Z + N; it is always a whole number.Electrons are ~1836× lighter, so they add negligibly to the mass.
3. Nuclide = an atomic species with a specified Z and A, e.g. 612C or 1123Na."Nuclide" names one exact proton–neutron combination, unlike "element" which lumps all isotopes together.
 species        Z    A    N = A−Z    meaning
 ₆C¹²           6    12   6          nuclide of carbon
 ₁₁Na²³         11   23   12         nuclide of sodium
 A = Z + N in every case
⚡ JEE Trick Read ZAX as "bottom = protons, top = protons + neutrons"; neutrons = A − Z. Trap: mass number A is a nucleon count (an integer), not the atomic mass in u (which carries decimals from binding energy and isotope mixing).
Key point: atomic number Z = proton count (defines the element); mass number A = protons + neutrons (Z + N); a nuclide is a specific atom identified by both Z and A, written ZAX.
2What are isotopes and isobars? Give examplesIsotopes & isobarsExplain with examples (i) Atomic number (ii) Mass number (iii) Isotopes, and (iv) Isobars. Give any🟢 Exact—
Concept Isotopes and isobars are the two ways two atoms can be "the same in one number but different in the other." Isotopes share the atomic number Z (same element) but differ in mass number A — they are the same element with different neutron counts. Isobars share the mass number A but have different Z — they are different elements that happen to weigh the same in nucleon count. Keeping straight which number is held fixed is the whole idea.
Key relation isotopes: same Z, different A (⇒ different N); isobars: same A, different Z. Fixing Z fixes the element (isotopes); fixing A fixes only the total nucleon count, which several elements can share (isobars).
Solution
1. Isotopes: atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons, hence different A.Same proton count = same chemical element; extra neutrons only change the mass.
2. Example of isotopes: 11H, 12H, 13H (all Z = 1); or 1735Cl and 1737Cl.
3. Isobars: atoms of different elements (different Z) that have the same mass number A.Different proton counts make them chemically different, but their nucleon totals match.
4. Example of isobars: 1840Ar, 1940K and 2040Ca (all A = 40, different Z).
 type       fixed   varies   example
 isotopes   Z       A, N     ₁H¹, ₁H², ₁H³   (Z=1)
 isobars    A       Z, N     ₁₈Ar⁴⁰, ₁₉K⁴⁰, ₂₀Ca⁴⁰ (A=40)
⚡ JEE Trick Mnemonic: isotopes → same protons (same top-of-table element); isobars → same "bar" = same mass number A. Trap: isotopes are one element, isobars are different elements — swapping this is the classic exam error.
Key point: isotopes = same Z, different A (same element, e.g. ₁H¹/₁H²/₁H³); isobars = same A, different Z (different elements, e.g. ₁₈Ar⁴⁰/₁₉K⁴⁰/₂₀Ca⁴⁰).

Concept To sort a list of nuclides into isotopes and isobars you compare them two numbers at a time. Read off each nuclide's Z (bottom, from the element symbol) and A (top). Any pair sharing the same Z are isotopes; any pair sharing the same A but different Z are isobars. It is purely a matching exercise once the two numbers are tabulated.
Key relation same Z ⇒ isotopes ; same A, different Z ⇒ isobars. Z is read from the element's identity; A is the superscript. Compare columns, not the symbols themselves.
Solution
1. Tabulate Z and A for each nuclide (Z from the element: C = 6, N = 7, S = 16, U = 92, Np = 93, …).Turn every symbol into its (Z, A) pair before comparing.
2. Group by equal Z → these are isotopes. Example: 1632S and 1634S share Z = 16 ⇒ isotopes of sulphur.
3. Group by equal A but different Z → these are isobars. Example: 614C and 714N share A = 14 with Z = 6 vs 7 ⇒ isobars.
4. A heavy-element case: 92239U and 93239Np share A = 239, different Z ⇒ isobars.

4Describe the isotopes of carbonIsotopes of carbonIsotopes of Carbon / C-12 / C-13 / C-14 /easy explained🟢 Exact—
Concept Carbon always has Z = 6 (six protons), so all its isotopes are chemically carbon; they differ only in neutron number and therefore in mass number A. Three isotopes matter: 12C and 13C are stable and make up natural carbon, while 14C is radioactive and is the basis of radiocarbon dating. Because they share Z, they behave identically in chemical reactions.
Key relation all carbon isotopes: Z = 6, N = A − 6. Fixing Z at 6 keeps the element carbon; the neutron count N = A − 6 is what changes between isotopes. Natural carbon's average mass ≈ 12.011 u, close to 12 because 12C dominates.
Solution
1. 12C: Z = 6, A = 12 ⇒ 6 neutrons. Stable; ≈ 98.9% of natural carbon; defines the amu (exactly 12 u).
2. 13C: Z = 6, A = 13 ⇒ 7 neutrons. Stable; ≈ 1.1% abundance; used in NMR.
3. 14C: Z = 6, A = 14 ⇒ 8 neutrons. Radioactive (β-emitter, half-life ≈ 5730 yr); trace amounts; used in carbon dating.
4. All three have identical chemistry (same 6 electrons); only mass and nuclear stability differ.Chemistry is set by electrons/protons, not neutrons.
 isotope   Z   A    N = A−6   nature        abundance
 ¹²C       6   12   6         stable        ≈ 98.9%
 ¹³C       6   13   7         stable        ≈ 1.1%
 ¹⁴C       6   14   8         radioactive   trace
⚡ JEE Trick For any element's isotopes, the number that never changes is Z (here 6); neutrons = A − Z. Trap: the periodic-table value 12.011 is the abundance-weighted average of ¹²C/¹³C, not the mass of any single isotope — the ¹²C standard is exactly 12.000.
Key point: carbon's isotopes all have Z = 6 and differ only in neutrons — ¹²C (6 n, stable, ~98.9%), ¹³C (7 n, stable, ~1.1%) and radioactive ¹⁴C (8 n); identical chemistry, different mass.

1What is the total energy of an electron in an atom? (KE + PE)Total energyPotential Energy, Kinetic Energy and Total Energy of the Electron in Bohr's Orbit for JEE and NEET🟢 ExactcSum of kinetic and potential energy
Concept An orbiting electron carries energy of two kinds: kinetic energy because it is moving, and potential energy because it is held in the nucleus's electric field. Its total energy is simply the sum of these two. This is the definition every Bohr-model energy relation is built on, so getting it right is the foundation for KE = −TE and PE = 2·TE.
Formula TE = KE + PE, all in eV (or J). Total mechanical energy is by definition kinetic plus potential; there is no third term for an electron in an orbit. For a Bohr orbit the pieces work out to KE = +13.6 Z²/n² and PE = −27.2 Z²/n².
Solution
1. Kinetic energy KE comes from the electron's motion (always positive).
2. Potential energy PE comes from the Coulomb attraction to the nucleus (negative, since attraction lowers energy).
3. By definition total energy is their sum: TE = KE + PE — option (c).
4. Check with the Bohr magnitudes: KE = +13.6/n², PE = −27.2/n² ⇒ TE = 13.6/n² − 27.2/n² = −13.6/n² eV ✓ (correctly negative).
 piece   sign   Bohr value (H)
 KE      +      +13.6/n²
 PE      −      −27.2/n²
 TE = KE + PE = −13.6/n²
⚡ JEE Trick TE = KE + PE always; then the virial shortcuts KE = −TE and PE = 2·TE let you jump between them. Trap: PE is twice as large in magnitude as KE and negative, so the sum lands at −KE — the total energy is negative even though KE is positive.
Answer: (c) Sum of kinetic and potential energy.

2. Binding lowers energy below this reference, so any trapped electron has E < 0.
3. The formula confirms it: En = −13.6 Z²/n², which is negative for all finite n ⇒ option (c), Negative.
4. Illustration (H): E₁ = −13.6, E₂ = −3.4, E₃ = −1.51 eV — all below zero, rising toward 0 as n grows.
 n     E_n (H)     sign
 1     −13.6 eV    −
 2     −3.4 eV     −

3What happens to total energy as principal quantum number n increases?Energy vs nEnergy of electron-nth Bohr's orbit-Quick formula - solved problems-IIT JEE NEET-AdiChemistry🟢 ExactcIncreases (less negative)
Concept The energy of a Bohr orbit depends on n through the factor 1/n². As n grows, 1/n² shrinks, so the size of the (negative) energy gets smaller — the value climbs toward zero. Physically the electron in a higher orbit is farther from the nucleus, less tightly bound, and therefore at higher (less negative) energy. It never becomes positive until the electron is actually freed at n = ∞.
Formula En = −13.6 Z²/n² eV; as n ↑, 1/n² ↓, so |En| ↓ and En rises toward 0. A smaller fraction times a fixed negative constant gives a number closer to zero, i.e. larger (less negative).
Solution
1. Write the dependence: En ∝ −1/n².
2. Increase n: 1/1² = 1, 1/2² = 0.25, 1/3² = 0.111, 1/4² = 0.0625 — the factor keeps decreasing.
3. Multiply by −13.6 (H): E₁ = −13.6, E₂ = −3.4, E₃ = −1.51, E₄ = −0.85 eV — each is less negative than the last.
4. So as n increases the total energy increases (becomes less negative), approaching 0 — option (c)."Increase" here means moving up the number line, i.e. −13.6 → −0.85 is an increase.
 n    1/n²     E_n = −13.6/n²
 1    1.000    −13.6 eV
 2    0.250    −3.4 eV
 3    0.111    −1.51 eV
 4    0.0625   −0.85 eV   ← rising toward 0
⚡ JEE Trick Higher orbit = higher (less negative) energy = more weakly bound. The gaps also shrink fast (∝ 1/n²), which is why spectral lines crowd together near the series limit. Trap: "less negative" IS an increase — students wrongly call −0.85 "smaller" than −13.6; in signed terms it is larger.
Answer: (c) Increases (less negative).

9How does KE of the electron vary with orbit radius r?KE vs radiusPotential Energy, Kinetic Energy and Total Energy of the Electron in Bohr's Orbit for JEE and NEET🟢 ExactcKE ∝ 1/r
Concept In a circular Bohr orbit the electron's kinetic energy comes from balancing the Coulomb attraction against the centripetal requirement. Working the force balance shows KE equals half the magnitude of the Coulomb potential energy, and since that potential goes as 1/r, so does the kinetic energy. Physically, a smaller (tighter) orbit means a faster electron and higher KE.
Formula KE = ½ · (ke²/r) ∝ 1/r. Force balance ke²/r² = mv²/r gives mv² = ke²/r, so KE = ½mv² = ke²/2r, which is inversely proportional to r.
Solution
1. Coulomb attraction supplies the centripetal force: ke²/r² = mv²/r.
2. Multiply both sides by r: mv² = ke²/r.
3. Kinetic energy KE = ½mv² = ½ · (ke²/r) = ke²/(2r).
4. Since k, e are constants, KE ∝ 1/r — option (c).Everything except r is fixed, so KE simply scales inversely with the radius.

⚡ JEE Trick For a 1/r potential: KE ∝ 1/r, PE ∝ −1/r, TE ∝ −1/r — all scale the same way with radius. Since rn ∝ n², this also gives KE ∝ 1/n². Trap: KE rises as r shrinks (inner orbits are faster), so small r ⇒ large KE, not the reverse.

Concept In a Bohr orbit the Coulomb attraction is exactly the centripetal force, and that single balance locks the three energies together: KE is positive, PE is twice as large and negative, and TE = KE + PE comes out equal and opposite to KE. Solution
1. Force balance: kZe²/r² = mv²/r ⇒ mv² = kZe²/r.Coulomb pull supplies the centripetal requirement for the circular orbit.

3. Potential energy of the electron–nucleus pair: PE = −kZe²/r = −2 KE.Attractive Coulomb pair, so PE is negative with twice the magnitude of KE.
