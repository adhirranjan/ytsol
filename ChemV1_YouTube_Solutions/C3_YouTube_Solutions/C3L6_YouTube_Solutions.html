1(Level 1) The reactant completely consumed first that determines the amount of product formed: Excess / Limiting / Catalyst / Stoichiometric reagentLimiting reagent — definitionLimiting Reagent — Class 11🟡 SimilarbLimiting reagent
Concept When two reactants are mixed in amounts that are not in the exact ratio demanded by the balanced equation, one of them runs out before the other. The reactant that is used up first stops the reaction — once it is gone, no more product can form no matter how much of the other reactant is still lying around. That controlling reactant is called the limiting reagent, and it alone fixes the maximum amount of product (the theoretical yield).
Definitions Limiting reagent = reactant fully consumed first, decides the product amount. Excess reagent = the reactant left over. Catalyst = speeds the reaction but is not consumed. Stoichiometric = a mixture in the exact balanced ratio, where nothing is left over. Only the "consumed first / decides product" description matches the limiting reagent.
Solution
1. Read the defining phrase: "completely consumed first" AND "determines the amount of product formed."Two properties are quoted; the correct term must satisfy both.
2. Test each option against both properties:
 option           consumed first?   sets product amount?   fit
 Excess reagent   no (left over)    no                     ✗
 Limiting reagent yes               yes                    ✓
 Catalyst         no (recovered)    no                     ✗
 Stoichiometric   nothing left      shared, not one        ✗
3. Only "Limiting reagent" satisfies both clauses, so it is option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Fix the identity chain in one line: limiting = runs out first = decides product; excess = leftover. A catalyst is never a reagent that gets "consumed," so it can be struck out instantly. Trap: "stoichiometric reagent" sounds technical but describes the balanced-ratio case where there is no limiting reagent at all.
Answer: (b) Limiting reagent.
2(Level 2) For A + B → products (1:1), 2 mol A mixed with 3 mol B. Identify the limiting reagentLimiting reagent (mol ratio)Limiting Reagent with numericals🟡 SimilaraA
Concept The balanced equation demands a fixed mole ratio between reactants. When you supply them in some other ratio, one reactant is short relative to what the equation asks. The short one — the limiting reagent — is found by comparing "how much you have" against "how much you need" for each reactant, using the coefficients as the currency of exchange.
Formula For each reactant compute the mole ÷ coefficient ratio; the smallest value marks the limiting reagent. Dividing moles by the coefficient tells how many "reaction packets" each reactant can supply; whichever supplies the fewest packets runs out first. Reaction A + B → products has coefficients 1 for A and 1 for B.
Solution
1. Write the required ratio from the equation: A : B = 1 : 1.One mole of A reacts with exactly one mole of B.
2. Divide each supply by its coefficient:
  • A: 2 ÷ 1 = 2.0 packets
  • B: 3 ÷ 1 = 3.0 packets
3. Compare: 2.0 < 3.0, so A supplies fewer packets and is the limiting reagent.2 mol A can consume only 2 mol B, leaving 3 − 2 = 1 mol B unreacted (B is in excess).
4. Cross-check by the "need" method: to use all 3 mol B you would need 3 mol A, but only 2 mol A is present ⇒ A runs out first. ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Always divide moles by coefficient and pick the minimum — it works for any ratio, unlike naïvely picking the smaller mole number. Here the raw moles (2 vs 3) happen to agree, but for a 1 : 2 reaction the smaller mole count can be the excess. Trap: don't call B limiting just because... it isn't the smaller number — always run the ÷coefficient test.
Answer: (a) A.
3(Level 3) 4 mol X mixed with 5 mol Y for X + 2Y → Products. Identify limiting reagent and excess leftLimiting & excess leftEasiest trick for limiting reagent🟡 SimilarcY is limiting, 1.5 mol of X remains
Concept This is a two-part limiting-reagent problem: first find which reactant runs out, then find how much of the other is left. The coefficient 2 in front of Y means each mole of X devours two moles of Y, so Y is consumed twice as fast per reaction event. We use the mole ÷ coefficient test to spot the limiting reagent, then subtract the amount actually used from the amount supplied of the excess reagent.
Formula Limiting reagent = min of mole ÷ coefficient. Amount of excess consumed = (mol of limiting ÷ its coeff) × (coeff of excess); left over = supplied − consumed. Coefficients: X = 1, Y = 2.
Solution
1. Ratio test — divide each supply by its coefficient:
  • X: 4 ÷ 1 = 4.0
  • Y: 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5
2. 2.5 < 4.0, so Y is the limiting reagent (fewest reaction packets).Y is used up twice as fast, so despite 5 mol being more than 4 mol of X, Y still runs out first.
3. Find X consumed by the 5 mol Y using X : Y = 1 : 2 ⇒ X used = 5 × (1 ÷ 2) = 2.5 mol.Every 2 mol Y pulls in 1 mol X, so 5 mol Y pulls in 2.5 mol X.
4. X remaining = supplied − used = 4 − 2.5 = 1.5 mol X.
 reactant   mol   coeff   mol/coeff   role       used    left
 X           4     1       4.0         excess     2.5     1.5 mol
 Y           5     2       2.5  ← min  LIMITING   5.0     0
⚡ JEE Trick The larger raw mole count (5 mol Y) can still be limiting once a coefficient > 1 enters — always divide by the coefficient before judging. For the leftover, convert the limiting reagent straight into the excess using the coefficient ratio and subtract. Trap: don't stop at "Y is limiting"; the question also asks the excess left, so finish the subtraction to reach 1.5 mol X.
Answer: (c) Y is limiting, 1.5 mol of X remains.
4(Level 4) Doubling the excess reagent does not change the amount of product formed. Why?Product depends on limiting reagentIntroduction to Limiting & Excess Reactant🟡 SimilarbProduct depends only on the limiting reagent
Concept The maximum product a reaction can make is set entirely by the reactant that runs out first — the limiting reagent. The excess reagent already has more than enough material; piling on even more of it just increases the leftover pile, because there is no additional limiting reagent for it to react with. So the theoretical yield is "capped" by the limiting reagent alone.
Key idea product formed ∝ moles of limiting reagent (through the balanced equation). Product is generated only when both partners are available; once the limiting reagent is exhausted the reaction halts, regardless of how much excess remains.
Solution
1. Take a concrete case: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 2 mol H₂ (limiting) and 5 mol O₂ (excess).H₂ needs O₂ = 1 mol; only 1 of the 5 mol O₂ is used.
2. Product = 2 mol H₂O, fixed by the 2 mol H₂.
3. Now double the excess: O₂ = 10 mol, H₂ still 2 mol. H₂ is still limiting, still 2 mol.
4. Product is still 2 mol H₂O — unchanged; only the leftover O₂ grows (from 4 to 9 mol).Extra excess has no partner to react with, so it cannot make extra product.
 O₂ supplied   H₂ (limiting)   H₂O formed   O₂ left
 5 mol         2 mol           2 mol        4 mol
 10 mol        2 mol           2 mol        9 mol   ← product same
5. Hence the product depends only on the limiting reagent — option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Yield is "bottlenecked" by the limiting reagent — think of a sandwich needing 2 bread + 1 filling: adding more filling makes no extra sandwiches once bread runs out. Trap: options claiming product rises with excess, or depends on total mass, ignore the bottleneck — only the limiting reagent counts.
Answer: (b) Product depends only on the limiting reagent.
5(Level 5) For 2M + N → product, 6 mol M + 4 mol N, then 2 mol more N added (M stays 6 mol). What happens to product?Excess reagent added — no changeIntroduction to Limiting & Excess Reactant🟡 SimilarbProduct remains unchanged because M is the limiting reagent
Concept To predict what happens when we add more of one reactant, we must first know which reactant is limiting. If the added reactant is the excess one, the product cannot increase, because the limiting reagent is unchanged and still caps the yield. Here M stays fixed while N is increased, so the outcome hinges on whether M or N is limiting.
Formula Limiting reagent = min of mole ÷ coefficient. Coefficients: M = 2, N = 1. Product is set by the limiting reagent; adding excess leaves it unchanged.
Solution
1. Ratio test on the original mixture (6 mol M, 4 mol N):
  • M: 6 ÷ 2 = 3.0
  • N: 4 ÷ 1 = 4.0
2. 3.0 < 4.0, so M is limiting and N is in excess.6 mol M needs only 3 mol N; the 4th mol of N is already surplus.
3. Now add 2 mol N → N = 6 mol, but M is still 6 mol.The added N piles onto the already-excess reactant; M — the bottleneck — is untouched.
4. Re-test: M: 6 ÷ 2 = 3.0; N: 6 ÷ 1 = 6.0. M still limiting at 3.0 packets ⇒ same product.Product is capped by M, which did not change, so the yield is identical.
 stage        M(mol)  N(mol)  M/2   N/1   limiting   product
 original      6       4       3.0   4.0   M          set by M
 +2 mol N      6       6       3.0   6.0   M          UNCHANGED
⚡ JEE Trick Adding more of the excess reagent never raises the yield — only adding the limiting reagent (or both in ratio) can. Identify the bottleneck first, then ask whether the change touched it. Trap: the raw counts (M and N both 6 after addition) tempt "now they're equal" — but with coefficient 2 on M, equal moles still leaves M limiting.
Answer: (b) Product remains unchanged because M is the limiting reagent.

1What happens to the excess reagent in a chemical reaction? (Remains unreacted)Excess reagent — conceptIntroduction to Limiting & Excess Reactant🟡 SimilarbRemains unreacted
Concept A reaction stops the instant the limiting reagent is used up. Whatever excess reagent has not yet reacted at that moment has no partner left to combine with, so it simply stays behind unreacted in the vessel. It is neither destroyed nor turned into extra product — it is a spectator once the limiting reagent is gone.
Key idea excess left = supplied − amount consumed by the limiting reagent. Only the portion of the excess that matches the limiting reagent's demand reacts; the remainder is inert leftover.
Solution
1. Identify roles: the limiting reagent is fully consumed; the other reactant is the excess.
2. When the limiting reagent hits zero, the reaction halts — no partner remains for the surplus.A collision needs both reactants; with one absent, no further product forms.
3. Therefore the excess reagent remains unreacted (option b) — it is recoverable at the end.
4. Eliminate distractors: it does not "fully convert to product" (that is the limiting reagent), nor does it vanish or change the yield.
⚡ JEE Trick "Excess = leftover" — the very name tells you it survives. In numerical problems the excess left = (initial) − (used), and it is a favourite follow-up question. Trap: choices saying the excess "increases the product" contradict the definition of limiting reagent.
Answer: (b) Remains unreacted.
