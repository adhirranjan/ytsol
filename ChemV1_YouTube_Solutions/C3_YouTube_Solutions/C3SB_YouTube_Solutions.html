1An impure sample of Na₂CO₃ weighing 1.48 g was dissolved in water and to check the purity of sample. Carbonate content was precipitated by CaCl₂ as CaCO₃. The mass of CaCO₃ precipitated was found to be 1.132 g. If impurities contribute negligible in the mass of precipitate, then calculate the % purity of sample.Purity from gravimetric precipitationPercentage Purity with example - Mole Concept🟡 Similar81
Concept This is a gravimetric purity problem. The impurity is inert — it never reaches the precipitate — so every mole of CaCO₃ you weigh came from one mole of Na₂CO₃ in the sample. Work backwards: precipitate → moles → mass of pure Na₂CO₃ → percentage of the 1.48 g taken. Do not compare the two masses directly; they are different compounds.
Formula n(CaCO₃) = n(Na₂CO₃), then % purity = mass of pure salt ÷ mass of sample × 100. Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl is 1 : 1 in carbonate, so the carbonate is simply transferred, mole for mole.
Solution
1. M(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol; M(Na₂CO₃) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106 g/mol.
2. n(CaCO₃) = 1.132 ÷ 100 = 0.01132 mol.Mass of precipitate ÷ its molar mass.
3. n(Na₂CO₃) = 0.01132 mol as well.One CO₃²⁻ in, one CaCO₃ out — the impurity adds nothing to the precipitate.
4. Mass of pure Na₂CO₃ = 0.01132 × 106 = 1.200 g.
5. % purity = 1.200 ÷ 1.48 × 100 = 81.1 % ≈ 81 %.
6. Sanity check: impurity = 1.48 − 1.20 = 0.28 g, i.e. 19 % — and 81 + 19 = 100 ✓.
 species   M       mass      moles
 CaCO3     100     1.132 g   1.132/100 = 0.01132
 Na2CO3    106     ?         same = 0.01132
 pure salt 0.01132 x 106 = 1.200 g
 sample    1.48 g  -> 1.200/1.48 x 100 = 81.1 %
 impurity  1.48 - 1.20 = 0.28 g  -> 19 %
⚡ JEE Trick In any purity-by-precipitation item, route through moles, never masses: 1.132/1.48 = 76.5 % is the manufactured distractor. The impurity is defined as inert, so it contributes to the denominator only. Ratio 106/100 > 1 tells you the pure mass must exceed the precipitate mass — if your answer says otherwise, you inverted the factor.
Answer: 81 % (81.1 %).
2A 100 ml solution of 0.1 M HCl was titrated with 0.2 N NaOH solution. The titration was discontinued after adding 30 mL of NaOH solution. The remaining titration was completed by adding 0.25 N KOH solution. The volume of KOH required for completing the titration is (a) 70 mL (b) 32 mL (c) 35 mL (d) 16 mLEquivalent-based titration100 mL 0.1 N HCl, 0.2 N NaOH then 0.25 N KOH titration🟢 Exactd16 mL
Concept Two different bases neutralise one acid, so drop moles and work in milliequivalents. Equivalents add up regardless of which base supplies them: meq of acid = meq of NaOH + meq of KOH. That is the law of equivalence, not a mole ratio — normality already carries the n-factor, so no balancing is needed.
Formula meq = N × V(mL) and meq(acid) = Σ meq(base). For HCl and for monoacidic bases the n-factor is 1, so normality equals molarity and 0.1 M HCl is 0.1 N.
Solution
1. HCl is monobasic, so N = M = 0.1 N.n-factor 1: one replaceable H⁺ per formula unit.
2. meq(HCl) = 0.1 × 100 = 10 meq to be neutralised in total.
3. meq(NaOH) = 0.2 × 30 = 6 meq before the titration was stopped.
4. Acid still unneutralised = 10 − 6 = 4 meq.Equivalents from either base are interchangeable — only the running total matters.
5. V(KOH) = meq ÷ N = 4 ÷ 0.25 = 16 mL — option (d).
6. Check: 6 + 0.25 × 16 = 6 + 4 = 10 meq = meq(HCl) ✓.
 step        N      V(mL)   meq = N x V
 HCl taken   0.1    100     10.0
 NaOH added  0.2     30      6.0
 balance                    10 - 6 = 4.0
 KOH needed  0.25    ?      4/0.25 = 16 mL
 check       6.0 + 0.25 x 16 = 10.0 meq = HCl
⚡ JEE Trick Milliequivalents are additive — that single line kills the whole problem. The distractors are made the usual ways: 70 mL if you forget the NaOH already did part of the job (10/0.25 minus nothing), 32 mL if you use 0.1 M as 0.2 N somewhere, 35 mL if you mix normality with volume ratios. Whenever two titrants of different normality attack one analyte, convert everything to meq first and never touch a balanced equation.
Answer: (d) 16 mL.
3A sample of clay was partially dried and then found to contain 50% silica and 7% water. The original clay contained 12% water. What is the percentage of silica in the original sample?Percentage composition on dryingPartially dried clay: 50% silica, 7% water🟢 Exact47.3
Concept Drying removes only water — silica and the rest of the mineral matter are non-volatile, so their combined mass is conserved through the drying. The difficulty is that the two percentage sets are quoted on different totals. Fix a 100 g basis on the sample you know best (the dried clay), find the invariant water-free mass, then use the 12 % figure to recover the original mass.
Formula Water-free matter is invariant: (100 − %w₁)·W₁ = (100 − %w₂)·W₂, then % silica = mass of silica ÷ W(original) × 100. The silica mass itself never changes; its percentage rises on drying only because the denominator shrinks.
Solution
1. Basis: 100 g of the partially dried clay = 50 g silica + 7 g water + 43 g other solids.
2. Water-free matter = 100 − 7 = 93 g.This 93 g is untouched by drying — it is the anchor of the whole problem.
3. The original clay was 12 % water, so its dry matter was 88 %: 0.88 × W = 93 ⇒ W = 93 ÷ 0.88 = 105.68 g.
4. Silica inside that original 105.68 g is still 50 g — none of it evaporated.
5. % silica (original) = 50 ÷ 105.68 × 100 = 47.31 % ≈ 47.3 %.
6. Check: original water = 105.68 − 93 = 12.68 g = 12.0 % ✓, and 12.68 − 7 = 5.68 g of water was driven off.

⚡ JEE Trick One line does it — multiply the known percentage by the ratio of the two water-free fractions: 50 × 88/93 = 47.3 %. Inverting that ratio (50 × 93/88 = 52.8 %) is the standard slip, so check the direction first: the original clay is wetter, so every dry component must show a smaller percentage in it than in the dried clay. Never subtract the 5 % water difference straight off 50 %.
Answer: 47.3 % silica in the original clay.
4A quantity of 1 g of metallic carbonate XCO₃ is completely converted into a chloride XCl₂ weighing 1.11 g. The atomic mass of the element 'X' is (a) 10 (b) 20 (c) 30 (d) 40Atomic mass from mass ratioMolar mass of metal carbonate M2CO3 from data🟡 Similard40
Concept One mole of XCO₃ gives exactly one mole of XCl₂ — X is conserved, only the anion is swapped. So the same number of moles is expressed twice, once with molar mass M + 60 and once with M + 71. Equate the two mole counts and M falls out. This is not a limiting-reagent question; nothing is in excess.
Formula 1 ÷ (M + 60) = 1.11 ÷ (M + 71). M(CO₃²⁻) = 12 + 48 = 60 and M(Cl₂) = 2 × 35.5 = 71, so the two molar masses differ only by the anion attached to the same X.
Solution
1. M(XCO₃) = M + 60 and M(XCl₂) = M + 71.X keeps its +2 charge, so it takes one CO₃²⁻ or two Cl⁻.
2. n(XCO₃) = 1/(M + 60) and n(XCl₂) = 1.11/(M + 71); complete conversion makes these equal.
3. Cross-multiply: M + 71 = 1.11(M + 60) = 1.11M + 66.6.
4. 71 − 66.6 = 1.11M − M → 4.4 = 0.11M.
5. M = 4.4 ÷ 0.11 = 40 — option (d), i.e. calcium.
6. Check: CaCO₃ = 100, CaCl₂ = 111; 1 g CaCO₃ = 0.01 mol → 0.01 × 111 = 1.11 g ✓.
 species  molar mass   mass    moles
 XCO3     M + 60       1.00 g  1/(M+60)
 XCl2     M + 71       1.11 g  1.11/(M+71)

⚡ JEE Trick Whenever one salt is completely converted into another of the same metal, write mass₁/M₁ = mass₂/M₂ and solve — no equation, no ratio table. Shortcut worth memorising: mass gain per mole = 71 − 60 = 11, and here the gain is 0.11 g, so n = 0.01 mol and M = 1/0.01 − 60 = 40 in one line. The distractors 10, 20, 30 come from using Cl = 35.5 once instead of twice, or CO₃ = 44 (CO₂!) instead of 60.
Answer: (d) 40.
5A 1.50 g sample of potassium bicarbonate having 80% purity is strongly heated. Assuming the impurity to be thermally stable, the loss in weight of the sample on heating is (a) 3.72 g (b) 0.72 g (c) 0.372 g (d) 0.186 gThermal decomposition mass lossMass lost in KHCO3 decomposition - worked solution🟡 Similarc0.372 g
Concept Two ideas stacked: % purity first, then mass loss on decomposition. Only the 80 % that is KHCO₃ reacts — the impurity is thermally stable and stays put, so it can never appear in the weight loss. The loss itself is just the mass of the gases that escape, H₂O and CO₂; the K₂CO₃ residue stays in the crucible.
Formula 2KHCO₃ → K₂CO₃ + H₂O↑ + CO₂↑, so loss = n(KHCO₃) × 31. Per 2 mol (200 g) KHCO₃ the escaping gases are 18 + 44 = 62 g, i.e. 31 g of loss per mole of KHCO₃.
Solution
1. Pure KHCO₃ = 1.50 × 0.80 = 1.2 g.The other 0.30 g is inert impurity — it neither decomposes nor evaporates.
2. M(KHCO₃) = 39 + 1 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol, so n = 1.2 ÷ 100 = 0.012 mol.
3. From the equation, 2 mol KHCO₃ (200 g) → K₂CO₃ (138 g) + H₂O (18 g) + CO₂ (44 g).
4. Gases lost per 2 mol = 18 + 44 = 62 g, so loss per mole = 31 g.Check by residue: 200 − 138 = 62 g, the same number.
5. Loss = 0.012 × 31 = 0.372 g — option (c).
6. Sanity: final mass = 1.50 − 0.372 = 1.128 g = 0.828 g K₂CO₃ + 0.30 g impurity ✓.

⚡ JEE Trick Memorise 31 g lost per mole of MHCO₃ (half of 62) — bicarbonates of Na and K both follow it. Answer: (c) 0.372 g.
6If the atomic mass were given by as 1/6ᵗʰ part and molecular mass as 1/12ᵗʰ part by mass of one atom of C¹² isotope, then what would be the molecular mass of water? Suppose atomic masses of hydrogen and oxygen on new scale are 1 and 16, respectively, (a) 18 (b) 9 (c) 36 (d) UnpredictableRedefined atomic mass scale1/6 in place of 1/12 mass of carbon atom scale🟡 Similarc36
Concept Atomic and molecular masses are only ratios to a chosen reference. The question breaks the usual convention: atoms are weighed against (1/6)mC-12 but molecules against (1/12)mC-12. Absolute masses of atoms never change — only the yardstick does. So convert the given atomic masses into real mass, add them, then divide by the molecular unit.
Formula real mass = (atomic mass) × (1/6)mC and molecular mass = real mass ÷ (1/12)mC. A "mass" on any scale is just real mass ÷ unit of that scale, so switching from a 1/6 unit to a 1/12 unit doubles every number.
Solution
1. On the new atomic scale H = 1, so m(H) = 1 × (1/6)mC = mC/6.
2. O = 16, so m(O) = 16 × (1/6)mC = 16mC/6.
3. Real mass of H₂O = 2(mC/6) + 16mC/6 = (18/6)mC = 3mC.Adding real masses is always legal — that step is scale-free.
4. Molecular mass = real mass ÷ (1/12)mC = 3mC × 12/mC = 36 — option (c).
5. Shortcut: the molecular unit is half the atomic unit, so the number doubles: 18 × 2 = 36 ✓.Smaller unit ⇒ larger number, for the same physical mass.
6. Note nothing physical changed — a water molecule still weighs 3 × the mass of one C-12 atom on either scale.
 species  new atomic mass   real mass (in mC)
 H        1                 1/6
 O        16                16/6
 H2O      2(1/6) + 16/6  =  18/6 = 3 mC
 mol unit (1/12) mC
 mol mass 3 mC / ((1/12) mC) = 36
⚡ JEE Trick On any redefined scale, ask only: did the unit get bigger or smaller? Here the molecular unit (1/12) is half the atomic unit (1/6), so every molecular number doubles — 18 → 36, no algebra needed. Distractor (a) 18 is for candidates who never notice the two scales differ; (b) 9 halves instead of doubling; (d) "unpredictable" tempts anyone who thinks a changed unit changes chemistry. It does not — only the label on the number.
Answer: (c) 36.
7The vapour density of a sample of hydrogen fluoride gas is measured by an experiment as 20. It may represent that (F = 19) (a) some molecules of hydrogen fluoride are dissociated (b) some molecules of hydrogen fluoride are in dimer form (c) all hydrogen fluoride molecules are in dimer form (d) some hydrogen fluoride molecules are in trimer formVapour density and associationVapour density of HF gas measured as 20🟢 Exactc, d
Concept Vapour density is a measured quantity; the molar mass it reports is only an average over whatever species are actually in the vapour. Compare 2 × VD with the true formula mass: bigger means association, smaller means dissociation. Here 2 × 20 = 40 is exactly double HF's 20, so the vapour must be twice as heavy per particle — and this is a multi-correct item, so every option must be tested.
Formula M(apparent) = 2 × VD; for a fraction α of the units n-merised, M(app) = M ÷ (1 − α + α/n). The total mass is fixed while the particle count falls from 1 to (1 − α + α/n), so the average mass rises by exactly that reciprocal factor.
Solution
1. True HF: M = 1 + 19 = 20, so its normal VD = 10. Observed VD = 20 ⇒ M(app) = 40 g/mol.
2. M(app) > M ⇒ association. So (a) is wrong — dissociation splits particles and drives M(app) below 20.
3. (c) all molecules dimeric: (HF)₂ has M = 40 exactly ⇒ VD = 20 ✓ correct.
4. (b) only some dimerised: the vapour is a mix of M = 20 and M = 40 particles, so 20 < M(app) < 40 — it can never reach 40 ✗.
5. (d) some trimerised: 20 ÷ (1 − 2α/3) = 40 ⇒ 1 − 2α/3 = ½ ⇒ α = 0.75. Legitimate (0 < α < 1): 75 % of the HF units sit in trimers, 25 % stay monomeric ✓ correct.
6. Correct options: (c) and (d).
 VD 20  ->  M(app) = 40 ; true M(HF) = 20
 (a) dissociation  -> M(app) < 20        x
 (b) some dimer    -> 20 < M(app) < 40   x
 (c) all dimer (HF)2 = 40                <-- answer
 (d) trimer, a = 3/4 : 20/(1-2a/3) = 40  <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Always write M(app) = 2 × VD first, then place it against the true formula mass — above ⇒ association, below ⇒ dissociation. The subtle trap is (b) vs (d): partial aggregation gives an intermediate mass, so it can only hit 2M if the aggregate is bigger than a dimer. Partial dimerisation is bounded above by 40 and so is excluded, while partial trimerisation reaches 40 comfortably at α = ¾.
Answer: (c) and (d).
8A volume of 10 ml of a mixture of H₂ and O₂ is exploded. If the final volume becomes 1 ml, the composition of original mixture may be (a) 7 ml H₂, 3 ml O₂ (b) 6 ml H₂, 4 ml O₂ (c) 5 ml H₂, 5 ml O₂ (d) 3 ml H₂, 7 ml O₂Eudiometry of H2-O2 explosion10 ml H2 + O2 exploded, final volume 1 ml🟢 Exacta, b
Concept After the spark the tube is cooled, so the water condenses and contributes nothing to the final volume. Whatever 1 mL survives must therefore be the excess reagent — and it may be excess H₂ or excess O₂. That is precisely why this multi-correct item has two answers: each "residual volume" equation has two branches, and both must be solved.
Formula 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O(l), so volumes react 2 : 1. With h mL of H₂ in the 10 mL: O₂ left = (10 − h) − h/2, H₂ left = h − 2(10 − h). At one T and P, volume ratios equal mole ratios (Avogadro), so the coefficients can be read directly as millilitres.
Solution
1. Contraction = 10 − 1 = 9 mL of gas consumed; the surviving 1 mL is a single unreacted gas.
2. Reacting mixture is 3 volumes (2 H₂ + 1 O₂), so 9 mL consumed = 6 mL H₂ + 3 mL O₂ reacted.This one step fixes the reacted part; the leftover 1 mL is then simply added to whichever gas is in excess.
3. Branch A — the 1 mL is excess O₂: mixture = 6 mL H₂ + 4 mL O₂ = option (b) ✓ (10 − 1.5×6 = 1 ✓).
4. Branch B — the 1 mL is excess H₂: mixture = 7 mL H₂ + 3 mL O₂ = option (a) ✓ (3×7 − 20 = 1 ✓).
5. Test (c) 5 + 5: H₂ limiting, O₂ used = 2.5, residue = 2.5 mL ✗. Test (d) 3 + 7: O₂ used = 1.5, residue = 5.5 mL ✗.
6. Correct options: (a) and (b).

  6    4     H2       3.0      1 mL O2   <-- answer (b)

Concept "Ratio of the atoms" means a ratio of moles, not of masses — Avogadro's number cancels, so atom ratio = mole ratio. Convert A's mass to moles, scale by the given ratio to get B's moles, then convert back to mass with B's atomic weight. Doping problems always run mass → mole → ratio → mole → mass.
Formula n = mass ÷ atomic weight and N(A) : N(B) = n(A) : n(B) = 1 : 10⁻⁹. N = n × NA for both elements, so the same NA divides out and the atom ratio equals the mole ratio exactly.
