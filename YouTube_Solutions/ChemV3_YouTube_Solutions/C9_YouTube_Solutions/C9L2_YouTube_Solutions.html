1 (i)
Fill in the blank: Water has maximum density at ________.
C9L2VRecapQ1iMaximum density of water
🟢 ExactWater has maximum de…
4°C
Concept Most liquids keep contracting on cooling until they freeze, so their density is greatest just above the freezing point. Water is anomalous: its density peaks at 4 °C (277 K) and then falls on further cooling to 0 °C. The trap is to answer 0 °C, the freezing point, because ice at 0 °C is less dense than water at 4 °C.
Key relation density of water: maximum at 4 °C = 1.000 g/cm3; ice at 0 °C = 0.917 g/cm3 Two opposing effects, thermal contraction and growth of open hydrogen-bonded clusters, balance exactly at 4 °C.
Solution
1. Above 4 °C, cooling slows the molecular motion, so the molecules pack closer and the density rises as in any normal liquid.
2. Below 4 °C, water molecules begin to organise into open, cage-like hydrogen-bonded clusters that resemble the structure of ice.
3. Each such cluster holds the molecules further apart than in the random liquid, so the density now falls on cooling.
4. The turning point, where contraction and cluster formation cancel, is at 4 °C (more exactly 3.98 °C), where ρ = 1.000 g/cm3.
5. At 0 °C freezing completes the open lattice and the volume jumps by about 9%: ρ(ice) = 1/1.09 = 0.917 g/cm3, so ice floats.
6. Consequence: in a freezing lake the 4 °C water sinks to the bottom while ice forms on top, which keeps aquatic life alive.
7. Hence the blank is filled by 4 °C (277 K).
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the pair: water densest at 4 °C, ice lighter than water. The trap answer is 0 °C. The same fact is why D2O is densest at 11.2 °C, a variant that is sometimes asked.
Answer: 4 °C.
1 (ii)
Fill in the blank: The structure of ________ is open cage-like structure.
C9L2VRecapQ1iiStructure of ice
🟡 SimilarWhy Does Ice Float o…
ice
Concept In ice every oxygen atom is bonded to four others through hydrogen bonds. This builds a three-dimensional network full of empty hexagonal channels. The question asks which substance has this open cage-like structure; the trap is to name water, whose liquid structure is only partly ordered.
Key relation ice: each O has 2 covalent O–H and 2 hydrogen bonds, tetrahedral, O…O = 276 pm Four-fold tetrahedral hydrogen bonding forces a wide-open hexagonal lattice with large vacant space.
Solution
1. In an H2O molecule the oxygen is sp3 hybridised, with two bond pairs and two lone pairs directed to the corners of a tetrahedron.
2. Each H of one molecule can bond to a lone pair of a neighbour, and each O can accept two such bonds, so every molecule links to four neighbours.
3. Four tetrahedral links per molecule produce six-membered puckered rings of O atoms stacked into a hexagonal (wurtzite-like) lattice.
4. This arrangement has large empty channels, so the structure is an open cage, much like a honeycomb; the O…O distance is 276 pm.
5. When ice melts, about 15% of the hydrogen bonds break and the cage partly collapses, so the liquid is denser than the solid.
6. Liquid water keeps short-lived clusters but has no complete cage, so the description fits ice.
7. The blank is therefore filled by ice.
⚡ JEE Trick Open cage means low density and floating. Whenever a question says “open cage”, “hexagonal” or “less dense”, the answer is ice, not liquid water.
Answer: ice.
1 (iii)
Fill in the blank: ________ is regarded as universal solvent.
C9L2VRecapQ1iiiWater as solvent
🟡 SimilarWhy water is called…
water
Concept A solvent that can dissolve the widest range of substances is called a universal solvent. Water is given this title because it dissolves most ionic compounds and many polar covalent ones. The trap is the word universal: it is a loose description, not a claim that water dissolves everything.
Key relation solvation: ion + n H2O → hydrated ion, ΔHhyd compensates the lattice energy The high dielectric constant (ε = 78.5) and hydrogen bonding let water pull ions apart and hold them.
Solution
1. Water is strongly polar: the O–H bond dipoles do not cancel in the bent molecule, giving μ = 1.84 D.
2. Its high dielectric constant (78.5 at 25 °C) cuts the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions to about 1/78 of the value in vacuum.
3. Water’s partial charges surround each ion (ion–dipole attraction): O points to cations, H points to anions, releasing hydration energy.
4. This hydration energy offsets the lattice energy, so NaCl, KNO3 and many other ionic salts dissolve.
5. Hydrogen bonding with the O–H, N–H and C=O groups makes sugars, alcohols, acids and amines soluble too.
6. Non-polar substances (oils, I2 in large amounts, hydrocarbons) are insoluble, which is why the term is only “universal” in a broad sense.
7. The blank is filled by water.
⚡ JEE Trick Universal solvent = water. Do not write ammonia or alcohol; ammonia is only a good non-aqueous solvent. Remember that “like dissolves like”: water takes polar and ionic solutes only.
Answer: Water.
1 (iv)
Fill in the blank: ________ hydrides have exact number of electrons to form normal covalent bond.
C9L2VRecapQ1ivElectron-precise hydrides
🟡 SimilarMoleular hydrides ar…
Electron precise
Concept Covalent (molecular) hydrides of p-block elements are sorted by comparing the available valence electrons with the number needed for normal two-centre two-electron bonds. The hydrides that have exactly the electrons needed are electron-precise, for example CH4, SiH4 and GeH4 of group 14. The trap is mixing up this class with electron-deficient and electron-rich.
Key relation electron-precise: valence electrons = 2 × (number of bonds), no lone pair, no vacant bonding position Group 14 central atoms have 4 valence electrons and form 4 normal bonds, so nothing is left over and nothing is lacking.
Solution
1. Three types of covalent hydride exist: electron-deficient (fewer electrons than needed), electron-precise (exact number) and electron-rich (excess).
2. The question describes hydrides with the exact number of electrons to form normal covalent bonds, which defines the electron-precise type.
3. Test with CH4: C supplies 4 electrons and 4 H atoms supply 4, giving 8 electrons = 4 bond pairs, with no lone pair and no empty orbital.
4. Compare B2H6: 12 valence electrons are shared among 8 atoms needing 16 for normal bonds, so it is electron-deficient (3c–2e bonds).
5. Compare NH3: 8 electrons form 3 bonds and leave 1 lone pair, so it is electron-rich (groups 15–17).
6. The hydrides of group 14 (CH4, SiH4, GeH4, SnH4) therefore make up the precise class.
7. The blank is filled by electron precise.
⚡ JEE Trick Group 13 = deficient, group 14 = precise, groups 15–17 = rich. Precise hydrides have tetrahedral geometry with no lone pair on the central atom.
Answer: Electron precise.
1 (v)
Fill in the blank: ________ Hydrides have more number of electrons than normal covalent bonds.
C9L2VRecapQ1vElectron-rich hydrides
🟡 SimilarTypes of hydrides: E…
Electron rich
Concept In an electron-rich hydride the central atom has more electrons than are needed to form its normal bonds, and the extra electrons stay as lone pairs. Hydrides of groups 15, 16 and 17 (NH3, H2O, HF) belong here. The trap is to call them electron-precise because the bonds themselves are normal.
Key relation electron-rich: lone pairs on the central atom = (valence electrons − bonds formed) / 2 ≥ 1 Group 15–17 atoms have 5, 6 or 7 valence electrons but form only 3, 2 or 1 bonds, so lone pairs remain.
Solution
1. The stem asks for hydrides with more electrons than the normal covalent bonds need, which is the electron-rich class.
2. For NH3: N has 5 valence electrons and forms 3 N–H bonds, so 5 − 3 = 2 electrons remain as 1 lone pair.
3. For H2O: O has 6 valence electrons and forms 2 bonds, so 6 − 2 = 4 electrons remain as 2 lone pairs.
4. For HF: F has 7 valence electrons and forms 1 bond, so 7 − 1 = 6 electrons remain as 3 lone pairs.
5. These lone pairs are what allow hydrogen bonding and make the molecules Lewis bases, as in NH3 + H+ → NH4+.
6. CH4 (no lone pair) is precise and B2H6 (too few electrons) is deficient, so neither fits the stem.
7. The blank is filled by electron rich.
⚡ JEE Trick Count lone pairs on the central atom: one or more means electron-rich. Electron-rich hydrides are the ones that show hydrogen bonding (N, O, F).
Answer: Electron rich.
2 (i)
True or false: NH3 is an electron precise hydride.
C9L2VRecapQ2iClassification of NH3
🟡 SimilarAsseration: Ammonia…
False
Concept Hydrides are classified by comparing the electrons present with those needed for normal bonds. NH3 has a lone pair left on nitrogen after forming three bonds, so it is not precise. The trap is that all three N–H bonds are normal two-electron bonds, which tempts one to call it precise.
Key relation valence electrons of N = 5; bonds formed = 3; lone pairs = (5 − 3)/2 = 1 A lone pair means more electrons than normal bonding needs, so the hydride is electron-rich.
Solution
1. The statement says NH3 is electron precise; test it by counting electrons on nitrogen.
2. N has 5 valence electrons and H atoms supply 3, so there are 8 electrons in all, i.e. 4 pairs around N.
3. Only 3 of the 4 pairs are bond pairs; the fourth is a lone pair on N.
4. A precise hydride has exactly enough electrons for its bonds and no lone pair, as in CH4 (8 electrons, 4 bond pairs).
5. NH3 has an excess pair, so it is electron-rich, like H2O and HF (groups 15–17).
6. Its lone pair explains the pyramidal shape, the basic nature and the hydrogen bonding (b.p. 240 K).
7. The statement is therefore false.
⚡ JEE Trick Electron-precise belongs to group 14 only (CH4, SiH4). Any hydride with a lone pair on the central atom is electron-rich.

2 (ii)
True or false: Ionic hydrides have high melting & boiling point.
C9L2VRecapQ2iiIonic hydrides
🟡 SimilarChemistry Hydrogen p…
True
Concept Ionic (saline) hydrides such as NaH and CaH2 are crystalline solids made of M+ or M2+ and H− ions. They are held by strong electrostatic lattice forces, so a great deal of energy is needed to melt or boil them. The trap is to confuse them with covalent hydrides, which are gases or volatile liquids.
Key relation lattice energy ∝ (q+q−)/(r+ + r−); high lattice energy gives high m.p. Strong ion–ion attraction extends through the whole crystal.
Solution
1. Alkali and heavier alkaline-earth metals are strongly electropositive and transfer an electron to hydrogen, forming H− (hydride) ions.
2. The solid is a three-dimensional ionic lattice, usually of NaCl type for alkali hydrides.
3. Melting means separating ions held by strong coulombic forces, so the energy needed is high; NaH decomposes near 800 K, and CaH2 melts at about 1089 K.
4. Saline hydrides are therefore hard, non-volatile, crystalline, non-conducting as solids and conducting in the melt.
5. In the melt, electrolysis gives H2 at the anode (2H− → H2 + 2e−), the proof of H− ions.
6. By contrast covalent hydrides such as CH4 (b.p. 112 K) are gases because only weak van der Waals forces act.
7. The statement is true.
⚡ JEE Trick Ionic hydrides are the s-block salts and conduct only when molten, liberating H2 at the anode. High m.p. is the hallmark of ionic bonding, as with NaCl.
Answer: True.
2 (iii)
True or false: The order of thermal stability of the alkali metal hydride is LiH > NaH > RH > RbH > CsH
C9L2VRecapQ2iiiStability of alkali hydrides
🟡 SimilarThe correct order of…
True
Concept Thermal stability of an alkali metal hydride falls as the metal ion grows larger down the group. The stem prints “RH”, which is a misprint for KH, so the series reads LiH > NaH > KH > RbH > CsH. The trap is to treat the printed RH as a different compound; the order itself is the standard one.
Key relation stability ∝ lattice energy ∝ 1/(rM+ + rH−) H− is large and polarisable, and a bigger cation packs it less tightly and gives a smaller lattice energy.
Solution
1. All alkali hydrides are ionic M+H− solids of the NaCl type, formed by M + (1/2)H2 → MH.
2. Going down group 1 from Li to Cs the cation radius increases, so the lattice energy and hence the stability decrease.
3. Li+ is small and polarises H− into a very stable lattice; Cs+ is large and gives the weakest lattice.
4. Decomposition MH → M + (1/2)H2 therefore becomes easier down the group (ΔHf of LiH = −90 kJ/mol, of CsH only −54).
5. Hence the order is LiH > NaH > KH > RbH > CsH, as the stem says.
6. Note the misprint: the printed RH stands for KH; the key treats the statement as true.
7. The statement is true.
⚡ JEE Trick Thermal stability of ionic hydrides, like the carbonates and nitrates of the same group, falls with the cation size here, because the H− is large and soft. LiH is the only one stable to its melting point (961 K).
Answer: True (with RH read as KH).
2 (iv)
True or false: Inter stitial hydrides are also called non-stoichiometric hydride.
C9L2VRecapQ2ivInterstitial hydrides
🟡 SimilarInterstitial Hydride…
True
Concept Interstitial (metallic) hydrides are formed by d-block and f-block metals, in which hydrogen atoms occupy the holes between the metal atoms. Because the holes are not always completely filled, the H : M ratio is not a fixed whole number, so these are also called non-stoichiometric hydrides. The trap is to expect a definite formula such as MH2 every time.
Key relation interstitial hydride: MHx with x variable, e.g. LaH2.87, TiH1.73, PdH0.6–0.8 Occupancy of the octahedral and tetrahedral holes depends on temperature and H2 pressure, so x is not fixed.
Solution
1. When a d- or f-block metal absorbs hydrogen, H2 dissociates and the small H atoms enter the interstitial (octahedral and tetrahedral) holes of the metal lattice.
2. The metal lattice usually expands only slightly and keeps its metallic lustre and conductivity, so the products are called metallic hydrides.
3. Not every hole is filled, and the fraction filled changes with conditions, giving formulae such as LaH2.87, TiH1.8 and PdH0.7.
4. Compounds whose composition does not obey the law of definite proportions are non-stoichiometric (berthollide) compounds.
5. Hence interstitial hydrides and non-stoichiometric hydrides are two names for the same class.
6. Some, like Pd, can absorb up to about 900 times their own volume of H2, a property called occlusion.
7. The statement is true.
⚡ JEE Trick Interstitial = non-stoichiometric = metallic hydride; all three terms are equivalent. Saline hydrides, by contrast, have fixed formulae such as NaH and CaH2.
Answer: True.
2 (v)
True or false: LiAlH4, NaBH4 are examples of complex hydride.
C9L2VRecapQ2vComplex hydrides
🟡 SimilarWhich of the followi…
True
Concept Complex hydrides contain hydride ions bonded to a central atom that forms an anion, such as [AlH4]− or [BH4]−, with an alkali metal cation. LiAlH4 (lithium aluminium hydride) and NaBH4 (sodium borohydride) are the standard examples. The trap is to class them as ionic hydrides like NaH.
Key relation complex hydride: M+[EH4]− with E = Al or B, formed from LiH + AlH3 A hydride donor (H−) is bound to an electron-deficient AlH3 or BH3, making a tetrahedral complex anion.
Solution
1. Complex hydrides are formed when an ionic hydride donates H− to an electron-deficient hydride, e.g. 4LiH + AlCl3 → LiAlH4 + 3LiCl.
2. LiAlH4 consists of Li+ and the tetrahedral [AlH4]− ion (sp3 Al); NaBH4 has [BH4]−.
3. Both contain the central atom coordinated by hydride ligands, which is what the term complex hydride means.
4. They are strong reducing agents (LiAlH4) and a milder one (NaBH4), widely used in organic chemistry.
5. They are also the reagents for making diborane: 4BCl3 + 3LiAlH4 → 2B2H6 + 3LiCl + 3AlCl3.
6. LiAlH4 reacts violently with water: LiAlH4 + 4H2O → LiOH + Al(OH)3 + 4H2.
7. The statement is true.
⚡ JEE Trick Complex hydrides = alanates and borohydrides, and they work as sources of H− for reduction. Keep NaBH4 in the same class as LiAlH4, though it is the milder reductant.
Answer: True.

🟡 SimilarHydrides || Classifi…
A-s, B-r, C-q, D-p
Concept Match each hydride with its type by asking which element forms it and what bonding it shows. BeH2 is a polymeric chain, AsH3 is a covalent molecular hydride, LaH3 is an interstitial (f-block) hydride and LiAlH4 is a complex hydride. The trap is to call BeH2 ionic like the other s-block hydrides.

1. (A) BeH2: Be is small and has high polarising power, so it forms covalent bonds; the hydride has chains with Be–H–Be 3c–2e bridges, i.e. polymeric (A → s).

3. (C) LaH3: La is an f-block (d-block start) metal whose hydride is non-stoichiometric (LaH2.87) with H atoms in lattice holes, so it is interstitial (C → q).
4. (D) LiAlH4: Li+ with the [AlH4]− anion is a complex hydride (D → p).
5. Each column-II entry is used once: p = complex, q = interstitial, r = covalent, s = polymeric, so the pairing is consistent.
6. Check the near-miss: BeH2 would be “covalent” in a loose sense, but because the option “polymeric” is available and AsH3 needs the covalent slot, A → s.
7. The matching is A-s, B-r, C-q, D-p.

C9L2VRecapQ3iiBoiling points of group-15 hydrides
🟡 SimilarThe boiling point of…
A-s, B-q, C-p, D-r
Concept Boiling points of the group-15 hydrides do not fall steadily down the group. NH3 is raised by hydrogen bonding, PH3 is the lowest, and then AsH3 and SbH3 rise with size and van der Waals (dispersion) forces. The trap is to give NH3 the lowest value because it is the lightest.
Key relation b.p.: PH3 (186 K) < AsH3 (211 K) < NH3 (240 K) < SbH3 (256 K) Hydrogen bonding lifts NH3, but the growing dispersion forces of SbH3 beat it.
Solution
1. List the four boiling points from lowest to highest: 186 K (q) < 211 K (p) < 240 K (s) < 256 K (r).
2. For PH3, AsH3 and SbH3 only dispersion forces act, and they grow with molar mass (34, 78, 125 g/mol), so these three boil in the order PH3 < AsH3 < SbH3.
3. PH3 is therefore the lowest, 186 K (B → q); AsH3 is next, 211 K (C → p); SbH3 is highest, 256 K (D → r).
4. NH3 forms N–H…N hydrogen bonds, so its b.p. (240 K) is far above what its mass (17) would give (about 130 K), so it sits at 240 K (A → s).
5. Yet the hydrogen bond is weaker than the dispersion forces in the heavy SbH3, so NH3 falls between AsH3 and SbH3.
6. This gives the irregular trend 240, 186, 211, 256 K down the group.
7. The matching is A-s, B-q, C-p, D-r.
⚡ JEE Trick Rank by b.p.: PH3 lowest, SbH3 highest, NH3 second from the top. Hydrogen bonding makes NH3 abnormal, but it does not make it the highest, a common trap.
Answer: A-s, B-q, C-p, D-r.

Which of the following hydride is electron deficient hydride?
(a) B2H6   (b) NH3   (c) H2O   (d) CH4
C9L2VRecapQ4iElectron-deficient hydride
🟡 SimilarWhich of the followi…
(a) B₂H₆
Concept An electron-deficient hydride has too few valence electrons to form a normal two-electron bond for each connection, so it uses multicentre bonds. Diborane, B2H6, is the standard example (group 13). The trap is to pick NH3 or H2O, which are electron-rich, or CH4, which is precise.
Key relation B2H6: 12 valence electrons, but 8 normal 2c–2e bonds would need 16 electrons Boron has 3 valence electrons and an empty p-orbital, so it needs 3c–2e (banana) bridge bonds.
Solution
1. Count the electrons in each choice and compare with the requirement for normal bonds.
2. (a) B2H6: 2 × 3 (B) + 6 × 1 (H) = 12 electrons, while 8 atoms joined by normal bonds would need 16 electrons. It is deficient by 4 electrons.
3. Diborane makes up for this with two B–H–B 3c–2e bridges (4 electrons) plus 4 terminal 2c–2e bonds (8 electrons) = 12 electrons.
4. (b) NH3: 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, so electron-rich.
5. (c) H2O: 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs, so electron-rich.
6. (d) CH4: 4 bonds, no lone pair, so electron-precise.
7. Only B2H6 is deficient, so the answer is (a).
⚡ JEE Trick Deficient = group 13 (B, Al, Ga). Diborane has 4 terminal and 2 bridging hydrogens, and the bridge bonds are 3c–2e, not ordinary bonds. Distractors: NH3, H2O have lone pairs; CH4 is exactly saturated.
Answer: (a) B2H6.
4 (ii)
The high density of water as compared to ice is due to:
(a) hydrogen bonding interactions   (b) dipole-dipole interactions   (c) dipole-induced dipole interactions   (d) induced dipole induced dipole interactions
C9L2VRecapQ4iiDensity of water vs ice
🟢 ExactThe high density of…
(a) hydrogen bonding interactions
Concept Ice is less dense than liquid water, so the density of water is higher than that of ice. The cause is hydrogen bonding: in ice every molecule is locked into an open cage by four H-bonds, while in water some are broken and the molecules pack closer. The trap is to pick a dipole interaction because water is polar, though these are not what differentiate the two phases.
Key relation ρ(water, 4 °C) = 1.000 g/cm3 > ρ(ice, 0 °C) = 0.917 g/cm3 The difference arises purely from the extent and geometry of hydrogen bonding, not from the other, weaker forces.
Solution
1. In ice each O is bonded by hydrogen bonds to four neighbours in a rigid, open, hexagonal lattice with large empty spaces; hence the low density 0.917 g/cm3.
2. On melting, about 15% of the hydrogen bonds break and the rigid cage collapses; molecules move into the spaces and pack more closely.
3. So the density of water is greater than that of ice by about 9%: 1.000 / 0.917 = 1.09.
4. This difference traces directly to hydrogen bonding, because it is the strong directional H-bond that holds the open lattice. (a) is correct.
5. Dipole–dipole (b) forces act in both phases and are non-directional enough to allow close packing, so they do not give an open structure.
6. Dipole–induced dipole (c) and induced-dipole–induced-dipole (d) forces are far weaker, and they cannot account for the cage.
7. The answer is (a).
⚡ JEE Trick The density anomaly of water, ice floating on water and the 4 °C maximum all trace back to hydrogen bonding. Dipole forces and dispersion forces are present but do not explain the open cage.
Answer: (a) hydrogen bonding interactions.
4 (iii)
The shape of water molecule is same as that of:
(a) C2H2   (b) CO2   (c) NH3   (d) Cl2O
C9L2VRecapQ4iiiShape of water molecule
🟢 ExactThe shape of water m…
(d) Cl2O
Concept Shape is decided by VSEPR: count the bond pairs and lone pairs on the central atom. Water has two bond pairs and two lone pairs on oxygen, so it is bent (V-shaped), not linear. The trap is to match on formula type (H2O vs Cl2O) rather than on electron-pair geometry, and then check every option.
Key relation H2O : AX2E2 → sp3 O, bent, ≈ 104.5° The same AX2E2 pattern in another molecule gives the same shape.
Solution
1. Oxygen in H2O has 6 valence electrons; two are used for O–H bonds, leaving 2 lone pairs.
2. Steric number = 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs = 4, so the hybridisation is sp3 and the electron geometry is tetrahedral.
3. Two lone pairs occupy two corners, so the molecule itself is bent (V-shaped) with an angle compressed from 109.5° to about 104.5°.
4. (a) C2H2: H–C≡C–H, sp carbons, linear. (b) CO2: O=C=O, sp carbon, no lone pair on C, linear.
5. (c) NH3: 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair gives a trigonal pyramid (AX3E), not bent.
6. (d) Cl2O: central O has 2 bond pairs (to Cl) and 2 lone pairs, so AX2E2, bent, the same as water. The angle is larger (≈ 111°) because Cl is bulky, but the shape is identical.
7. So only Cl2O has the same shape as H2O. This agrees with the key (d).
⚡ JEE Trick Match the steric number and lone-pair count, not the formula. Any central atom with 2 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs (H2S, OF2, Cl2O, SCl2) is V-shaped. Linear shapes need zero lone pairs (CO2) or 3 (XeF2) on the centre.
Answer: (d) Cl2O.
4 (iv)
Among C2H2, NH3, NaH and B2H6, which are covalent hydrides?
(a) NH3 and B2H6   (b) NaH and CaH2   (c) NaH and NH3   (d) CaH2 and B2H6
C9L2VRecapQ4ivCovalent hydrides
🟢 ExactAmong `CaH_2, NH_3,…
(a) NH3 and B2H6
Concept Hydrides split by the nature of the other element: s-block metals give ionic (saline) hydrides, p-block elements give covalent (molecular) hydrides, d-block metals give metallic hydrides. The stem prints C2H2 but the options use CaH2, almost certainly a misprint for CaH2 (as in Suggested Q4); the answer is the same either way.
Key relation Covalent hydrides = H + p-block non-metal (N, B, C, O ...); ionic hydrides = H− + s-block metal Electronegativity difference decides ionic versus covalent character.
Solution
1. NaH: Na is a group 1 metal (EN 0.9) and H is 2.1, a large difference, so NaH is ionic (Na+H−), a saline hydride.
2. CaH2: Ca is a group 2 metal, so CaH2 is also ionic (Ca2+2H−), a saline hydride. This rules out (b) and (d).
3. NH3: N is a p-block non-metal, and the N–H bond is a shared pair, so NH3 is a covalent (electron-rich) hydride.
4. B2H6: B is a p-block element; the molecule has 3-centre 2-electron bridge bonds, so it is a covalent, electron-deficient hydride.

Concept Occlusion (taking up hydrogen into the interior of a solid) is a property of certain metals, not of covalent hydrides. The other three statements are standard facts about complex, polymeric and ionic hydrides.

(a) HCl   (b) NaH   (c) CaH2   (d) HI

🟢 ExactIn which of the foll…
(b), (c) b, c
Concept Hydrogen has oxidation state −1 only when it is bonded to an element less electronegative than itself. Compare the electronegativity of each partner with that of H (2.1).
Key relation Oxidation state of H = −1 if bonded to a more electropositive element Electronegativity: Na 0.9, Ca 1.0 < H 2.1 < Cl 3.0, I 2.5.
Solution
1. (a) HCl: Cl (3.0) is more electronegative than H (2.1), so H is +1 and Cl is −1. Not a match.
2. (b) NaH: Na (0.9) is far less electronegative, so Na is +1 and H is −1 (ionic Na+H−). Match.
3. (c) CaH2: Ca is +2 and the two H share −2, so H is −1. Match (Ca2+(H−)2).
4. (d) HI: I (2.5) is still more electronegative than H (2.1), so H is +1. Not a match.
5. Balance check for NaH: (+1) + (−1) = 0, and for CaH2: (+2) + 2(−1) = 0.
6. Remember that the same element H is +1 in HCl and HI but −1 in NaH and CaH2: the sign is fixed by the partner, not by the element. Hydrogen is never assigned −1 in compounds with non-metals such as HF, H2O or NH3. So hydrogen is in the negative state in NaH and CaH2, the saline hydrides of s-block metals.
⚡ JEE Trick Hydrogen is −1 only with metals of group 1, 2 (and a few others); with every halogen it is +1, even iodine. Check that the oxidation numbers in a formula add up to zero before you decide.
Answer: (b) and (c).

(a) Sodium is reduced   (b) Hydrogen is oxidised   (c) Hydrogen is reduced   (d) No element is oxidised or reduced

🟢 ExactIn the reaction of s…

Concept Write the equation and assign oxidation numbers on both sides. The hydrogen atoms in the product H2 come from two different sources, with different starting oxidation states.
Key relation NaH + H2O → NaOH + H2 This is a comproportionation: H(−1) and H(+1) both become H(0).
Solution
1. Oxidation numbers: in NaH, Na = +1 and H = −1. In H2O, H = +1 and O = −2.
2. In the products NaOH has Na = +1, O = −2 and H = +1, and H2 has H = 0.
3. (a) Na stays +1 before and after, so sodium is not reduced. (a) is wrong.
4. (b) The H of NaH goes −1 → 0, losing an electron, so hydrogen is oxidised. (b) is correct.
5. (c) The H of water goes +1 → 0, gaining an electron, so hydrogen is also reduced. (c) is correct.
6. (d) Since (b) and (c) occur, elements are both oxidised and reduced. (d) is wrong. Oxygen stays at −2 throughout.

Concept Ortho-H2 has nuclear spins parallel (triplet), para-H2 has them antiparallel (singlet). The ortho : para ratio depends on temperature, and each statement has to be tested against that.
Key relation Ortho : para ≈ 3 : 1 at room temperature, about 1 : 1 near 80 K, and pure para near 0 K Para is the lower-energy form, so cooling favours it.
Solution
1. (a) Para-H2 is the lower-energy (more stable) form, so it has the lower internal energy. (a) is correct.
2. (b) This is reversed. Ortho has parallel nuclear spins (triplet) and para has antiparallel spins (singlet). (b) is wrong.
3. (c) At absolute zero all molecules relax to the lowest-energy form, which is para, not ortho. (c) is wrong.

6. So the correct set is (a) and (d).

🟡 SimilarAnomalous Behaviour…
(a), (b) a, b
Concept Water's anomalous properties (high boiling point, ice floating on water) come from hydrogen bonding. Each statement concerns either the number of H-bonds, the density or the shape.
Key relation Each H2O: 2 H-bonds as donor + 2 as acceptor = 4 Ice is tetrahedral and open; water is bent (104.5°), not linear.
Solution
1. (a) Each O has two lone pairs (accepting two H-bonds) and each molecule has two O–H bonds (donating two), giving a maximum of 4 H-bonds per molecule. (a) is correct.
2. (b) In ice each molecule is tetrahedrally H-bonded in an open hexagonal cage with empty space, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats. (b) is correct.
3. (c) The density of water is maximum at 4 °C (277 K), not 0 °C. At 0 °C the open ice-like structure begins to form. (c) is wrong.

6. So the correct set is (a) and (b).
