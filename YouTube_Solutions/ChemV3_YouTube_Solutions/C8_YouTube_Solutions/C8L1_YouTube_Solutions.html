1 (i)Fill in the blank: The oxidation number of chromium in Cr2O72− is ________.Oxidation number of CrHow to find the Oxidation Number for Cr in the Cr2O7 2- ion. (Dichromate ion)🟢 Exact+6
Concept The oxidation number is the charge an atom would carry if every bond were treated as fully ionic. In any ion the oxidation numbers of all atoms add up to the net charge on the ion. Here oxygen is an ordinary oxide oxygen (no O–O peroxide bond), so each O takes its standard value of −2, and the two chromiums share the rest of the balance equally. Dichromate is one of the most common exam species, so this value is worth knowing on sight.
Key relation Σ(oxidation numbers) = charge on ion, i.e. 2·(Cr) + 7·(O) = −2 Dichromate carries a 2− charge, and both Cr atoms are equivalent by symmetry, so each carries the same value.
Solution
1. Fix oxygen at its usual value O = −2, since Cr2O72− has no peroxide linkage.
2. Let the oxidation number of each chromium be x; there are two Cr and seven O.
3. Write the charge balance: 2x + 7(−2) = −2.
4. Simplify: 2x − 14 = −2, so 2x = +12. Move the oxygen total to the right.
5. Divide: x = +12/2 = +6.
6. Both Cr atoms are symmetry-equivalent, so each chromium is +6.
7. Cross-check against chromate, CrO42−: Cr + 4(−2) = −2 also gives Cr = +6, confirming that chromium sits at its group maximum in both ions. Dichromate is just two chromate units sharing one oxygen.
 Cr2O7(2-) :  2x + 7(-2) = -2
              2x - 14    = -2
              2x         = +12
              x = +12/2  = +6      <-- each Cr
 check CrO4(2-): x + 4(-2) = -2 -> +6
⚡ JEE Trick Chromium sits in group 6, so its highest oxidation state is +6 — the value it shows in CrO42−, Cr2O72− and CrO3. If you ever compute more than +6 for chromium, you have miscounted the oxygens or missed a peroxide bond; re-check before moving on.
Answer: oxidation number of Cr in Cr2O72− = +6.
1 (ii)Fill in the blank: In Redox reaction both ________ and ________ process takes place simultaneously.Redox simultaneous processOxidation and Reduction always occur simultaneously🟡 Similaroxidation, reduction
Concept The word redox is itself a contraction of reduction–oxidation. Electrons are never created or destroyed, so an electron lost by one species must be gained by another at the same time. The two half-changes are therefore inseparable — one cannot happen without the other, and that is exactly what the blanks are testing. A reaction in which only one of them occurred would violate conservation of charge.
Key relation electrons lost in oxidation = electrons gained in reduction This electron book-keeping is what forces the two processes to occur simultaneously.
Solution
1. Oxidation is loss of electrons (or addition of oxygen / removal of hydrogen in the classical picture).
2. Reduction is gain of electrons (or removal of oxygen / addition of hydrogen).
3. The electrons released by the species being oxidised have to go somewhere.
4. They are taken up by the species being reduced, in the same reaction. No free electrons float about in solution.
5. Hence oxidation and reduction are coupled and happen at the same time.
6. Example: in Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu, Zn is oxidised while Cu2+ is reduced, together.
7. The species that is oxidised acts as the reducing agent, and the one that is reduced acts as the oxidising agent — so every redox reaction also pairs an oxidant with a reductant. Same coupling, viewed from the agents' side.
 Zn       -> Zn(2+) + 2e-     (oxidation)
 Cu(2+) + 2e- -> Cu           (reduction)
 ----------------------------------------
 both half-changes : one supplies, one takes
 Zn = reducing agent ; Cu(2+) = oxidising agent
⚡ JEE Trick Remember OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). The two blanks must be the two opposite processes — never two copies of the same word. If a problem shows only electrons being lost, look for where they are gained; they are always somewhere in the same equation.
Answer: in a redox reaction both oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously.
1 (iii)Fill in the blank: The ________ of oxygen and ________ of hydrogen is called as oxidation.Classical definition of oxidationOxidation = O2 Add, H2 Remove, e- Loss! I Class 11 Chemistry🟡 Similaraddition, removal
Concept Before the electronic theory, chemists defined oxidation and reduction in terms of two specific elements — oxygen and hydrogen. This is the classical (oxygen–hydrogen) definition, and it is exactly what this blank is testing. It survives today because the two phrasings always agree: adding the electronegative element or removing the electropositive one both raise the oxidation state.
Key relation oxidation = add O or remove H ; reduction = add H or remove O The two elements play opposite roles, so the sentence needs "addition" for oxygen and "removal" for hydrogen.
Solution
1. The sentence reads: the ____ of oxygen and ____ of hydrogen is called oxidation.
2. Classically, gaining oxygen is oxidation — e.g. 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO.
3. So the first blank (for oxygen) is addition.
4. Classically, losing hydrogen is also oxidation — e.g. H2S → S + H2 removes hydrogen. Both changes raise the oxidation state.
5. So the second blank (for hydrogen) is removal.
6. Both fillings agree with the electronic view: each raises the central atom's oxidation number.
7. Cross-check: in 2H2S + O2 → 2S + 2H2O, sulphur loses hydrogen and is oxidised from −2 to 0, matching "removal of hydrogen = oxidation". The classical and oxidation-number views coincide.
 oxidation (classical):
   + oxygen   e.g. C + O2 -> CO2
   - hydrogen e.g. 2H2S -> 2S + 2H2O (H lost)
 ----------------------------------------
 oxygen -> addition ;  hydrogen -> removal
⚡ JEE Trick Tie oxygen to "addition" and hydrogen to "removal" for oxidation; simply swap both words for reduction. The electronegative element (O) is added, the electropositive one (H) is taken away — a pattern that never fails in the classical scheme.
Answer: the addition of oxygen and removal of hydrogen is called oxidation.
1 (iv)Fill in the blank: The ________ of hydrogen and ________ of oxygen is called reduction.Classical definition of reductionOxidation = O2 Add, H2 Remove, e- Loss! I Class 11 Chemistry🟡 Similaraddition, removal
Concept Reduction is the mirror image of oxidation in the classical definition. Where oxidation adds oxygen or strips hydrogen, reduction does the opposite: it adds hydrogen or strips oxygen. This blank checks that you can reverse the two roles cleanly, because the examiner often pairs this sentence with the oxidation one to see if you swap the words correctly.
Key relation reduction = add H or remove O (opposite of oxidation) So the hydrogen blank takes "addition" and the oxygen blank takes "removal".
Solution
1. The sentence reads: the ____ of hydrogen and ____ of oxygen is called reduction.
2. Classically, gaining hydrogen is reduction — e.g. Cl2 + H2 → 2HCl adds hydrogen to chlorine.
3. So the first blank (for hydrogen) is addition.
4. Classically, losing oxygen is reduction — e.g. CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O removes oxygen from copper. Both changes lower the oxidation state.
5. So the second blank (for oxygen) is removal.
6. This is exactly the reverse of the oxidation statement, as it must be.
7. Cross-check: in CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, copper loses oxygen and drops from +2 to 0 — reduction by removal of oxygen, confirming the blanks. Hydrogen here is the reducing agent.
 reduction (classical):
   + hydrogen e.g. Cl2 + H2 -> 2HCl
   - oxygen   e.g. CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O
 ----------------------------------------
 hydrogen -> addition ;  oxygen -> removal
⚡ JEE Trick Just flip the oxidation rule: for reduction, hydrogen is added and oxygen is removed. The electropositive element (H) comes in, the electronegative one (O) goes out — so if you memorise one sentence, the other is simply its opposite.
Answer: the addition of hydrogen and removal of oxygen is called reduction.
1 (v)Fill in the blank: The oxidation number of oxygen in OF2 is ________.Oxidation number of O in OF2How to find the Oxidation Number for O in OF2🟢 Exact+2
Concept Oxygen is usually −2, but that rule quietly assumes oxygen is the most electronegative atom in the molecule. Fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen — it is the most electronegative element of all — so in OF2 the bonding electrons are assigned to fluorine and oxygen is forced into a positive oxidation state. This is the single famous exception where oxygen goes positive, and examiners love to test it.
Key relation Σ(oxidation numbers) = 0 for a neutral molecule, with F = −1 Fluorine is always −1 in its compounds because nothing is more electronegative.
Solution
1. OF2 is a neutral molecule, so all oxidation numbers sum to 0.
2. Fluorine, the most electronegative element, is −1 in every compound; there are two F atoms.
3. Let oxygen be x. Then x + 2(−1) = 0.
4. So x − 2 = 0, giving x = +2. Oxygen is positive because F outranks it.
5. Contrast H2O, where oxygen outranks hydrogen and so is −2.
6. Hence in OF2 the oxidation number of oxygen is +2.
7. Compare O2F2: 2x + 2(−1) = 0 gives x = +1 per oxygen, because there is now an O–O bond as well. The number of fluorines per oxygen sets the value.
 OF2 (neutral) :  x + 2(-1) = 0
                  x - 2     = 0
                  x         = +2   <-- oxygen
 (F = -1 always ; F beats O in E.N.)
 compare O2F2 : 2x - 2 = 0 -> x = +1
⚡ JEE Trick Oxygen is +2 only in OF2 and +1 in O2F2. Whenever fluorine appears, give F its −1 first and let the other atom absorb the balance — do not reflexively write O = −2. Fluorine is the one element with no positive oxidation state at all.
Answer: oxidation number of oxygen in OF2 = +2.

Concept Carbon suboxide, O=C=C=C=O, is a linear molecule in which the three carbons are not all alike, so a single average value hides the truth. The clean way to get each carbon's oxidation number is the bond-counting method: count every bond to the more-electronegative oxygen as +1 to that carbon, and every bond between two identical carbon atoms as 0.
Key relation per bond: to O gives +1 to C, C–C gives 0 ; check Σ = 0 Only the C–O bonds shift a carbon's oxidation number.
Solution
1. Structure O=C(x)=C(Y)=C(Z)=O; take each O = −2, molecule neutral.
2. Terminal carbon x is double-bonded to one O (two C–O bonds) and double-bonded to the central C.
3. The two bonds to O give +2; the C=C bond gives 0. So x = +2.
4. By the same count, terminal carbon Z = +2. The molecule is symmetric about the centre.
5. Central carbon Y is double-bonded to a carbon on each side — all C–C bonds, none to O — so Y = 0.
6. Check the oxygens too: each O is double-bonded only to carbon, so each O = −2, two oxygens give −4.
7. Overall balance: 2(+2) + 0 + 2(−2) = +4 − 4 = 0, matching the neutral molecule, so x = +2, Y = 0, Z = +2. A zero sum confirms the per-atom assignments.
 O=C=C=C=O
     x  Y  Z

 Z : same as x               -> +2
 Y : only C=C bonds          ->  0
 O : each double-bonded to C -> -2
 sum: 2(+2)+0 + 2(-2) = 0  OK
⚡ JEE Trick The usual "average" formula gives +4/3 per carbon — a fraction that hides the real picture. Count bonds instead: the atoms touching oxygen take the positive charge, while the buried middle carbon, bonded only to its neighbours, stays exactly 0.
Answer: x = +2, Y = 0, Z = +2.

2The oxidation number of oxygen in peroxides is: (Level-2)

Concept Oxygen is −2 in most compounds, but a peroxide contains an oxygen–oxygen single bond (O–O), as in hydrogen peroxide H2O2 and sodium peroxide Na2O2. Because the two oxygens share that bond equally, that bond contributes nothing to either atom's oxidation number, and each oxygen ends up at only −1, not −2. This is one of the standard exceptions to the oxygen rule.
Key relation peroxide ion O22−: 2(O) = −2 ⇒ each O = −1 The like–like O–O bond contributes 0 to each atom.
Solution
1. A peroxide carries the O22− unit, e.g. H2O2 or Na2O2.
2. The total charge on the two bonded oxygens is −2.
3. The two oxygens are equivalent, so each takes half: −2/2 = −1.
4. Verify with H2O2: 2(+1) + 2(O) = 0 ⇒ 2O = −2 ⇒ O = −1. Matches the ion result.
5. So peroxide oxygen is −1, which is option (b).
6. Reject the distractors: −2 is ordinary oxide, +2 occurs only in OF2, and 0 is free elemental O2.
7. Cross-check with Na2O2: 2(+1) + 2(O) = 0 again gives O = −1, so the value holds whatever the counter-ion. The −1 comes from the O–O bond, not the metal.
 H2O2 :  2(+1) + 2(O) = 0
         2(O) = -2
         O    = -1        <-- peroxide
 Na2O2:  2(+1) + 2(O) = 0 -> O = -1
 (O-O bond -> each O is -1, not -2)
⚡ JEE Trick Three oxygen exceptions worth memorising: peroxide O = −1, superoxide O = −1/2 (as in KO2), and OF2 O = +2. If you ever see an O–O single bond in the structure, drop the usual −2 down to −1 for those two atoms.
Answer: (b) −1.

3. Charge contributed by Y = n(−b) = −bn; by Z = r(−c) = −cr.
4. Neutrality demands the sum be zero: am + (−bn) + (−cr) = 0 → am − bn − cr = 0.
5. Move the negatives to the other side: am = bn + cr, i.e. bn + cr = am. The single positive term must balance the two negative terms.
6. Sanity-check with a real salt: Al2(SO4)3 has Al +3 (m = 2), S +6, O −2; the one positive sum 2(3) + ... still equals the negative total, exactly the pattern am = bn + cr.
7. That is option (d); option (a) wrongly adds all three contributions as positives, while (b) and (c) each drop one of the two negative terms.

 am - bn - cr = 0

 am = bn + cr              <-- (d)
⚡ JEE Trick Total positive charge always equals total negative charge in a neutral compound. Reading “+a from m X atoms” = “b from n Y + c from r Z” gives am = bn + cr instantly, with no sign juggling.

4The sum of oxidation states of all carbon atoms in toluene molecule is
(a) −1   (b) −7/8   (c) −8/7   (d) −8		Sum of carbon oxidation states in tolueneThe sum of oxidation states of all carbon atoms in toluene molecule...🟢 Exact(d) −8
Concept The question asks for the sum of the oxidation states of every carbon atom, not the average per carbon. Treat the molecule as one neutral unit: the carbons and hydrogens together must total zero, and each hydrogen is +1, so the carbon sum follows at once. The fractional options are the per-atom-average traps.
Key relation Σ(C oxidation states) + Σ(H oxidation states) = 0, with each H = +1 Only C and H are present in toluene C7H8, and the molecule is neutral.
Solution
1. Toluene is C6H5CH3, molecular formula C7H8.
2. Each hydrogen bonds to carbon (less electronegative), so every H is +1; eight of them give +8.
3. The molecule is neutral, so Σ(C) + (+8) = 0 → Σ(C) = −8.
4. This −8 is the sum over all seven carbons taken together. Individual carbons differ (the CH3 carbon is −3, ring carbons vary), but their total is fixed by the formula.
5. The average per carbon would be −8/7 ≈ −1.14, which is option (c) — a distractor for those who divide; option (b) −7/8 inverts the ratio, and option (a) −1 treats the whole molecule as if it held a single carbon.
6. As a bond-by-bond check: the methyl carbon (3 C–H) is −3, the ipso ring carbon (no H, only C–C) is 0, and the five C–H ring carbons are −1 each (−5 total). Their sum is −3 + 0 + (−5) = −8, agreeing with the formula method.
7. The question wants the sum, so the answer is −8, option (d).
 toluene = C7 H8   (neutral)
 sum(C) + 8(+1) = 0
 sum(C) + 8 = 0
 ------------------------------
 sum(C) = -8              <-- answer
 (average = -8/7, a trap)
⚡ JEE Trick For any hydrocarbon CxHy, the sum of all carbon oxidation states is simply −y (minus the number of hydrogens), since each H is +1. Here y = 8, so the sum is −8 without drawing a single bond.
Answer: (d) −8.
5The oxidation states of N in aniline and nitrobenzene are, respectively,
(a) −3, +3   (b) −1, +5   (c) −3, +5   (d) −3, +1		N oxidation states in aniline/nitrobenzeneThe oxidation states of N in aniline and nitrobenzene are, respectively,🟢 Exact(a) −3, +3
Concept Oxidation number is counted by assigning each bonding pair to the more electronegative atom, then comparing the electrons kept with the free-atom count (5 for nitrogen). The electronegativity order O > N > C > H decides every assignment; whether nitrogen keeps a lone pair is what separates the amine from the nitro group.
Key relation oxidation number = (valence e⁻ of free atom) − (e⁻ assigned to it in the molecule) N keeps a lone pair in aniline but none in the nitro group, where both bonds to O give electrons away.
Solution
1. Aniline C6H5NH2: nitrogen bonds to two H and one C, all less electronegative, so N keeps all those bonding electrons (6) plus its lone pair (2) = 8 assigned.
2. Oxidation number of N = 5 − 8 = −3. Same as in NH3, since the environment is three bonds to less-electronegative atoms.
3. Nitrobenzene C6H5NO2: the –NO2 nitrogen forms four bonds (one to C, three bond-pairs shared with two O) and has no lone pair.
4. Oxygen is more electronegative, so N keeps nothing from the O bonds; only the N–C bond gives N its 2 electrons.
5. Oxidation number of N = 5 − 2 = +3. Same value as N in nitrite NO2− and HNO2.
6. So aniline gives −3 and nitrobenzene gives +3, which is option (a).
 aniline  N: keeps 2H+1C bonds + lone pair
   ON = 5 - 8 = -3
 nitro    N: no lone pair, only C-N kept
   ON = 5 - 2 = +3
 ------------------------------
 (-3, +3)                  <-- (a)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember nitrite/nitro nitrogen = +3 and amine nitrogen = −3. The +5 distractors assume N gives away its lone pair too, but in the nitro group there is simply no lone pair to start from — counting it as +5 double-counts.
Answer: (a) −3, +3.

Concept This is a definitions check on what an oxidation number means in ionic versus covalent compounds, and whether it must be an integer. One or more options may be correct, so each statement is judged on its own merits against the standard definition.
Key relation oxidation number = the charge an atom would carry if every shared pair were given fully to the more electronegative partner This single definition covers both ionic (real electron transfer) and covalent (hypothetical transfer) cases.
Solution
1. (a) In ionic compounds electrons are genuinely transferred, so the oxidation number is the number of electrons an atom actually loses (+) or gains (−). This is correct.
2. (b) In a covalent compound no full transfer occurs, so the oxidation number is defined as the charge the atom would have acquired if the bonds were fully ionic. This is the standard textbook wording — correct.
3. (c) Oxidation numbers can be fractional when a formula averages over inequivalent atoms: S in Na2S4O6 is +2.5, Fe in Fe3O4 is +8/3, C in C3O2 averages non-integer. So fractional values do occur — correct.
4. (d) “None of these” contradicts (a)–(c), each shown valid, so (d) is false.
5. Therefore the correct statements are (a), (b) and (c).
 (a) ionic : electrons lost/gained   TRUE
 (b) covalent: hypothetical charge   TRUE
 (c) fractional ON possible          TRUE
     e.g. S in Na2S4O6 = +2.5
 (d) none of these                   FALSE
 ------------------------------
 correct = (a), (b), (c)   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick The usual mistake is rejecting (c) on the belief that oxidation numbers are always integers. Any mixed-valence or averaged formula (Fe3O4, Na2S4O6) breaks that myth, so keep (c).
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).
7Which of the following is (are) redox reaction(s)?
(a) Cu + S → CuS   (b) Cu2+ + S2− → CuS   (c) Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu   (d) (1/2)Cl2 + Br−(aq) → Cl−(aq) + (1/2)Br2		Identify redox reactionsWhich of the following is a redox reaction?🟡 Similar(a), (c), (d) three are redox; (b) is not
Concept A reaction is redox only if at least one element changes its oxidation number between reactants and products. We track each element across every reaction; combinations that merely assemble pre-formed ions (no change) are not redox. One or more options may be correct.
Key relation redox ⇔ Δ(oxidation number) ≠ 0 for some element Pairing an existing cation with an existing anion keeps every oxidation number fixed, so it is non-redox.
Solution
1. (a) Cu + S → CuS: Cu goes 0 → +2 (oxidised) and S goes 0 → −2 (reduced). Numbers change, so this is redox.
2. (b) Cu2+ + S2− → CuS: copper stays +2 and sulphur stays −2 on both sides; it is just precipitation/combination, not redox.
3. (c) Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu: Zn goes 0 → +2 and Cu2+ goes +2 → 0 (displacement). Numbers change, so this is redox.
4. (d) (1/2)Cl2 + Br− → Cl− + (1/2)Br2: Cl goes 0 → −1 and Br goes −1 → 0. Numbers change, so this is redox.
5. Only (b) shows no change in any oxidation number — both ions arrive pre-charged and simply pair up into a precipitate, so no electrons move.
6. Hence the redox reactions are (a), (c) and (d), and (b) is merely an ionic combination.
 (a) Cu 0->+2, S 0->-2        redox
 (b) Cu2+ stays +2, S2- -2    NOT
 (c) Zn 0->+2, Cu +2->0       redox
 (d) Cl 0->-1, Br -1->0       redox
 ------------------------------
 redox = (a), (c), (d)     <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick If the reactants already carry their final charges as free ions (like Cu2+ + S2−), the step is bond-forming, not electron-transfer, so it cannot be redox. Displacement and element + element combinations almost always are redox.
Answer: (a), (c) and (d).
8Which of the following reaction(s) is (are) not oxidation reduction?
(a) H+ + OH− → H2O   (b) (1/2)H2 + (1/2)Cl2 → HCl   (c) CaCO3 → CaO + CO2   (d) 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2		Identify non-redox reactionsWhich one of the following is not a redox reaction :- (1) CaCO3->CaO+CO2🟡 Similar(a), (c) (a) and (c) are not redox
Concept Here we want the reactions that are not oxidation–reduction, i.e. those where no element changes oxidation number. Neutralisation and thermal decomposition into same-state fragments are the classic non-redox examples; combination of elements and peroxide breakdown are redox. One or more options may be correct.
Key relation non-redox ⇔ Δ(oxidation number) = 0 for every element Disproportionation (one element going both up and down) still counts as redox.
Solution
1. (a) H+ + OH− → H2O: H stays +1 and O stays −2; pure neutralisation, so not redox.
2. (b) (1/2)H2 + (1/2)Cl2 → HCl: H goes 0 → +1 and Cl goes 0 → −1; numbers change, so this is redox (excluded).
3. (c) CaCO3 → CaO + CO2: Ca stays +2, C stays +4, O stays −2 throughout; thermal decomposition with no change, so not redox.
4. (d) 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2: peroxide O is −1 and splits into −2 (water) and 0 (O2). Oxygen disproportionates, so this is redox (excluded).
5. Only (a) and (c) leave every oxidation number untouched; both are acid–base / decomposition processes rather than electron transfers.
6. Reactions (b) and (d) do change oxidation numbers, so they are redox and are excluded; hence the non-redox reactions are (a) and (c).

 (b) H 0->+1, Cl 0->-1        redox
 (c) Ca +2, C +4, O -2 same   NOT redox
 (d) O -1 -> -2 and 0         redox (disprop.)
 ------------------------------
 not redox = (a), (c)      <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Peroxide decomposition always traps students into calling it non-redox because the formula “looks” like rearrangement; check oxygen: −1 splitting to −2 and 0 is textbook disproportionation, firmly redox.
Answer: (a) and (c).

10Which of the following statement(s) is (are) correct?
(a) Oxidation of a substance is followed by reduction of another   (b) Reduction of a substance is followed by oxidation of another   (c) Oxidation and reduction are complementary reactions   (d) It is not necessary that both oxidation and reduction should take place in the same reaction		Correct statements about redoxOxidation and Reduction Reactions - Basic Introduction🟡 Similar(a), (b), (c) redox halves are coupled
Concept The central idea of redox is that electrons are conserved: whatever one species loses, another must gain in the same reaction. We test each statement against this coupling principle. The “may be correct” wording means we evaluate each option independently.
Key relation electrons lost in oxidation = electrons gained in reduction, in the same reaction An isolated half-reaction cannot run alone; oxidation and reduction are two faces of one electron transfer.
Solution
1. (a) “Oxidation of a substance is followed by reduction of another”: the electrons released must be accepted by some species, so an oxidation is always accompanied by a reduction — correct.
2. (b) “Reduction of a substance is followed by oxidation of another”: the electrons gained had to come from somewhere, so a reduction requires a matching oxidation — correct.
3. (c) “Oxidation and reduction are complementary reactions”: they are the two halves of one electron-transfer process and occur together — correct.
4. (d) “It is not necessary that both occur in the same reaction”: this denies the coupling and is false; in any redox change both must occur simultaneously.
5. Statements (a), (b) and (c) all restate electron conservation from different angles; (d) contradicts it.
6. Hence the correct statements are (a), (b) and (c).
 electrons lost = electrons gained
 (a) oxidation implies a reduction   TRUE
 (b) reduction implies an oxidation   TRUE
 (c) they are complementary halves    TRUE
 (d) can occur separately             FALSE
 ------------------------------
 correct = (a), (b), (c)   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick Picture a balance sheet: electrons have no “account” of their own, so every electron leaving an oxidised atom must land on a reduced one. That conservation makes (d) impossible and (a)–(c) automatic.
Answer: (a), (b) and (c).
