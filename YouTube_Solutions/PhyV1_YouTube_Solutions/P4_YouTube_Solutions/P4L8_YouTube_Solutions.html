Which formula gives range (R) in terms of u and θ? (a) R=u²cos²θ/g (b) R=2ucosθ/g (c) R=u²sin(2θ)/g (d) R=u sinθ/g
P4L8VSuggQ1Range formula R = u²sin2θ/g
🟢 ExactRange Formula DERIVE…
cR = u² sin(2θ) / g
Concept The range R is the horizontal distance a ground-launched projectile covers before returning to launch height. Horizontal motion is force-free, so the horizontal speed u cosθ stays constant; the projectile is airborne for the time of flight T fixed entirely by the vertical motion. Range is (constant horizontal speed) × (flight time), so the correct option must be (i) dimensionally a length and (ii) maximal at θ = 45°.
Formula R = ux·T = (u cosθ)(2u sinθ/g) with T = 2u sinθ/g. The identity 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ folds the two factors into one clean expression.
Solution
1. Constant horizontal speed: ux = u cosθ (m s⁻¹).
2. Flight time from the vertical axis: T = 2u sinθ ÷ g (s).The body climbs for u sinθ/g and falls for the same time back to y = 0.
3. Range = speed × time: R = (u cosθ)(2u sinθ/g) = 2u² sinθ cosθ / g.
4. Apply 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ: R = u² sin2θ / g — option (c).
5. Check the maximum: sin2θ = 1 at θ = 45°, giving Rmax = u²/g, the standard result.
6. Check units: (m s⁻¹)² ÷ (m s⁻²) = m ✓; options (b) and (d) reduce to seconds, so they are not lengths at all.
⚡ JEE Trick Two filters flatten three options instantly: units (a range is metres, so it must be u² over g) and the 45° maximum (only sin2θ peaks there). Trap: (a) u²cos²θ/g is also a length, but it is largest at θ = 0° — a flat launch with zero range — so it cannot be R.
Answer: (c) R = u² sin2θ / g.

A ball is thrown with initial velocity 100 m/s at 30° above the horizontal. How far from the throwing point will the ball hit? (g=10 m/s²)
P4L8VSuggQ2Horizontal range (numeric)
🟢 ExactA baseball is thrown…
500√3 m ⚠
Concept "How far from the throwing point will the ball hit" — for a ball thrown from ground level over level ground, the landing point is the horizontal range R. Projectile motion splits cleanly into two independent 1-D motions: horizontally there is no force, so ux stays constant; vertically gravity acts with constant a = −g. The ball is in the air only as long as the vertical motion allows, so R = (constant horizontal speed) × (time of flight), and that product collapses to the single range formula.
Assumptions Ground is level (launch height = landing height = 0 m), air resistance is neglected, and g = 10 m s⁻² as given. The book writes "g = 10 m/s" — a misprint for m s⁻²; the value 10 is what is intended.
Formula R = u² sin2θ / g — R in m, u = launch speed in m s⁻¹, θ = launch angle (degrees), g = 9.8 or 10 m s⁻². Units: (m s⁻¹)² ÷ (m s⁻²) = m² s⁻² × s² m⁻¹ = m ✓ — dimensionally a length, as a range must be. Built from T = 2u sinθ / g (s) and R = ux·T = (u cosθ)·T (m).
Solution
1. List the data with units: u = 100 m s⁻¹, θ = 30°, g = 10 m s⁻².
2. Resolve the launch velocity into components:ux = u cos30° = 100 × 0.8660 = 86.60 m s⁻¹ (constant); uy = u sin30° = 100 × 0.500 = 50.0 m s⁻¹ (decelerated by g).
3. Time of flight from the vertical motion: T = 2uy ÷ g = (2 × 50.0 m s⁻¹) ÷ (10 m s⁻²) = 100 ÷ 10 = 10.0 s.Up-and-down are symmetric: it takes uy/g = 5.0 s to rise and another 5.0 s to fall back to launch height. Units: (m s⁻¹)/(m s⁻²) = s ✓.
4. Horizontal distance = constant speed × time: R = ux × T = 86.60 m s⁻¹ × 10.0 s = 866 m.No horizontal force ⇒ uniform velocity ⇒ plain distance = speed × time.
5. Cross-check with the one-line range formula: R = u² sin2θ ÷ g = (100)² × sin60° ÷ 10 = 10000 × 0.8660 ÷ 10 = 866 m ✓ — same answer, so the two routes agree.
6. Exact surd form: R = 10000 × (√3/2) ÷ 10 = 500√3 m ≈ 866 m.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise u²/g as the range "unit": here u²/g = 10000/10 = 1000 m, so R = 1000 × sin2θ. Then any angle is one sine away — 30° ⇒ sin60° = 0.866 ⇒ 866 m; 45° ⇒ sin90° = 1 ⇒ the maximum 1000 m. Trap: students double the answer instead of the angle — the formula needs sin 2θ = sin 60°, not 2 sin 30° (= 1.0, which would wrongly give 1000 m).
Answer: R = 500√3 m ≈ 866 m from the throwing point (with T = 10.0 s).

🟢 ExactA body is projected…
(i) 45 m · (ii) 6.0 s · (iii) 180√3 m
Concept One launch, three standard outputs, all from the same component split. Resolve u into ux = u cosθ (never changes — no horizontal force) and uy = u sinθ (uniformly slowed by g). Max height is how far the vertical part rises before uy is spent; time of flight is twice the time to the top; range is ux times that full flight time.
Formula H = uy²/2g (m); T = 2uy/g (s); R = ux·T = u² sin2θ/g (m). All three come from v = u − gt and v² = u² − 2gs on the vertical axis, plus uniform horizontal motion. Data: u = 60 m s⁻¹, θ = 30°, g = 10 m s⁻².
Solution
Given projected with 60 m/s at 30° to horizontal; g = 10 m/s²
Find (i) maximum height (ii) time of flight (iii) horizontal range
1. Components: uy = 60 × sin30° = 60 × 0.500 = 30.0 m s⁻¹; ux = 60 × cos30° = 60 × 0.8660 = 51.96 m s⁻¹.
2. (i) Max height: H = uy²/2g = (30.0)² ÷ (2 × 10) = 900 ÷ 20 = 45 m.v² = uy² − 2gH with v = 0 at the apex.
3. (ii) Time of flight: T = 2uy/g = (2 × 30.0) ÷ 10 = 60 ÷ 10 = 6.0 s.3.0 s up, 3.0 s down — symmetric over level ground.
4. (iii) Range: R = ux·T = 51.96 × 6.0 = 311.8 m.
5. Write it exactly: R = u² sin2θ/g = 3600 × sin60° ÷ 10 = 3600 × (√3/2) ÷ 10 = 180√3 m ≈ 311.8 m ✓ — the two routes agree.
⚡ JEE Trick At θ = 30° everything is friendly: uy = u/2 exactly, so H = u²/8g, T = u/g and R = (√3/2)u²/g — no calculator needed. Cross-tie: R = 4H/tanθ = 4 × 45 ÷ 0.5774 = 311.8 m ✓. Trap: "max height" uses only uy; students who plug the full u = 60 into H = u²/2g get 180 m, four times too big.
Answer: (i) H = 45 m · (ii) T = 6.0 s · (iii) R = 180√3 ≈ 311.8 m.

Particle projected at an angle to horizontal has range R, time of flight t₁. Same projectile at same speed, another angle, same range: time of flight t₂. Show t₁t₂ = 2R/g
P4L8VSuggQ4Complementary-angle projectiles
🟢 ExactA projectile is thro…
—
Concept Two launches at the same speed hit the same range only when their angles are complementary — θ and 90°−θ — because R ∝ sin2θ and sin2(90°−θ) = sin2θ. Their times of flight differ (one carries sinθ, the other cosθ), and this problem asks you to prove their product equals 2R/g — a compact identity that ties the two flights to their shared range.
Formula t₁ = 2u sinθ/g, t₂ = 2u sin(90°−θ)/g = 2u cosθ/g (s); R = u² sin2θ/g (m). t₂ uses sin(90°−θ) = cosθ; the shared range guarantees the two flights are the complementary pair.
Solution
1. First flight time: t₁ = 2u sinθ ÷ g.
2. Same speed, angle 90°−θ (the only other angle giving the same R): t₂ = 2u cosθ ÷ g.
3. Multiply: t₁t₂ = (2u sinθ/g)(2u cosθ/g) = 4u² sinθ cosθ / g².
4. Use 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ: t₁t₂ = 2u² sin2θ / g².Just the double-angle identity, nothing more.
5. From R = u² sin2θ/g we get u² sin2θ = Rg; substitute: t₁t₂ = 2(Rg)/g² = 2R/g. ∎
6. Numerical check (u = 20, θ = 30°, g = 10): t₁ = 2.0 s, t₂ = 3.46 s, product = 6.93; and 2R/g = 2 × 34.6 ÷ 10 = 6.93 ✓.
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the trio for complementary launches: same R, t₂/t₁ = cotθ, and t₁t₂ = 2R/g. The last one is dimension-safe (s² = m ÷ (m s⁻²) ✓) and lets you get one flight time from the other plus the range without touching θ. Trap: it is not t₁t₂ = R/g or 2R/g² — carry the factor 2 and the single power of g.
Answer: proven — t₁t₂ = 2R/g (identity; no numerical key for this derivation).

Stone projected with velocity a at angle θ reaches max height H₁. Projected at angle (π/2−θ), reaches H₂. Relation between range R, H₁, H₂?
P4L8VSuggQ5Complementary angles: R vs H₁, H₂
🟢 ExactA stone projected wi…
aR = 4√(H₁H₂)
Concept Same speed a at complementary angles θ and (π/2−θ) gives the same range but different heights: the steep launch reaches H₁, the shallow one H₂. Because H uses sin²(angle), the two heights carry sin²θ and cos²θ, whose product folds neatly back into the range. The question wants R written using only H₁ and H₂.
Formula H = a² sin²θ / 2g (m); R = a² sin2θ / g (m). For the partner angle sin(π/2−θ) = cosθ, so its height carries cos²θ.
Solution
1. Height at angle θ: H₁ = a² sin²θ ÷ 2g.
2. Height at angle (π/2−θ): H₂ = a² sin²(π/2−θ) ÷ 2g = a² cos²θ ÷ 2g.
3. Multiply: H₁H₂ = a⁴ sin²θ cos²θ ÷ 4g².
4. Take the square root: √(H₁H₂) = a² sinθ cosθ ÷ 2g.All squared factors come out cleanly; sinθ, cosθ, a and g are positive.
5. Recognise 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ, so a² sinθ cosθ ÷ 2g = a² sin2θ ÷ 4g = R/4.
6. Hence √(H₁H₂) = R/4, i.e. R = 4√(H₁H₂) — option (a).
⚡ JEE Trick Complementary heights combine as a geometric mean: R = 4√(H₁H₂), while their sum gives H₁ + H₂ = a²/2g (independent of θ). Trap: it is 4√(H₁H₂), not 2√(H₁H₂) — the range formula has 2 sinθ cosθ, and the extra 2 from a²/2g vs a²/g pushes the coefficient to 4.
Answer: (a) R = 4√(H₁H₂).

🟡 SimilarEx-73: body projecte…
141.4 m, 20.41 m, 2.041 s ⚠
Concept One launch, three standard outputs — all three fall out of the same component split. Resolve u into ux (never changes, no horizontal force) and uy (uniformly decelerated at g). Then: time to the top is when uy is killed off by g; max height is how far the body rises in that time; range is ux multiplied by the full flight time, which by up–down symmetry is twice the time to the top. Given thrown with 40 m/s at 30° to horizontal
Find (i) horizontal range (ii) maximum height (iii) time to reach maximum height

If speed is the same but angle changes from θ to 90°−θ, what happens to time of flight?
P4L8V1Q3Complementary angles — time of flight
🟡 SimilarTwo bodies projected…
cRemains the same ⚠
Concept This is the heart of the whole lesson: for complementary angles θ and 90°−θ at the same speed, the range is identical but the time of flight is not. The reason is which trig function each depends on. Range carries sin2θ, and sin2(90°−θ) = sin(180°−2θ) = sin2θ — unchanged. Time of flight carries sinθ, and sin(90°−θ) = cosθ — a different number unless θ = 45°. So the steep partner stays up longer while covering the same ground.
Formula T = 2u sinθ / g (s) with u in m s⁻¹, g in m s⁻². For the partner angle: T′ = 2u sin(90°−θ)/g = 2u cosθ / g (s). Units: (m s⁻¹)/(m s⁻²) = s ✓ for both. Dividing gives T′/T = cosθ / sinθ = cot θ — a pure number, as a ratio must be.
Assumptions Same launch speed u, same g, level ground, no air resistance — only the angle is changed.
Solution
1. Original flight time: T = 2u sinθ ÷ g (s).From v = u − gt on the vertical axis: the body returns to launch height after 2u sinθ/g seconds.
2. New angle 90°−θ, same speed u ⇒ T′ = 2u sin(90°−θ) ÷ g = 2u cosθ ÷ g (s).The identity sin(90°−θ) = cosθ is the only step needed.
3. Take the ratio to remove u and g: T′ ÷ T = cosθ ÷ sinθ = cot θ.Both u (m s⁻¹) and g (m s⁻²) cancel, leaving a dimensionless number ✓.
4. Read off the three cases from cot θ:
  • θ < 45° ⇒ cot θ > 1 ⇒ T′ > T ⇒ the time of flight increases.
  • θ > 45° ⇒ cot θ < 1 ⇒ T′ < T ⇒ the time of flight decreases.
  • θ = 45° ⇒ cot 45° = 1 ⇒ T′ = T ⇒ unchanged (the angle is its own complement).
5. Numerical check with u = 20 m s⁻¹, g = 10 m s⁻², θ = 30° → 60°:T = 2(20)(0.500)/10 = 2.0 s; T′ = 2(20)(0.866)/10 = 3.46 s. Ratio 3.46/2.0 = 1.73 = cot30° ✓ — it went up because 30° < 45°. Meanwhile R = 400(0.866)/10 = 34.6 m for both.
6. So the one thing that is certainly false is "remains the same" (except at exactly 45°); the direction of the change is fixed by whether θ is below or above 45°.
⚡ JEE Trick Lock in the one-liner: complementary angles ⇒ same range, T′/T = cot θ, and T₁·T₂ = 2R/g. That last identity is Suggested Problem 4 of this very lesson and instantly proves the times differ while R is shared. Trap: option (c) "remains the same" is bait — it is the range that is preserved under θ → 90°−θ, never the time of flight (unless θ is exactly 45°).
Answer: the time of flight changes — it scales by cot θ, so it increases for θ < 45° (option b) and decreases for θ > 45° (option a). ⚠ The printed options are indeterminate: with no value or range given for θ, neither (a) nor (b) can be singled out; only (c) "remains the same" and (d) "becomes zero" are definitely wrong. If the book intends the usual θ < 45° launch, the answer is (b) Increases.

To hit a target, the shooter should point his gun:

🟢 ExactAssertion: to hit a…
cslightly higher than the target
Concept A bullet is a projectile: the instant it leaves the barrel gravity begins to pull it down, so during its flight it drops below the straight line of the barrel. If the shooter aimed exactly at the target, the bullet would strike below it. To compensate for that fall, the barrel must point slightly higher than the target so the drop over the flight time brings the bullet down onto it.
Formula Vertical drop below the line of sight: Δy = (1/2) g t² (m), with t = (horizontal distance)/(u cosθ) the time of flight (s). Horizontal aim sets t; gravity produces the drop Δy that the raised barrel must cancel.
Solution
1. Treat the bullet as launched with a large horizontal speed toward the target.
2. Time to reach the target: t = distance ÷ (u cosθ) (s) — small but non-zero.
3. In that time the bullet falls Δy = (1/2) g t² below the barrel's straight line.Independent of the bullet's horizontal speed, the fall is purely gravitational.
4. Aiming straight at the target ⇒ impact is Δy below it — a miss.
5. So raise the muzzle so the line of fire passes Δy above the target; gravity then curves the bullet down onto it — slightly higher than the target, option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Any projectile falls below its launch line by (1/2)gt²; the faster the bullet, the smaller t and the tinier the needed lift — but it is never zero. Trap: horizontal speed does not reduce the drop distance at a fixed range, only the flight time; over the same range the fall depends on t = range/(u cosθ), so a slow bullet needs a much higher aim.
Answer: (c) slightly higher than the target.
