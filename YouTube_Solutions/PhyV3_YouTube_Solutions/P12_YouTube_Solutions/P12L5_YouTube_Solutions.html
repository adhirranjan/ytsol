1Which of the following statements best describes the moment of inertia of a body? (a) It measures how fast a body rotates about an axis (b) It represents the resistance of a body to change in its rotational motion (c) It depends only on the total mass of the body (d) It is independent of the axis of rotation — (Level-1)Meaning of moment of inertiaSystems of Particles & Rotational Motion 01 | Moment of inertia | Parallel, perpendicular axis | JEE🟡 SimilarbIt represents the resistance of a body to change in its rotational motion
Concept Moment of inertia is the rotational twin of mass: mass says how hard it is to change a body's velocity, I says how hard it is to change its angular velocity. But mass belongs to the body alone, while I belongs to the body and a chosen axis, because it weights every particle by the square of its distance from that axis.
Formula I = Σmiri², and τ = Iα. The second equation is the one that gives I its physical meaning: for a fixed torque, a bigger I buys a smaller α, exactly as a bigger m buys a smaller a for a fixed force.
Solution
1. Test (a). Angular speed ω is the quantity that measures "how fast a body rotates"; I never appears in the definition ω = dθ/dt. A flywheel at rest still has its full moment of inertia, so (a) is wrong.
2. Test (b). Compare τ = Iα with F = ma. I sits where m sits, so I is the rotational inertia — the opposition a body offers to any change in its rotational state. This is the definition.
3. Test (c). For a ring and a disc of the same mass M and radius R about the central axis, Iring = MR² but Idisc = MR²/2 — a factor of 2 apart on identical mass. So I depends on how the mass is distributed, not only on how much there is.
4. Test (d). The same solid sphere gives I = 2MR²/5 about a diameter but I = 2MR²/5 + MR² = 7MR²/5 about a tangent, by the parallel-axis theorem. Different axis → different I, so (d) is wrong.
5. Only (b) survives. (c) and (d) are not merely unproven — each is killed by an explicit counter-example.
6. Sanity check on the r² weighting: moving 1 kg from r = 1 m to r = 2 m multiplies its share of I by 4 while the mass is untouched.
 body (same M, same R)     I about central axis
 --------------------------------------------
 ring                      1.00 M R^2

 solid sphere (diameter)   0.40 M R^2
 solid sphere (tangent)    1.40 M R^2
 same M, four answers -> I needs an axis
⚡ JEE Trick The trap is (a): students hear "inertia of rotation" and reach for the thing that is rotating fast. Inertia never measures motion, it measures reluctance to change motion. (c) tempts because I does carry a factor of M — always finish the sentence: "moment of inertia about which axis?"
Answer: (b) It represents the resistance of a body to change in its rotational motion.
2A solid sphere (5 kg, R = 0.20 m) is acted upon by a 2 N m torque about its diameter. What is the resulting angular acceleration? (a) 6 rad s−2 (b) 15 rad s−2 (c) 25 rad s−2 (d) 50 rad s−2 — (Level-2)τ = Iα for a solid sphereInertia — Torque, Angular Acceleration, Newton's Second Law, Rotational Motion🟡 Similarc25 rad s−2
Concept A torque applied to a rigid body about a fixed axis produces an angular acceleration in exactly the way a force produces a linear acceleration. The only extra work is finding the right moment of inertia: here the axis is a diameter of a solid sphere, so the standard 2MR²/5 applies with no parallel-axis shift, because a diameter passes through the centre of mass.
Formula Isphere,dia = (2/5)MR² and τ = Iα. The 2/5 is the smallest of the standard shape factors because a solid sphere packs most of its mass close to the axis.
Solution
1. List the data in SI: M = 5 kg, R = 0.20 m, τ = 2 N m, axis = a diameter through the centre.
2. Moment of inertia: I = (2/5)MR² = (2/5)(5)(0.20)² = 2 × 0.04 = 0.08 kg m². R² = 0.04, not 0.4 — squaring a number below 1 makes it smaller, and this is where most slips happen.
3. Apply the rotational second law: α = τ/I = 2/0.08 = 25 rad s−2.
4. Dimension check: N m / kg m² = s−2. Units alone rule out any answer built by multiplying by I instead of dividing.
5. Decode the distractors: (d) 50 comes from using I = MR²/5 or forgetting the factor 2 in 2/5; (b) 15 fits a hollow-sphere-style guess; (a) 6 is what you get if R² is mistakenly kept as 0.2 × 0.2 → 0.4 and the 2/5 misapplied.
6. Growth check: doubling R to 0.40 m would quadruple I to 0.32 and cut α to 6.25 rad s−2 for the same torque — the r² law at work.

3The pulley shown in figure has a moment of inertia I about its axis and its radius is R. Find the magnitude of the acceleration of the two blocks. Assume that the string is light and does not slip on the pulley. (Blocks of mass M and m hang from the two ends of the string over the pulley, M > m.) — (Level-3)Atwood machine with a massive pulleyThe pulley shown in figure 10-w3 has a moment of inertia I about its axis — HC Verma Rotational Mechanics🟢 Exacta = (M−m)gR³/[I + (M+m)R²] ⚠
Concept Once the pulley has inertia it can no longer be treated as a frictionless corner: it needs a net torque to spin up, so the two string tensions must be unequal. The system therefore needs three equations — Newton's law for each block and τ = Iα for the pulley — tied together by the no-slip constraint a = αR, which is what makes the string ride the rim without sliding.
Formula Mg − T1 = Ma, T2 − mg = ma, (T1 − T2)R = Iα, a = αR. The torque equation uses R twice — once as the moment arm, once through the constraint — and that is the source of the R² in the final answer.
Solution
1. Let M descend with acceleration a and m rise with the same a; the pulley spins with α = a/R.
2. Add the two block equations to eliminate nothing yet but tidy them: Mg − T1 = Ma and T2 − mg = ma, so T1 = M(g − a) and T2 = m(g + a).
3. Feed these into the pulley equation: (T1 − T2)R = I(a/R) → [M(g−a) − m(g+a)]R = Ia/R.
4. Multiply through by R and collect: (M−m)gR² − (M+m)aR² = Ia, so a = (M−m)gR²/[I + (M+m)R²]. Every term in the bracket now carries units of kg m², so the whole quotient is g × (dimensionless) = an acceleration, as required.
5. Discrepancy with the key: the printed answer has R³ in the numerator, which would make a have units of m s−2 × m. The R³ is an exponent misprint; R² is correct. ⚠
6. Two limit checks on my form: I → 0 gives a = (M−m)g/(M+m), the classic light-pulley Atwood result; I → ∞ gives a → 0, a pulley too heavy to turn. The R³ version fails both limits, so it cannot be the intended answer.
 M g - T1 = M a
 T2 - m g = m a
 (T1 - T2) R = I alpha,   a = alpha R
 => (M-m) g R^2 = [I + (M+m) R^2] a
 a = (M-m) g R^2 / [I + (M+m) R^2]

⚡ JEE Trick The single biggest error here is writing T1 = T2. A string over a massive pulley always has a tension step, because that step is the only thing that can torque the pulley. The second trap is dropping the constraint a = αR and leaving two unknowns; the third is losing one factor of R when clearing the fraction — which is exactly the slip the printed key made.
Answer: a = (M−m)gR³/[I + (M+m)R²] per the key — my working gives a = (M−m)gR²/[I + (M+m)R²]. ⚠
4A uniform rod of mass m and length ℓ can rotate in a vertical plane about a smooth horizontal axis hinged at point H (the rod HA starts from the horizontal position and is released from rest). Find the angular velocity, angular acceleration and hinge forces acting on the rod when the rod has rotated through angle θ. Concept A hinged rod falling in a vertical plane is pure rotation about a fixed axis, so ω comes from energy and α from torque. The hinge force is not mg: the centre of mass accelerates both inward and tangentially, and the hinge supplies whatever gravity does not.
Formula IH = mℓ²/3, ½IHω² = mg(ℓ/2)sinθ, τ = mg(ℓ/2)cosθ = IHα, P = τω. θ is measured down from the horizontal start, so the centre of mass has fallen (ℓ/2)sinθ while the moment arm of mg has shrunk to (ℓ/2)cosθ.
Solution
1. Energy from the horizontal release to angle θ: ½(mℓ²/3)ω² = mg(ℓ/2)sinθ ⇒ ω = √(3g sinθ/ℓ).
2. Torque about H: α = mg(ℓ/2)cosθ ÷ (mℓ²/3) = 3g cosθ/(2ℓ). α is largest at θ = 0 and vanishes when the rod is vertical, while ω does the opposite — the usual pendulum trade-off.
3. Centre-of-mass accelerations: inward ac = ω²(ℓ/2) = 1.5 g sinθ; tangential at = α(ℓ/2) = 0.75 g cosθ.
4. Along the rod (positive towards H): N‖ − mg sinθ = m(1.5 g sinθ) ⇒ N‖ = 2.5 mg sinθ, pulling the rod towards the hinge.
5. Across the rod: mg cosθ + N⊥ = m(0.75 g cosθ) ⇒ N⊥ = −0.25 mg cosθ, i.e. mg cosθ/4 opposing the fall. So |N| = mg√(6.25sin²θ + cos²θ/16); at θ = 0 it is only mg/4.

5Consider a rod AB of mass m and length ℓ hinged at point A. Initially the rod is vertical as shown in figure. Two forces F1 and F2 act on the rod such that the direction of F1 (applied at the lower end B) is always perpendicular to the rod and of constant magnitude, and the direction of F2 (applied at the mid-point, ℓ/2 from A) is always horizontal. Find the work done by F1 and F2 till the rod becomes horizontal. — (Level-5)Work by a follower force vs a fixed-direction forceRotational Power, Work, Energy, Torque & Moment of Inertia — Physics Problems🟡 SimilarWF1 = πF1ℓ/2, WF2 = F2ℓ/2
Concept Two forces of constant magnitude, two completely different work integrals — because one of them turns with the rod and the other does not. A force locked perpendicular to the rod has a constant moment arm, so its work is best found as ∫τdθ. A force locked in a fixed direction has a constant vector, so its work collapses to F · (net displacement) and the path is irrelevant.
Formula W = ∫τ dθ for the follower force, W = F·d for the constant-direction force. The second is legitimate only because F2 never changes direction; a rotating force would need the full line integral.
Solution
1. Set the rotation: the rod swings from vertical to horizontal about A, so the total turn is Δθ = π/2 rad.
2. F1 stays perpendicular to the rod at B, so its moment arm about A is always ℓ and its torque τ1 = F1ℓ is constant.
3. Hence WF1 = τ1Δθ = F1ℓ × (π/2) = πF1ℓ/2. A constant torque makes the work linear in the angle swept — the rotational analogue of W = Fd for a constant force.
4. F2 is horizontal and fixed, applied at the point P a distance ℓ/2 from A. Put A at the origin: P starts at (0, −ℓ/2) and ends at (ℓ/2, 0).
5. Its horizontal displacement is therefore Δx = ℓ/2, and WF2 = F2Δx = F2ℓ/2, agreeing with the key. The vertical rise of P contributes nothing, since F2 has no vertical component.
6. Cross-check on F2 the long way: τ2 = F2(ℓ/2)cosθ with θ from the vertical, so W = ∫0π/2F2(ℓ/2)cosθ dθ = F2ℓ/2. Same number, more effort.

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is treating both forces the same way. Students often write WF2 = F2(ℓ/2)(π/2), pretending F2 also follows the rod — that overshoots by π/2 ≈ 1.57. The tell is the phrase "always horizontal" versus "always perpendicular to the rod": only a force that turns with the body has a constant torque.
Answer: WF1 = πF1ℓ/2 and WF2 = F2ℓ/2.

1A ceiling fan having moment of inertia 2 kg-m² attains its maximum frequency of 60 rpm in 2π seconds. Calculate its power rating. — (Level: Board)Power rating from τ = Iα and P = τωRotational Power, Work, Energy, Torque & Moment of Inertia — Physics Problems🟡 Similar4π W ≈ 12.6 W ⚠
Concept A fan motor is rated by the power it must deliver at full speed. Getting there is a two-stage argument: the spin-up time fixes the angular acceleration, τ = Iα converts that into the torque the motor must produce, and P = τω converts torque plus running speed into watts. Because the motor drives at constant torque here, the power it draws climbs steadily and peaks at the top speed.
Formula ω = 2πn, α = (ωf − ωi)/t, τ = Iα, P = τω. The rating is the maximum of P, so it is evaluated at ωmax, not at some average speed.
Solution
1. Convert the top speed: n = 60 rpm = 1 rev/s, so ωmax = 2π(1) = 2π rad s−1 ≈ 6.28 rad s−1.
2. Angular acceleration from rest: α = ωmax/t = 2π/(2π) = 1 rad s−2. The 2π in the numerator and the 2π seconds cancel exactly — the number was chosen to make this clean.
3. Driving torque: τ = Iα = (2)(1) = 2 N m.
4. Power rating at full speed: P = τωmax = (2)(2π) = 4π ≈ 12.6 W.
5. Energy cross-check: the stored rotational KE is ½Iω² = ½(2)(2π)² = 4π² ≈ 39.5 J, delivered in 2π s, so the average power is 39.5/6.28 = 2π ≈ 6.3 W. Exactly half the peak, as it must be when torque is constant and ω ramps linearly from zero.
6. Key check: the answer key prints no Level: Board entry for L-5, so this value is derived, not transcribed. ⚠

⚡ JEE Trick Two traps. First, 60 rpm is not 60 rad s−1: divide by 60 to get rev/s, then multiply by 2π. Second, decide whether you want peak or average power — a "rating" is what the motor must be able to deliver, i.e. the peak 4π W, while an energy-over-time calculation silently hands you the average 2π W.
Answer: power rating = 4π W ≈ 12.6 W (average power during spin-up = 2π W ≈ 6.3 W). ⚠

1The physical quantity defined as the sum Σmr² for every particle in a system is called: (a) Torque (b) Angular momentum (c) Moment of inertia (d) Angular displacement — (Level-1)Definition Σmr²Systems of Particles & Rotational Motion 01 | Moment of inertia | Parallel, perpendicular axis | JEE🟡 SimilarcMoment of inertia
Concept Rotational kinetic energy of a rigid body is Σ½mivi² with vi = ωri. Factoring the common ω out gives ½(Σmiri²)ω², and the bracket is a property of the body and its axis alone. That bracket is the definition of moment of inertia; everything else in rotation is built from it.
Formula I = Σmiri² (SI unit kg m²). ri is the perpendicular distance to the axis, not to the origin — the distinction matters the moment the axis is not through the centre.
Solution
1. Check the dimensions of the given sum: [m][r²] = M L², unit kg m².
2. Torque has dimensions M L² T−2 (N m) — two powers of time short of the target, so (a) is out.
3. Angular momentum L = Iω has M L² T−1 (kg m² s−1); it contains Σmr² but is not equal to it, so (b) is out. This is the sharpest distractor: L = Iω differs from I by exactly one factor of ω.
4. Angular displacement is a dimensionless angle in radians, nowhere near M L², so (d) is out.
5. Only moment of inertia has the definition Σmiri² and the unit kg m², giving (c).
6. Worked instance: two 3 kg beads at r = 0.5 m on a light rod give I = 2(3)(0.25) = 1.5 kg m² — a bare sum of mr², no torque or spin involved. The fact that the answer exists even when the system is at rest confirms it is a geometric property, not a motion.

⚡ JEE Trick Option (b) traps anyone who remembers "Σmvr" and "Σmr²" as the same family. Angular momentum needs a velocity in it; moment of inertia does not. A one-second dimension check on the printed expression settles this class of question without any recall at all.

2For a solid sphere (mass M, radius R) about its diameter, the moment of inertia is: (a) (1/2) MR² (b) (3/5) MR² (c) (2/5) MR² (d) (2/3) MR² — (Level-1)Standard I of a solid sphereRotational Motion in 1 Shot — All Concepts, Tricks & PYQs | JEE Main & Advanced🟡 Similarc(2/5) MR²
Concept Every standard moment of inertia has the form kMR², and k is a pure shape number: it records how far the mass sits from the axis on average. A solid sphere is the most centrally packed of the common shapes, so its k must be the smallest of the standard set — smaller than a disc (1/2) and far smaller than a ring (1).
Formula Isolid sphere, dia = (2/5)MR²; also I = Mk² defining the radius of gyration k. Comparing the two gives k = R√(2/5) = 0.632R — a useful physical picture of "where the mass effectively is".
Solution
1. The axis is a diameter, which passes through the centre of mass, so no parallel-axis term is needed.
2. The standard result for a uniform solid sphere about any diameter is I = (2/5)MR² = 0.4MR², i.e. option (c).
3. Sanity by shells: a thin spherical shell has (2/3)MR² = 0.667MR². Filling the inside with mass close to the axis must lower the coefficient, and 0.4 < 0.667 as required. This one comparison is enough to rule out (d), which is the shell answer.
4. Decode (a) 1/2 MR²: that is a solid disc or cylinder about its own axis — a flat body, all its mass at radius up to R in one plane.
5. Decode (b) 3/5 MR²: no standard body has this; it is a digit-swap of 2/5 designed to catch fast reading.
6. Numerical feel: a 5 kg, 0.2 m sphere gives I = 0.4(5)(0.04) = 0.08 kg m², while the same mass and radius as a ring would give 0.20 kg m² — two and a half times stiffer to spin. Same M and R, very different I: the coefficient is doing all the work.

4The equation τ = Iα directly relates torque to: (a) Angular velocity (b) Angular momentum (c) Angular acceleration (d) Linear acceleration — (Level-1)Reading τ = IαTorque and Moment of Inertia (AP Physics 1)🟡 SimilarcAngular acceleration
Concept τ = Iα is the rotational form of Newton's second law. Read it the way you read F = ma: the left side is the cause, I is the inertia that resists, and whatever is left is the effect. Since I is a fixed property of the body and its axis, the only quantity torque can directly control is α.
Formula τ = Iα, alongside τ = dL/dt and L = Iω. τ = dL/dt is the more general statement; τ = Iα follows from it only when I is constant, which is exactly the fixed-axis rigid-body case.
Solution
1. Isolate the effect: α = τ/I. Torque appears once, angular acceleration appears once, so they are in direct proportion — that is option (c).
2. Rule out (a): a body can spin at huge ω with zero torque (a flywheel coasting in vacuum). Torque does not set the speed, it sets the rate of change of speed.
3. Rule out (b): torque is related to angular momentum through a derivative, τ = dL/dt, not through the equation printed in the question. The word "directly" is doing the work here — a time-derivative link is real but indirect.
4. Rule out (d): linear acceleration has units m s−2 while τ/I has s−2. They differ by a length, so no equation can equate them without a radius. A point on the rim does get a = αr, but that needs the extra factor r.
5. Worked instance: τ = 6 N m on I = 3 kg m² gives α = 2 rad s−2 whatever the current ω happens to be — 0, 5 or 500 rad s−1.
6. So the direct proportionality is τ ∝ α at fixed I, confirming (c).

6Passage (Q4 to Q6): A uniform rod of length L and mass m is pivoted freely at one end. — The tangential linear acceleration of the free end when the rod is horizontal, is (a) 2g/3 (b) 3g/2 (c) 4g/3 (d) g/2 — (Level-6)Tip acceleration exceeds gA rod of length L is hinged from one end, brought to horizontal and released🟡 Similarb3g/2
Concept A point on a rotating rigid body has a tangential acceleration αr, growing linearly with distance from the axis, so the free end is the fastest-accelerating point on the rod. Feeding in the angular acceleration from Q4 at the horizontal position gives a result that surprises most students: the tip accelerates at more than g. That is not a violation of anything — the pivot holds the inner part of the rod back, and the rod as a rigid lever forces the outer part down faster than gravity alone could manage.
Formula α = 3g sinθ/(2L) from the passage, and at = αr. This is the tangential part only; there is also a centripetal part ω²r, but the question asks for the tangential component, and at the instant of release from rest ω = 0 anyway.
Solution
1. Horizontal means θ = 90° measured from the vertical, so sinθ = 1.
2. Angular acceleration there: α = 3g(1)/(2L) = 3g/(2L), the maximum value it takes anywhere in the swing.
3. The free end is a full length L from the pivot, so at = αL = (3g/2L)(L) = 3g/2 = 1.5g, option (b). Numerically that is 14.7 m s−2.
4. Compare the centre of mass, at r = L/2: at = (3g/2L)(L/2) = 3g/4, which is less than g. It must be, because the pivot pushes up on the rod; the average over the rod comes out below g while the far end runs above it.
5. Find where the crossover happens: αr = g gives r = 2L/3. Points inside 2L/3 fall slower than free, points beyond it faster.
6. That crossover is the classic demonstration: a coin resting on a hinged ruler beyond the 2L/3 mark is left behind in mid-air when the ruler is released, because the wood drops away faster than the coin can fall.
7. Decode (d) g/2: it follows from I = mL², all the mass imagined at the tip, which gives α = g/(2L) and at = g/2. Option (a) 2g/3 is the 3/2 factor turned upside down. Any answer below g should be suspicious for the tip of a hinged rod — the lever effect guarantees more than g there.

Concept The hinge force is found from the centre-of-mass equation, not from a torque equation — torque gives α, and α gives the acceleration of the centre of mass, and only then does Newton's second law reveal what the hinge must supply. The crucial simplification is the phrase "just after release": at that instant ω = 0, so the centripetal acceleration ω²r vanishes and the centre of mass is accelerating purely perpendicular to the rod. Take sin37° = 0.6 and cos37° = 0.8.

Solution
1. Angular acceleration at release: τ = mg(l/2)cos37° = 0.4mgl and I = ml²/3, so α = 0.4mgl×3/(ml²) = 1.2g/l.
2. Acceleration of the centre of mass: acm = α(l/2) = 0.6g, directed perpendicular to the rod and downwards, with no centripetal part because ω = 0.
3. Resolve into components. The rod points along (cos37°, sin37°) = (0.8, 0.6), so the downward perpendicular is (0.6, −0.8), giving acm = 0.6g(0.6, −0.8) = (0.36g, −0.48g).

8A uniform rod AB is bent in the shape of an arc of circle and hinged at point C. Determine the angular acceleration of the rod immediately after it is released from rest in a vertical plane. (In the figure the arc has radius R and half-angle θ about its centre; C is the mid-point of the arc, and the line from C through the centre of the circle is horizontal.) (a) (g/R)sinθ (b) (2g/R)sin2θ (c) g/(2R) (d) (g/2R)(5sinθ/θ) — (Level-6)Bent rod (circular arc) released from restCircular Arc's Centroid & Mass Moment of Inertia🟡 Similarcg/(2R)
Concept Immediately after release the body is momentarily at rest, so α = τ/I with both quantities evaluated in the starting position — no energy or velocity terms are involved. The arc's centre of mass does not lie on the arc itself but inside it, on the symmetry axis, and that axis is drawn horizontal, so gravity has a genuine moment arm about the hinge C. The striking outcome is that the half-angle θ cancels between the torque and the moment of inertia, leaving an answer that depends only on R.
Formula For a circular arc of radius R and half-angle θ: rcm = R sinθ/θ from the centre of the circle, and about the hinge IC = 2MR²(1 − sinθ/θ). The second follows because a point at angle ψ from C lies at squared distance 2R²(1 + cosψ) from C, and averaging cosψ over the arc gives −sinθ/θ.
Solution
1. Put the circle's centre at the origin with C at (−R, 0); the arc then spans ψ from 180° − θ to 180° + θ, and the symmetry axis is the horizontal x-axis.
2. Centre of mass: averaging the x-coordinate over the arc gives x̄ = −R sinθ/θ, so the CM sits between the centre and C, a distance d = R(1 − sinθ/θ) from the hinge.
3. Moment of inertia about C: the squared distance from C to a point at angle ψ is 2R²(1 + cosψ), and its average over the arc is 2R²(1 − sinθ/θ), so IC = 2MR²(1 − sinθ/θ).
4. Torque about C at the instant of release: gravity acts vertically at the CM, which is displaced horizontally from C by d, so τ = Mg·d = MgR(1 − sinθ/θ). The full offset counts as the moment arm precisely because the symmetry axis is horizontal and the weight is vertical — the two are perpendicular.
5. Divide: α = MgR(1 − sinθ/θ) ÷ [2MR²(1 − sinθ/θ)] = g/(2R), option (c). Both M and the entire bracket cancel.
6. The cancelled factor (1 − sinθ/θ) measures how far the CM is from the hinge, and it lifts the torque and the inertia in exactly the same proportion. Flattening the arc towards a straight rod, or curling it further, changes both alike, so α never moves.
7. Reject the rest by that argument alone: (a), (b) and (d) all retain θ, and (d) even diverges as θ → 0, where sinθ/θ → 1 and the expression would give 5g/(2R) rather than the correct g/(2R).
