Concept Dulong and Petit's law is an empirical rule for solid elements: the product of an element's atomic mass and its specific heat is very nearly a fixed constant, about 6.4 cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (per mole). Physically it says every solid element stores roughly the same heat per mole of atoms, so this constant lets chemists estimate an approximate atomic mass simply by measuring the specific heat.
Formula atomic mass × specific heat ≈ 6.4, where atomic mass is in g mol⁻¹ and specific heat in cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹, giving the molar heat capacity in cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. 6.4 cal ≈ 3R (R = 1.987 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, so 3R ≈ 5.96 ≈ 6 cal) — the classical value for a solid with 3 vibrational degrees of freedom. Rearranged: approx atomic mass = 6.4 ÷ specific heat.
Solution
1. State the law: for a solid element, atomic mass × specific heat = a constant.This is the whole content of Dulong–Petit; the only question is the numerical value of that constant.
2. The constant equals the molar heat capacity of a solid element ≈ 3R ≈ 6 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, rounded in the classical statement to 6.4 cal g⁻¹ K⁻¹.
3. Compare with the options: 2.34, 3.2, 6.4, 12.8 — only 6.4 matches the accepted value, option (c).3.2 is half of it and 12.8 is double it — common distractors around the true 6.4.

So when CuSO₄·5H₂O and ZnSO₄·7H₂O are said to form crystals of similar shape, the law lets us infer a chemical fact about copper and zinc: they act with equal valency in these salts.
Key relation isomorphous (same crystal shape) ⇒ same valency of the corresponding elements. Here both salts are of the type MSO₄·xH₂O where M is a divalent metal, so Cu and Zn each carry valency 2 (Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺). Isomorphism does not require equal molar mass, colour, or density.

1. Identify the shared formula type: CuSO₄·5H₂O and ZnSO₄·7H₂O are both metal(II) sulphate hydrates, MSO₄·xH₂O.
2. Apply the law: similar crystal shape ⇒ the metals occupy equivalent lattice positions ⇒ they must have the same valency.Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺ are both divalent, consistent with the MSO₄ formula (SO₄²⁻ needs a +2 cation).
3. Test the other options: equal molar mass, colour, or density are not implied by isomorphism — so option (b), equal valencies, is the only correct inference.

1Define the term mole.Mole definitionMole concept — mole, mass, molar mass, Avogadro's constant🟡 Similar—
Concept The mole is chemistry's counting unit — it bundles an enormous, fixed number of particles into one convenient package, exactly as "a dozen" means 12. Individual atoms and molecules are far too small and numerous to count one by one, so chemists agreed on a group size, Avogadro's number, that links the microscopic particle count to a macroscopic, weighable mass. This single definition underpins every stoichiometric calculation.
Key relation 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons) = the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. Anchoring the count to 12 g of C-12 fixes NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹ and makes the molar mass in g mol⁻¹ numerically equal to the atomic/molecular mass in u.
Solution
1. State the definition: a mole is the amount of a substance that contains as many elementary entities as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.This ties an abstract count to a concrete, measurable mass standard.
2. That number is Avogadro's number, NA = 6.022×10²³, so 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ particles.
3. Always specify the entity: 1 mol of O atoms = 6.022×10²³ atoms, while 1 mol of O₂ = 6.022×10²³ molecules (= 2×6.022×10²³ atoms)."Mole of what?" matters — the same number applies, but to different particles.
4. Consequence: 1 mole of a substance weighs its molar mass in grams and, for a gas at STP, occupies 22.4 L.
 quantity per mole      value
 particles              6.022×10²³ (N_A)
 mass                   molar mass in grams
 gas volume (STP)       22.4 L
⚡ JEE Trick Treat a mole exactly like "a dozen" — a fixed count (6.022×10²³) you multiply or divide by. Always ask "mole of what entity?" to avoid the atom-vs-molecule trap (1 mol O₂ = 2 mol O atoms). The 12 g of C-12 is the definition anchor, not a value to compute.
Key point: a mole is the amount of substance containing 6.022×10²³ (Avogadro's number) elementary entities — the same number of atoms present in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.

Concept Although both read "12 of carbon," the two phrases live in completely different worlds. 12 u is a microscopic, relative mass — it describes a single carbon atom on the amu scale. 12 g is a macroscopic, absolute mass you can weigh on a balance — it is a whole mole of carbon, i.e. 6.022×10²³ atoms. The gram value is therefore NA times heavier than the amu value, because 1 g = NA u.
Key relation 1 g = NA u = 6.022×10²³ u, so 12 g of C = NA × (12 u atoms) = 1 mole of C. Multiplying a single 12 u atom by Avogadro's number scales it up to the 12 g laboratory quantity — the amu and the mole are two ends of the same bridge.
Solution
1. 12 u of carbon = the mass of one carbon-12 atom, a relative mass on the amu scale (1 u = 1/12 of a C-12 atom).u is a unit for weighing single particles, not lab samples.
2. 12 g of carbon = the mass of one mole = 6.022×10²³ carbon atoms, an absolute mass in grams.Because the atomic mass is 12, one mole (NA atoms) weighs 12 g.
3. Relate them: 12 g contains NA atoms, each of mass 12 u, so 12 g = 6.022×10²³ × 12 u — the gram figure is 6.022×10²³ times the amu figure.
4. In short: 12 u → a single atom (micro, relative); 12 g → a mole of atoms (macro, absolute, weighable).

Concept Cannizzaro's method finds an element's atomic mass by studying many of its volatile compounds. For each compound you first get the molecular mass (from vapour density), then work out how much of the element it contains. The atomic mass is taken to be the smallest such mass of the element that ever appears in one molecule of any compound — reasoning that a single molecule can contain no fewer than one whole atom, so the minimum must be one atom's worth.
Key relation atomic mass = smallest mass of the element present in 1 molecule of any of its compounds. 3. Take the smallest of these masses — that value corresponds to just one atom, so it is the atomic mass.A molecule cannot hold a fractional atom, so the minimum element-mass seen equals one atom's mass.
4. Therefore the atomic mass is indicated by the smallest mass of the element found in any compound — option (c).

Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ is (CH₂O)₆, and both CH₃COOH and HCOOCH₃ are C₂H₄O₂ = (CH₂O)₂ — all three reduce to CH₂O, so all three must share one %C. Sucrose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ does not reduce to CH₂O, so it stands apart.
Formula %C = (total C mass ÷ molar mass) × 100. Mass fraction of carbon; computing it for the empirical unit CH₂O (12 in 30) settles every member of that family at once. Atomic masses: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.
Solution
1. (a) C₆H₁₂O₆: M = 72 + 12 + 96 = 180; %C = (72 ÷ 180) × 100 = 40%.
2. (b) CH₃COOH = C₂H₄O₂: M = 24 + 4 + 32 = 60; %C = (24 ÷ 60) × 100 = 40%.
3. (c) HCOOCH₃ = C₂H₄O₂ also: same molecular formula as (b) ⇒ %C = 40%.Acetic acid and methyl formate are isomers — identical composition, different structure.
4. (d) C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁: M = 144 + 22 + 176 = 342; %C = (144 ÷ 342) × 100 = 42.1% — different.Sucrose is a condensation product (lost H₂O), so it no longer fits the (CH₂O)ₙ pattern.
5. Same %C group: (a), (b), (c) at 40%; (d) is the odd one out.

Trap: sucrose "looks like" a carbohydrate cousin, but C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ ≠ (CH₂O)ₙ (11 oxygens vs 12 carbons) — checking the empirical ratio beats pattern-matching on names.
Answer: (a), (b), (c) — all 40% carbon.
10One mole of oxygen gas at STP is equal to (a) 6.022×10²³ molecules of oxygen (b) 6.022×10²³ atoms of oxygen (c) 16 g oxygen (d) 32 g oxygenMole of O₂ at STPOne mole of oxygen gas at STP is equal to…🟢 Exacta, d6.022×10²³ molecules of oxygen, 32 g oxygen
Concept "One mole of oxygen gas" means one mole of O₂ molecules — and each equivalent statement must be tested against that. A mole is 6.022 × 10²³ of the counted unit (here, molecules), and the mass of a mole equals the molar mass in grams (32 g for O₂). The classic confusions are molecule-vs-atom (a factor of 2 in count) and molecular-vs-atomic mass (32 vs 16 in grams).
Formula 1 mol O₂ = NA molecules = 2NA atoms = 32 g; NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, M(O₂) = 2 × 16 = 32 g mol⁻¹. The mole counts whatever entity is named; O₂ is diatomic, so atoms are double the molecules, and the molar mass doubles the atomic mass.
Solution
1. (a) 6.022 × 10²³ molecules of oxygen — correct: one mole of any species is NA of that species, here O₂ molecules.
2. (b) 6.022 × 10²³ atoms of oxygen — wrong: 1 mol O₂ contains 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms.NA atoms is only half a mole of O₂ (it is 1 mol of atomic O).
3. (c) 16 g oxygen — wrong: 16 g is the mass of 1 mol of O atoms, i.e. only 0.5 mol of O₂ gas.
4. (d) 32 g oxygen — correct: M(O₂) = 2 × 16 = 32 g mol⁻¹, so one mole weighs 32 g.
5. Correct statements: (a) and (d). (STP is a red herring here — mole, count and mass are condition-independent; only the 22.4 L volume needs STP.)
