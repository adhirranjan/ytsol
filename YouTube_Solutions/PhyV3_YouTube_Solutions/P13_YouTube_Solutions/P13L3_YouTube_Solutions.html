Concept Every element dm of the ring pulls the point at the centre straight towards itself, along a radius. For a uniform ring, the element diametrically opposite pulls with an equal force in the exact opposite direction, so the pulls cancel in pairs. The trap is to plug r = R into GM/r² as if the whole mass sat at one point; that formula needs every element to pull in the same direction, which here they do not.
Formula dE = G·dm/R² (along the radius towards dm) and on the axis E(x) = GMx/(R² + x²)3/2. Field is a vector, so the contributions add vectorially, not as magnitudes.
Solution
1. Take a small element dm at angle φ on the ring; its field at the centre O is dE = G·dm/R², pointing from O towards dm.
2. The element at angle φ + π has the same mass dm (uniform ring) and is at the same distance R. Uniform linear density λ = M/(2πR).
3. Its field at O is G·dm/R² pointing the opposite way, so the pair gives dE − dE = 0.
4. The whole ring is made of such diametric pairs, so the vector sum Enet = Σ(pairs) = 0.
5. Check with the axial formula: E(x) = GMx/(R² + x²)3/2 = GM·0/(R²)3/2 = 0 at x = 0. The centre is the x = 0 point of the axis.
6. Note the magnitude sum Σ G·dm/R² = GM/R² is not zero, which is exactly the number option (b) shows; it is the scalar sum, not the field.

Concept "Weight 50 kg" in this book means 50 kg-wt, i.e. the force mg on a 50 kg man. Weight changes because g changes; mass never does. Inside a uniform Earth only the mass enclosed within radius r pulls, so g falls linearly with depth and is zero at the centre. The trap is to answer 50 kg (confusing mass with weight) or to think gravity is strongest at the centre.
Formula gd = g(1 − d/R), equivalently g(r) = GMr/R³. Shell theorem: the outer shell exerts zero net force on a point inside it, so only the inner sphere of radius r counts.
Solution
1. Surface weight W = mg = 50 kg-wt, so the man's mass m = 50 kg, which stays 50 kg everywhere.
2. At depth d the effective pulling mass is M(r³/R³) with r = R − d. Uniform density ⇒ enclosed mass ∝ volume ∝ r³.
3. So g(r) = G·M(r³/R³)/r² = GMr/R³ = g·(r/R) = g(1 − d/R).
4. At the centre d = R: gc = g(1 − d/R) = g(1 − R/R) = 0.
5. Weight at centre Wc = m·gc = 50 × 0 = 0 kg-wt (a spring balance would read zero).
6. Physically: the man is pulled equally in every direction by the Earth around him, so the net force on him is zero, though his mass is still 50 kg and his inertia is unchanged.

Concept On a rotating Earth part of the true gravitational pull supplies the centripetal force mω²(R cosλ) needed to move in a circle about the axis. So the effective g is reduced by ω²R cos²λ. Formula g' = g − ω²R cos²λ ⇒ gpole − geq = ω²R. Sphere assumed, so the true g is the same everywhere; only rotation makes the difference.

6. Δg = ω²R = 5.29 × 10⁻⁹ × 6.37 × 10⁶ = 3.37 × 10⁻² m/s².

Concept A uniform hollow sphere (thin or thick-walled) exerts no net gravitational force on a mass anywhere inside its cavity, not just at the centre. Newton's shell theorem: nearer parts of the shell pull harder but there is less of them; farther parts pull weaker but there is more mass, and the two exactly balance. The trap in the figure is that P is drawn off-centre, tempting you to say the force depends on P's position.
Formula Einside = 0, Vinside = −GM/R = constant. E = −dV/dr, and a constant potential has zero gradient.
Solution
1. Draw a thin double cone with vertex at P; it cuts the shell in two small patches at distances r₁ and r₂ from P.
2. The patch areas grow as the square of distance: dA₁ ∝ r₁², dA₂ ∝ r₂², so their masses dm₁ ∝ r₁², dm₂ ∝ r₂². Same solid angle, uniform surface density.
3. Their pulls on P: dF₁ = Gm·dm₁/r₁² ∝ r₁²/r₁² = 1 and dF₂ ∝ r₂²/r₂² = 1, equal in size and opposite in direction.
4. The whole shell is covered by such cone pairs, so Fnet = 0 for every point of the cavity, centred or not.
5. A thick-walled sphere is just many thin concentric shells, each giving zero inside, so the total is also zero.
6. Potential check: inside, V = −GM/R everywhere, so E = −dV/dr = 0 ⇒ F = mE = 0.
