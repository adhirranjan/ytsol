1According to Amonton's First Law, the frictional force is directly proportional to: (a) surface area (b) mass of the object (c) normal reaction (d) speed of motion.Laws of friction FRICTION Class 11 | Static, Limiting and Kinetic Friction — Part 1 🟡 Similar cNormal reaction
Concept Amonton's First Law of dry friction: the friction force between two surfaces is proportional to the normal contact force pressing them together — not to how big the contact patch is, not to the mass by itself, and not to how fast the body slides.
Formula f_k = μ_k N and f_s ≤ μ_s N N is the normal reaction; μ is a dimensionless number fixed by the pair of materials.
Solution
1. Eliminate (a): Amonton's Second Law says friction is independent of the apparent area of contact.Doubling the footprint halves the pressure, so the real microscopic contact area — and hence friction — is unchanged.
2. Eliminate (b): mass only enters through N. On a horizontal floor N = mg, but on an incline N = mg cosθ, and if you pull at an angle N changes again. So mass is not the proportional quantity — N is.
3. Eliminate (d): Coulomb's law states kinetic friction is independent of sliding speed at moderate speeds.
4. What remains is (c) normal reaction, which is exactly what f = μN states.
⚡ JEE Trick Whenever an option list offers "mass" and "normal reaction" together, choose normal reaction. The classic trap is a block on an incline or a block pulled at an angle, where N ≠ mg and answers built on mass fail.
Answer: (c) normal reaction

Concept Kinetic friction on a body always opposes the relative velocity of that body with respect to the surface it touches — never the ground-frame velocity.

1Which of the following statements is true? (a) More force is required to start a motion than to maintain it against friction. (b) Less force is required to start a motion than to maintain it against friction. (c) Equal force is required to start a motion and to maintain it against friction. (d) None of these.Static vs kinetic friction Class 11 | Friction Lecture 1 | Static vs Kinetic Friction | JEE & NEET 🟡 Similar aMore force to start than to maintain
Concept Limiting static friction is larger than kinetic friction for the same pair of surfaces, so breaking a body loose costs more force than keeping it going.
Key relation μ_s > μ_k ⇒ f_limiting = μ_s N > f_k = μ_k N At rest the microscopic asperities have time to interlock and cold-weld; once sliding they only skim the tops.
Solution
1. To start motion you must exceed the limiting static friction μsN.
2. To maintain uniform motion you only need to balance the kinetic friction μkN.
3. Since μs > μk (e.g. wood on wood: 0.25 vs 0.20 in the chapter's table), the starting force is larger.
4. Hence (a) is true.This is why a heavy piece of furniture jerks forward the instant it starts to move — the resisting force suddenly drops.
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the ordering μs > μk > μrolling. Almost every theory MCQ in this level is a re-phrasing of that one inequality.
Answer: (a) More force is required to start a motion than to maintain it.
2If the smoothness of the surfaces in contact is increased, the force of friction: (a) must decrease (b) must increase (c) may decrease (d) none of these.Polishing limit, cold welding Basics of Coulomb Friction — laws and factors affecting friction 🟡 Similar cmay decrease
Concept Polishing reduces friction only up to a point. Beyond that limit the surfaces come so close that intermolecular adhesion (cold welding) takes over and friction rises again.
Key relation friction = interlocking term + adhesion term Polishing shrinks the interlocking term but grows the adhesion term by increasing the true contact area.
Solution
1. For ordinary rough surfaces, smoothing removes asperities and friction falls — so "decrease" is possible.
2. For highly polished surfaces (e.g. two optically flat metal plates), molecular bonds form across the interface and friction increases.
3. Because both outcomes are possible, the statement cannot be "must decrease" (a) — the correct hedged option is (c) may decrease.
⚡ JEE Trick Any option containing "must" is usually a trap in friction theory. When the physics has an exception (here, cold welding), the examiner's answer is the "may" option.
Answer: (c) may decrease

1Platform A is in free-fall. A block slides with velocity v on surface A, while observer B is in a frame accelerating with a = g/2. Concept Friction is a real interaction force between two surfaces. Real forces are the same in every reference frame — only the fictitious (pseudo) forces change when you switch to a non-inertial frame.
Key relation f = μN where N and the relative sliding are properties of the contact, not of the observer Changing observer does not change how hard the two surfaces press or scrape.
Solution
1. Friction depends on the normal reaction at the interface and the relative velocity of the two contacting bodies.
2. Both of those are relative quantities between the block and platform A — they do not change when you re-describe the motion from B's accelerating frame.
3. In B's frame you would add a pseudo-force to the block, but that pseudo-force is a separate entry in the free-body diagram; it does not alter the friction term.
4. Hence the friction value is unchanged: (a) Remains same.
⚡ JEE Trick Rule of thumb: contact forces, tension, spring force, gravity and friction are frame-invariant; only pseudo-forces appear or vanish. The trap is thinking free-fall "cancels gravity" and therefore kills friction — it changes the pseudo-force, not the contact.
Answer: (a) Remains the same

Find the minimum force applied on the block, up the inclined plane, in order to support it. (a) (√3 − 1)/√3 N (b) 1 N (c) 1/√3 N (d) √3 N. (g = 10 m/s²)Minimum force on an incline Friction on Inclined Plane | Angle of Repose | Numerical Problems | JEE / NEET 🟡 Similar a(√3 − 1)/√3 N
Concept To find the MINIMUM supporting force, let friction help as much as it possibly can: the block is on the verge of sliding down, so limiting friction acts fully up the incline and the applied force only has to cover the shortfall.
Formula F_min = mg sinθ − μ_s mg cosθ Subtract, do not add — friction is on your side when the block tends to slide down.
Solution
1. Weight: mg = (√2/10) × 10 = √2 N. With θ = 45°, sin45° = cos45° = 1/√2.
2. Along the plane: mg sin45° = √2 × (1/√2) = 1 N down the slope.
3. Normal reaction: N = mg cos45° = 1 N.
4. Limiting friction: μsN = (1/√3) × 1 = 1/√3 N, available up the slope.
5. Check that support is actually needed: tan45° = 1 > μs = 0.577, so the block would slide without help. ✓
6. Fmin = 1 − 1/√3 = (√3 − 1)/√3 N ≈ 0.42 N ⇒ option (a).

                   F + 0.577 = 1
                   F = 0.423 N = (sqrt3 - 1)/sqrt3
⚡ JEE Trick "Minimum force to support" ⇒ subtract friction. "Minimum force to push it up" ⇒ add friction (F = mg sinθ + μmg cosθ). Option (d) √3 N is roughly that opposite case — read the direction of impending motion carefully.
Answer: (a) Fmin = (√3 − 1)/√3 N ≈ 0.42 N

Concept Identical touching spheres put their centres at the vertices of an equilateral triangle, so each line of centres makes 60° with the horizontal (30° with the vertical). The top ball's weight is transmitted along those lines, and its horizontal part must be held by ground friction.
Formula 2 R cos30° = mg (top ball); then μ ≥ R sin30° / N_ground
Solution
1. Geometry: centres form an equilateral triangle, so each contact normal on the top ball is 30° from the vertical.Because the upper ball is smooth, the only force at each of its contacts is along the line of centres.
2. Top ball equilibrium: 2R cos30° = mg ⇒ R = mg/(2 × 0.866) = mg/√3.
3. That force acts on each lower ball. Its horizontal component pushing outward = R sin30° = (mg/√3)(1/2) = mg/(2√3).
4. Vertical load on the ground from one lower ball: N = mg (its own weight) + R cos30° = mg + (mg/√3)(√3/2) = mg + mg/2 = 3mg/2.
5. No sliding requires friction ≥ outward push: μN ≥ mg/(2√3) ⇒ μ ≥ [mg/(2√3)] / (3mg/2) = 1/(3√3).
6. So μmin = 1/(3√3) ≈ 0.192 ⇒ option (b).
