1The potential energy of a particle under a conservative force is given by U(x) = (x² − 3x) J. The equilibrium position of the particle is at (A) x = 1.5 m (B) x = 2 m (C) x = 2.5 m (D) x = 3 mEquilibrium from U(x)The potential energy of a conservative system is V(x) = (x² − 3x) J: find the equilibrium position🟢 ExactAx = 1.5 msimilarP8 · L5 · SolvedEx · Ex-4
Concept A conservative force is the negative slope of its potential-energy curve, F = −dU/dx. A particle is in equilibrium where the net force is zero, i.e. where the U–x graph is flat (dU/dx = 0), not where U itself is zero. U here is an upward-opening parabola, so its turning point is a minimum (stable). Option (D) x = 3 m is the trap: U(3) = 0, but the slope there is not zero.
Formula F = −dU/dx; equilibrium when dU/dx = 0; stable if d²U/dx² > 0. dW = F dx = −dU for a conservative force, so F is the negative slope of U; zero slope means zero force.
Solution
1. Differentiate: U(x) = x² − 3x, so dU/dx = 2x − 3.
2. Force on the particle: F = −dU/dx = −(2x − 3) = 3 − 2x.
3. Equilibrium: F = 0 ⇒ 3 − 2x = 0 ⇒ x = 3/2 = 1.5 m.
4. Nature: d²U/dx² = d(2x − 3)/dx = 2 > 0, so U is minimum there — stable equilibrium. Umin = (1.5)² − 3(1.5) = 2.25 − 4.5 = −2.25 J.
5. Direction check: for x < 1.5 m, F = 3 − 2x > 0 (pushes right); for x > 1.5 m, F < 0 (pushes left). The force always points back to x = 1.5 m.
6. Only (A) fits; x = 3 m is merely a root of U, and x = 2 m, 2.5 m give F = −1 N, −2 N.
 U = x^2 - 3x
 dU/dx = 2x - 3 = 0      ->  x = 1.5 m
 d2U/dx2 = 2 > 0         ->  stable,  U_min = -2.25 J
 x = 3 m: U = 0 but F = 3 - 6 = -3 N  (not equilibrium)
⚡ JEE Trick For any quadratic U = ax² + bx + c the equilibrium sits at the vertex x = −b/2a — here −(−3)/(2·1) = 1.5 m in one line, stable when a > 0. The book’s solved example does the same with U = ax² − bx ⇒ x = b/2a. Never pick the point where U = 0 (x = 3 m); equilibrium is where the slope of U vanishes, not U itself.
Answer: (A) x = 1.5 m.

Concept The block is pressed into a vertical wall, so the wall’s normal reaction is horizontal and is fixed by the push, not by the weight. The weight acts down the wall and the only force that can hold it up is static friction along the wall. “Just hold” means the block is on the verge of sliding down, so friction is at its limiting value μN. Do not write N = mg here — that habit belongs to blocks on a horizontal floor.
Formula N = F (horizontal balance), W = fL = μN = μF (vertical balance at the limit). Two independent directions: the push is balanced by the wall’s normal reaction, the weight by friction; limiting friction ties the two together through μ.
Solution
1. Forces on the block: push F = 10 N into the wall, normal reaction N from the wall (outward), weight W downward, static friction f upward along the wall.Friction opposes the impending downward slip, so it points up.
2. Horizontal equilibrium: N = F = 10 N.The wall pushes back with exactly the force pressing the block into it.
3. Vertical equilibrium: f = W.
4. “Just hold stationary” ⇒ friction is limiting: f = μN = 0.2 × 10 = 2 N.Any heavier block would exceed the 2 N that this normal force can generate as friction, and would slide.
5. Hence W = f = 2 N (a block of mass about 0.2 kg). Option (A).

Dividing (10/0.2 = 50 N) answers the inverse question “what push holds a 10 N block” and is exactly the planted distractor (C). Sanity check: with μ < 1 the supported weight must be less than the push of 10 N, and (A) is the only option below 10 N.
Answer: (A) 2 N.

Concept The weight of the hanging part, transmitted as tension at the edge, drags the rest of the chain; static friction on the part still on the table resists it, and that friction can grow only to μN with N = weight of the table portion alone — the hanging part presses on nothing.
