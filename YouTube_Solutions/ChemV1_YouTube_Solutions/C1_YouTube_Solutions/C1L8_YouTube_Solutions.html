1Which statement correctly defines an ideal gas per kinetic theory? (intermolecular forces negligible)Ideal gas conceptA-REALGAS🟡 SimilaraIntermolecular forces between molecules are negligible.
Concept The kinetic molecular theory builds the idea of a perfect (ideal) gas from a short list of postulates. Two of them are the decisive ones: gas molecules are treated as point masses of negligible volume, and there are no intermolecular forces (neither attraction nor repulsion) between them except during the instant of a collision. Everything else in ideal-gas behaviour — obeying PV = nRT exactly at all P and T — follows from these two assumptions. The question asks which single statement captures a defining postulate.
Formula The model behind the definition is PV = nRT, which holds exactly only when the two correction terms of a real gas both vanish: the attraction term a/V² → 0 and the excluded-volume term nb → 0. Zero intermolecular force kills the a-term; zero molecular size kills the b-term — leaving the plain ideal-gas equation.
Solution
1. List the ideal-gas postulates: molecules are point masses (no volume) in constant random motion, collisions are perfectly elastic, and intermolecular forces are negligible.These are the assumptions that make PV = nRT exact.
2. Test each option against the postulates: only "intermolecular forces between molecules are negligible" is a genuine ideal-gas assumption.
3. Reject the distractors: statements that give molecules finite volume, or that invoke attractive forces, describe a real gas, not an ideal one.
4. Hence the defining statement is option (a).
⚡ JEE Trick For "define an ideal gas" MCQs, circle the two magic phrases — "no intermolecular forces" and "negligible molecular volume." Any option that keeps forces or volume is describing a real gas. Trap: options that sound rigorous ("molecules attract weakly at large separation") are real-gas language and must be rejected.
Answer: (a) Intermolecular forces between molecules are negligible.
2Cause of deviation of a real gas from Boyle's law at high pressureDeviation causeA-DEVIATION🟡 SimilarcFinite size of molecules (excluded volume) becomes significant.
Concept An ideal gas assumes molecules occupy zero volume. At high pressure the gas is squeezed into a tiny volume, so the molecules' own size is no longer negligible compared with the container — the space genuinely available for movement is less than the measured volume V. This "excluded volume" effect makes the real volume larger than the ideal prediction (Z rises above 1), which is exactly the departure from Boyle's law seen at high P.
Formula van der Waals corrects the volume by subtracting the excluded volume: (P + a/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. The term nb is the volume the molecules themselves take up; at high P, V is small so nb is a large fraction of V and dominates, pushing Z = PV/nRT above 1.
Solution
1. Boyle's law (PV = constant at fixed T) assumes point molecules with no volume.
2. At high pressure V is very small, so the finite molecular volume (nb) becomes a significant fraction of V.When molecules are packed close, their own bulk can no longer be ignored.
3. The gas resists further compression, so real V > ideal V and PV rises above nRT (Z > 1) — a positive deviation from Boyle's law.
4. The responsible factor is therefore the finite size of molecules (excluded volume) — option (c).
⚡ JEE Trick Split the causes by regime: high pressure ⇒ molecular volume (b) dominates ⇒ Z > 1; low temperature ⇒ attractive forces (a) dominate ⇒ Z < 1. Trap: at high pressure the answer is not attractive forces — those matter most at low temperature; here it is the excluded volume.
Answer: (c) Finite size of molecules (excluded volume) becomes significant.
3At low P & high T real gases obey the ideal-gas equation closely because …Deviation conditionswatch?v=oaKibzneZF4🟢 ExactaAttractive forces and molecular volume become negligible.
Concept A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas exactly when its two "real" features stop mattering. At low pressure the molecules are far apart, so the volume they occupy is a negligible fraction of the huge container — the excluded-volume (b) term dies. At high temperature the molecules move so fast (large kinetic energy) that fleeting attractions cannot hold them — the attraction (a) term dies. With both corrections gone, PV = nRT holds and Z ≈ 1.
Formula (P + a/V²)(V − nb) = nRT collapses to PV = nRT when a/V² → 0 (large V at low P) and nb ≪ V (molecules tiny vs. container). Low P makes V enormous so both correction terms shrink; high T makes kinetic energy swamp the attractive potential energy.
Solution
1. Low pressure ⇒ large molar volume ⇒ molecules far apart ⇒ their own volume (nb) is negligible vs. V.
2. High temperature ⇒ high average speed ⇒ molecules zip past one another ⇒ intermolecular attractions have no time to act.Kinetic energy overwhelms the shallow attractive potential.
3. Both van der Waals corrections vanish, so real behaviour merges into PV = nRT and Z ≈ 1.
4. The reason is that attractive forces and molecular volume become negligible — option (a).
⚡ JEE Trick Memorise the "ideal window": low P + high T ⇒ Z ≈ 1. It is the mirror image of "high P + low T ⇒ maximum deviation." Trap: an option claiming molecules "move slower" at high T is wrong — high T means faster molecules, which is precisely why attractions cannot bind them.

Concept The compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT measures how far a real gas strays from ideal behaviour: Z = 1 is perfectly ideal, Z < 1 means the gas is more compressible than ideal (it occupies less volume than PV = nRT predicts), and Z > 1 means it is less compressible. Solution
1. Read the value: Z = 0.94, which is less than 1.
2. Z < 1 ⇒ Vreal = Z·Videal < Videal, i.e. the gas is more compressible than ideal.Multiplying Videal by 0.94 gives a smaller real volume.

4. This regime (Z < 1, attractions winning) is a negative deviation — option (b).
⚡ JEE Trick Read Z at a glance: Z < 1 → attractions dominate → negative deviation; Z > 1 → repulsion/size dominates → positive deviation; Z = 1 → ideal. Trap: "negative deviation" does not mean Z is negative — Z is always positive; it means Z dips below the ideal line of 1.

Concept The van der Waals equation fixes the two failings of the ideal-gas law by adding an internal-pressure correction (for attractions) and subtracting an excluded-volume correction (for molecular size). To find the pressure, solve the equation for P explicitly: P equals the "size-corrected" ideal pressure minus the attraction term. It is pure substitution once you rearrange — no iteration needed because V is given.
Formula (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT ⇒ P = nRT/(V − nb) − an²/V². Units: a in L² atm mol⁻², b in L mol⁻¹, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T in K, V in L. The first term is the pressure a gas of finite-size molecules would exert; the second subtracts the pull of attractions that lowers the observed pressure. Here n = 1.
Solution
1. List data: n = 1, a = 1.36, b = 0.032, T = 300 K, V = 1.00 L, R = 0.0821.
2. Compute the numerator nRT = 1 × 0.0821 × 300 = 24.63 L atm.
3. Compute the corrected volume V − nb = 1.00 − (1 × 0.032) = 0.968 L.
4. Repulsive/ideal term: nRT/(V − nb) = 24.63 ÷ 0.968 = 25.44 atm.24.63 ÷ 0.968 = 25.44 (dividing by less than 1 raises the pressure).
5. Attraction term: an²/V² = 1.36 × 1² ÷ (1.00)² = 1.36 atm.
6. Subtract: P = 25.44 − 1.36 = 24.08 atm.Attractions reduce the wall pressure, so the a-term is subtracted.

1Distinguish between an ideal gas and a real gasIdeal vs real gasA-REALGAS🟡 Similar—
Concept An ideal gas is a theoretical model built on the kinetic-theory postulates — point-mass molecules with no volume and no intermolecular forces — that obeys PV = nRT exactly at every pressure and temperature. A real gas is any actual gas (H₂, CO₂, N₂ …): its molecules do have finite size and do attract one another, so it obeys PV = nRT only approximately, deviating most at high pressure and low temperature. The distinction is essentially "no ideal gas exists; every real gas is an approximation to it."
Key relation Ideal: PV = nRT (Z = 1 always). Real: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT (Z = PV/nRT ≠ 1 in general). The constants a (attraction) and b (molecular volume) measure exactly how far a real gas departs from the ideal model.
Solution
1. Molecular volume: ideal gas molecules occupy no volume; real gas molecules have a finite size.
2. Intermolecular forces: absent in an ideal gas; present (attractions and, when close, repulsions) in a real gas.
3. Obedience to laws: an ideal gas obeys PV = nRT at all P, T; a real gas obeys it only at low P and high T.
4. Compressibility factor: ideal Z = 1 always; real Z > 1 or < 1, and it can be liquefied (an ideal gas cannot).
 property              ideal gas          real gas
 molecular volume      zero (point mass)  finite (constant b)
 intermolecular force  none               present (constant a)
 obeys PV=nRT          at all P, T        only at low P, high T
 Z = PV/nRT            = 1 exactly        ≠ 1 (>1 or <1)
 can be liquefied      no                 yes
⚡ JEE Trick Remember the two-word summary: an ideal gas has "no size, no force"; a real gas has "size (b) and force (a)." Every other difference (liquefiable, Z ≠ 1, deviation) flows from those two. Trap: no gas is truly ideal — real gases only approach ideal behaviour at low P and high T.
Key point: an ideal gas (no molecular volume, no intermolecular forces) obeys PV = nRT with Z = 1 at all conditions; a real gas has finite molecular size (b) and intermolecular attractions (a), so Z ≠ 1 and it obeys PV = nRT only at low pressure and high temperature.
2What is compressibility factor?Definition of ZA-ZFACTOR🟢 Exact—
Concept The compressibility factor Z is a single dimensionless number that tells you, at a glance, how much a real gas departs from ideal behaviour. It is defined as the ratio of the gas's actual PV product to the value nRT it would have if it were ideal. Because an ideal gas has PV = nRT exactly, its Z is always 1; any measured Z different from 1 is a direct, quantitative measure of non-ideality — and its sign of departure (above or below 1) reveals which molecular effect is winning.
Key relation Z = PV/nRT (dimensionless). Equivalently Z = Vreal/Videal, since Videal = nRT/P. The units of PV (L·atm) and nRT (L·atm) cancel, so Z is a pure number comparing real volume to ideal volume at the same P and T.
Solution
1. Define it: Z = PV/nRT — the actual PV divided by the ideal-gas value nRT at the same temperature.
2. Ideal case: PV = nRT, so Z = 1 at every pressure — the benchmark.By definition an ideal gas gives Z = 1 exactly.
3. Interpret departures: Z < 1 ⇒ Vreal < Videal ⇒ attractive forces dominate (negative deviation); Z > 1 ⇒ Vreal > Videal ⇒ repulsion/finite size dominates (positive deviation).
4. So Z both quantifies deviation and identifies its cause.
 Z value    V_real vs V_ideal   behaviour
 Z = 1      equal               ideal gas
 Z < 1      smaller             attractions dominate (−ve deviation)
 Z > 1      larger              size/repulsion dominates (+ve deviation)
⚡ JEE Trick Read Z as a "deviation meter": distance of Z from 1 = size of deviation; side of 1 = its cause (below ⇒ attractions, above ⇒ molecular volume). Trap: Z is dimensionless — never attach a unit; and Z = 1 does not prove a gas is truly ideal, only that its two corrections happen to cancel at that P, T.
Key point: the compressibility factor Z = PV/nRT = Vreal/Videal is a dimensionless measure of non-ideality — Z = 1 for an ideal gas, Z < 1 when attractions dominate, and Z > 1 when molecular volume/repulsion dominates.
3Show that Z can be written as Z = Vreal/VidealZ as volume ratioA-ZFACTOR🟡 Similar—
Concept Z is defined as PV/nRT, but there is an equivalent and very useful reading: Z is the ratio of the volume the real gas actually occupies to the volume an ideal gas would occupy at the same P and T. Proving this simply means replacing nRT in the definition using the ideal-gas law, because for a hypothetical ideal gas at the given pressure, Videal = nRT/P. The algebra takes two lines and converts an abstract ratio into a concrete "which is bigger, real or ideal volume?" picture.
Key relation Start from Z = PVreal/nRT. For an ideal gas at the same P, T: PVideal = nRT ⇒ nRT = PVideal. Both gases share the same P, T and amount n, so nRT is the same quantity and can be written as P·Videal.
Solution
1. Write the definition using the real (measured) volume: Z = (P · Vreal) ÷ (nRT).
2. For an ideal gas at the same P and T, PVideal = nRT, so substitute nRT = P · Videal.Videal is just nRT/P — the volume the ideal-gas law predicts under these conditions.
3. Substitute into the definition: Z = (P · Vreal) ÷ (P · Videal).
4. Cancel the common factor P: Z = Vreal / Videal.Same P on top and bottom divides out, leaving a pure volume ratio.
 Z = P·V_real / (nRT)
   = P·V_real / (P·V_ideal)     [since nRT = P·V_ideal]
   = V_real / V_ideal           [cancel P]
⚡ JEE Trick The one-step substitution to remember is nRT = P·Videal — it converts Z = PV/nRT into the volume ratio instantly. Once in this form, sign-reading is trivial: Z < 1 ⇒ Vreal < Videal (compressed by attractions), Z > 1 ⇒ Vreal > Videal (expanded by finite size).
Key point: substituting nRT = P·Videal into Z = PVreal/nRT cancels P and gives Z = Vreal/Videal — Z is the ratio of the real molar volume to the ideal molar volume at the same P and T.
