1Molarity equation of a mixture of solutions of same substance is given by (Level-1)Molarity of a mixtureMolarity of mixing two solutions (MV = M₁V₁ + M₂V₂)🟡 SimilarbM₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃ + … = M(V₁+V₂+V₃)
Concept When several solutions of the same solute are poured together, no solute is created or destroyed — the total moles of solute is conserved. Molarity times volume gives moles (M = n ÷ V ⇒ n = M·V), so each portion contributes MiVi moles. Adding these and dividing by the combined volume gives the final molarity. The correct algebraic statement of "moles in = moles out" is therefore the equation in option (b).
Formula n = M·V for each part (n in mol, M in mol L⁻¹, V in L). Conservation of moles: M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃ + … = Mfinal(V₁+V₂+V₃+…). The left side sums the moles supplied by every solution; the right side is (final molarity) × (total volume) = total moles in the mixture — the two must be equal.
Solution
1. Write the moles delivered by each solution: part 1 gives M₁V₁, part 2 gives M₂V₂, part 3 gives M₃V₃, and so on.Rearranging M = n/V for one portion gives n = M·V directly.
2. Add them for the total solute moles: ntotal = M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃ + … .Mixing does not change the number of solute particles, so moles simply add.
3. The total volume (assuming volumes are additive) is Vtotal = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + … .
4. Final molarity = total moles ÷ total volume, so Mfinal(V₁+V₂+V₃) = M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃, which is exactly option (b).
 quantity        expression
 moles part i    M_i · V_i
 total moles     M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃ + …
 total volume    V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + …
 mixing law      ΣM_iV_i = M_final · ΣV_i     ← option (b)

Keep every volume in the same unit (mL is fine on both sides since the unit cancels). Trap: this only holds for the same solute; for different solutes moles do not simply pool into one molarity.
Answer: (b) M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ + M₃V₃ + … = M(V₁+V₂+V₃).
21 M HCl and 2 M HCl are mixed in volume ratio of 4 : 1. Final molarity? (Level-2)Molarity of a mixture (HCl)1 M HCl and 2 M HCl mixed in ratio 4:1🟢 Exactc1.2 M
Concept Mixing two solutions of the same solute (here HCl) conserves the total moles of solute, so the final molarity is the total moles divided by the total volume. When only a volume ratio is given, you never need actual volumes — just assign convenient volumes in that ratio (4 parts and 1 part) and the parts cancel. The trap is averaging the molarities as (1+2)/2 = 1.5 M, which ignores that four times more of the dilute acid is present.
Formula Mfinal = (M₁V₁ + M₂V₂)/(V₁ + V₂) moles add on mixing; molarity = total moles ÷ total volume. Data: M₁ = 1 M, M₂ = 2 M, V₁ : V₂ = 4 : 1.
Solution
1. Let the volumes be V₁ = 4V and V₂ = 1V (any common unit).Only the ratio matters — V will cancel from numerator and denominator.
2. Moles from solution 1: n₁ = M₁V₁ = 1 × 4V = 4V mol-units.
3. Moles from solution 2: n₂ = M₂V₂ = 2 × 1V = 2V mol-units.
4. Total moles = 4V + 2V = 6V; total volume = 4V + V = 5V.Volumes of dilute aqueous solutions of the same acid are taken as additive.
5. Mfinal = 6V ÷ 5V = 6/5 = 1.2 M.V cancels, confirming the answer is independent of the actual sample sizes.

⚡ JEE Trick For a ratio a : b, use the weighted average in one line: M = (aM₁ + bM₂)/(a + b) = (4·1 + 1·2)/5 = 1.2 M. Sanity check: the answer must lie between 1 M and 2 M and sit closer to the solution present in larger amount (here 1 M). Trap: the simple mean 1.5 M is a planted wrong option in ratio-mixing questions.
Answer: (c) 1.2 M.

5Two samples of HCl (1.0 M and 0.25 M) mixed to prepare 0.75 M; find volumes (Level-5)Mixing to a target molarityTwo samples of HCl of 1.0 M and 0.25 M are mixed, find volumes of these samples🟢 ExactdI, II, III & IV (all give the 2 : 1 ratio)
Concept To hit a target molarity by blending a stronger and a weaker solution of the same solute, the moles again just add: M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ = Mtarget(V₁+V₂). This single equation fixes the ratio V₁ : V₂ — not the absolute volumes — so any pair of volumes in that ratio works. The question lists several volume pairs (I–IV) and asks which are valid; the answer is "all that share the required ratio."
Formula M₁V₁ + M₂V₂ = Mt(V₁+V₂) with M₁ = 1.0 M (strong), M₂ = 0.25 M (weak), Mt = 0.75 M. Rearranged to a ratio: V₁/V₂ = (Mt − M₂)/(M₁ − Mt) — the alligation rule. This "cross-difference" form comes straight from the conservation equation and gives the mixing ratio without needing actual volumes.
Solution
1. Apply conservation of moles: 1.0·V₁ + 0.25·V₂ = 0.75·(V₁ + V₂).Left = moles from both samples; right = moles present at the target concentration.
2. Expand the right side: 1.0V₁ + 0.25V₂ = 0.75V₁ + 0.75V₂.
3. Collect V₁ on the left, V₂ on the right: 1.0V₁ − 0.75V₁ = 0.75V₂ − 0.25V₂.Subtract 0.75V₁ and 0.25V₂ from both sides.
4. Simplify each side: 0.25V₁ = 0.50V₂.
5. Divide both sides by 0.25V₂: V₁/V₂ = 0.50 ÷ 0.25 = 2, i.e. V₁ : V₂ = 2 : 1.The strong acid must be twice the volume of the weak one; 0.75 sits twice as far from 0.25 as from 1.0.
6. Cross-check with alligation: (0.75 − 0.25)/(1.0 − 0.75) = 0.50/0.25 = 2 ✓. Every listed pair (I–IV) reduces to 2 : 1, so all are correct — option (d).
 alligation:   1.0 ─────┐        ┌──── (0.75 − 0.25) = 0.50  → parts of 1.0 M
                        0.75
              0.25 ─────┘        └──── (1.0 − 0.75) = 0.25  → parts of 0.25 M
 ratio V₁ : V₂ = 0.50 : 0.25 = 2 : 1
⚡ JEE Trick For "mix two strengths to reach a middle strength," use alligation: V₁ : V₂ = (Mt − M₂) : (M₁ − Mt) — the closer the target is to a solution, the less of that solution you need. Here the answer is a ratio, so any volumes obeying 2 : 1 qualify. Trap: don't hunt for unique volumes — the data fix only the ratio, which is why "all of them" (d) is right.
Answer: (d) I, II, III & IV (all give the 2 : 1 ratio).

22.0 g metal + O₂ → 3.2 g oxide; 1.42 g metal in steam → 2.27 g oxide. Which law?Law of definite proportions2.0 g metal → 3.2 g oxide — which law🟢 Exact—
Concept The same metal oxide is made by two different routes — direct combination with O₂, and reaction of the metal with steam. If the oxide formed has the same fixed composition by mass both times, the data illustrate the Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions: a given compound always contains the same elements in the same mass ratio, regardless of its source or method of preparation. The test is simply to compute the metal : oxygen mass ratio (or % metal) in each experiment and show they are equal.
Formula mass of O = mass of oxide − mass of metal; compare % metal = (wmetal/woxide) × 100 in both samples. Equal percentages ⇒ fixed composition ⇒ definite proportions.
Solution
1. Experiment 1: oxygen combined = 3.2 − 2.0 = 1.2 g; metal : oxygen = 2.0 : 1.2 = 5 : 3.
2. % metal in oxide 1 = (2.0/3.2) × 100 = 62.5%.
3. Experiment 2: oxygen combined = 2.27 − 1.42 = 0.85 g; metal : oxygen = 1.42 : 0.85 ≈ 5 : 3 (1.671 vs 1.667).The tiny difference is rounding in the given data, not a real composition change.
4. % metal in oxide 2 = (1.42/2.27) × 100 = 62.6% ≈ 62.5%.
5. Same mass ratio from two different preparations (dry O₂ vs steam) ⇒ the data verify the Law of Definite Proportions (Proust).
 expt   metal(g)  oxide(g)  oxygen(g)  % metal
 1      2.00      3.20      1.20       62.5
 2      1.42      2.27      0.85       62.6
 same ratio either route => Law of Definite Proportions   <-- answer
⚡ JEE Trick "Same compound, different methods/sources, same composition" ⇒ definite proportions. Contrast: "same two elements forming different compounds (H₂O vs H₂O₂)" ⇒ multiple proportions; "mass of products = mass of reactants" ⇒ conservation of mass. Trap: options bait you with multiple proportions — here only ONE oxide is formed, just by two routes.
Answer: Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions — % metal is 62.5% by both routes.

Concept A chemical formula is a mole recipe: one mole of CH₃CHO (acetaldehyde, condensed formula C₂H₄O) contains exactly 2 mol C atoms, 4 mol H atoms and 1 mol O atoms. For any sample, moles of an element = (moles of compound) × (subscript of that element). The classic slip is miscounting the condensed structural formula CH₃CHO — the H atoms are 3 (from CH₃) + 1 (from CHO) = 4, and the C atoms are 1 + 1 = 2.
Formula n(element) = n(compound) × subscript subscripts give atoms per molecule, hence moles per mole. CH₃CHO ⇒ molecular formula C₂H₄O; n(compound) = 2 mol.

1. Expand the condensed formula: CH₃–CHO has C: 1+1 = 2, H: 3+1 = 4, O: 1 ⇒ C₂H₄O.Count group by group; the aldehyde H in –CHO is the one usually missed.
2. Moles of acetaldehyde itself = 2 mol (given — the compound's own amount is unchanged).
3. Moles of C = 2 × 2 = 4 mol.
4. Moles of H = 2 × 4 = 8 mol.
5. Moles of O = 2 × 1 = 2 mol.
6. Check via molar mass: 2 mol × 44 g mol⁻¹ = 88 g total; 4×12 + 8×1 + 2×16 = 48 + 8 + 32 = 88 g ✓.Element masses must rebuild the sample mass — a 5-second audit of the counts.

1Which is used for trace concentration? (ppt / molarity / percent / molality)Trace-concentration unitsppm, ppb, ppt — trace concentration units🟡 Similarappt
Concept When a solute is present in extremely tiny amounts — pollutants in water, dopants in a crystal — molarity or percentage give clumsy numbers like 0.0000001%. Chemists instead use "parts per" units that count how many parts of solute sit among a huge number of parts of solution. The smaller the amount to be reported, the larger the denominator we need: ppm (10⁶), ppb (10⁹), ppt (10¹²). For genuine trace levels the finest of these, ppt, is the natural choice.
Formula parts per X = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × X. For ppm X = 10⁶, ppb X = 10⁹, ppt X = 10¹² (parts per trillion). A bigger multiplier lets a vanishingly small mass ratio show up as a readable whole number, so the tiniest concentrations are quoted in ppt.
Solution
1. Rank the units by how small a quantity each is built to express: percent (10²) < ppm (10⁶) < ppb (10⁹) < ppt (10¹²).Each step up the exponent describes a solute 1000× more dilute.
2. "Trace concentration" means the solute is present at the very lowest measurable levels.
3. The unit designed for the smallest such amounts is parts per trillion (ppt), option (a).Molarity, percent and molality are all far too coarse for trace levels.
 unit      multiplier   typical use
 percent   10²          bulk composition
 ppm       10⁶          minor impurities
 ppb       10⁹          very dilute contaminants
 ppt       10¹²         TRACE concentration   ✓
⚡ JEE Trick Read the exponent as "how dilute": ppm 10⁶, ppb 10⁹, ppt 10¹² — the highest power (ppt) is for the faintest traces. Trap: "ppt" almost always means parts per trillion here; a few old texts use "ppt" for parts per thousand, so let the context (trace levels) decide.
Answer: (a) ppt.

3Relation between molarity (M) and strength (S)Strength ↔ molarity4 ways to express concentration (M, strength)🟡 SimilarbS = M × molar mass
Concept Strength (S) is concentration expressed as grams of solute per litre of solution, while molarity (M) is moles per litre. The two describe the same solution but in different currencies — grams versus moles — and the exchange rate between grams and moles is the molar mass. So to turn moles-per-litre into grams-per-litre you simply multiply by how many grams sit in one mole.
Formula S = M × Mmolar, i.e. strength (g L⁻¹) = molarity (mol L⁻¹) × molar mass (g mol⁻¹). Multiplying mol L⁻¹ by g mol⁻¹ cancels the "mol" and leaves g L⁻¹ — exactly the units of strength. Equivalently M = S ÷ molar mass.
Solution
1. Start from the definitions: M = moles of solute per litre; S = grams of solute per litre.Both are "per litre," so only the solute measure (moles vs grams) differs.
2. Convert moles to grams using mass = moles × molar mass, applied per litre.
3. Therefore (grams per litre) = (moles per litre) × (grams per mole), i.e. S = M × molar mass — option (b).
4. Unit check: [mol L⁻¹] × [g mol⁻¹] = [g L⁻¹] ✓, confirming the relation.
 quantity   symbol   units        link
 molarity   M        mol L⁻¹      ×  molar mass (g mol⁻¹)
 strength   S        g L⁻¹        =  M × molar mass
⚡ JEE Trick Remember it as "grams = moles × molar mass, per litre" — multiply M by molar mass to go M→S, divide to go S→M. Trap: divide when you meant to multiply and the g L⁻¹ turns into mol²·g⁻¹ nonsense — always let the units guide you.
Answer: (b) S = M × molar mass.
