1 (i)Fill in the blanks: If A(x1,y1), B(x2,y2), C(x3,y3) are the vertices of ΔABC, then the co-ordinates of the centroid G(x,y) = ______.Centroid formulaCentroid of a Triangle || Derivation of Centroid of a Triangle || Coordinate Geometry || Avnish Sir🟡 SimilarG = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3)
Concept The centroid is the point where the three medians of a triangle meet. Because it balances the triangle, its co-ordinates are simply the arithmetic mean of the three vertices, and it always lies inside the triangle, splitting every median 2 : 1 measured from the vertex.
Formula G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). It is the average of the three position vectors, so no side lengths are needed — unlike the incentre.
Solution
1. A median joins a vertex to the mid-point of the opposite side; take median AD from A(x1,y1).
2. Mid-point of BC is D = ((x2+x3)/2, (y2+y3)/2).
3. The centroid G divides AD internally in the ratio 2 : 1 (vertex : mid-point). This 2 : 1 split is the defining property of the medians’ meeting point.
4. Section formula on A and D in 2 : 1: Gx = (2·(x2+x3)/2 + 1·x1)/(2+1) = (x1+x2+x3)/3.
5. Likewise Gy = (2·(y2+y3)/2 + y1)/3 = (y1+y2+y3)/3.
6. Hence G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). The same point is reached from any of the three medians, confirming concurrency.
  D = ((x2+x3)/2, (y2+y3)/2)
  Gx = (2*(x2+x3)/2 + x1)/3 = (x1+x2+x3)/3
  Gy = (2*(y2+y3)/2 + y1)/3 = (y1+y2+y3)/3
⚡ JEE Trick For the centroid just average the co-ordinates — never bother with side lengths or the section formula under exam pressure. A handy corollary: if the centroid and two vertices are known, the third vertex is 3G minus the sum of the other two, a one-line rescue for many problems.
Answer: G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3).
1 (ii)Fill in the blanks: If A(x1,y1), B(x2,y2), C(x3,y3) are the vertices of ΔABC, then the co-ordinates of the incentre I(x,y) = ______.Incentre formulaProof of the Formula to find the In-centre of a Triangle🟡 SimilarI = ((ax1+bx2+cx3)/(a+b+c), (ay1+by2+cy3)/(a+b+c))
Concept The incentre is the point where the three internal angle bisectors meet; it is the centre of the inscribed circle. Its co-ordinates are a weighted average of the vertices, each weighted by the length of the opposite side.
Formula I = ((ax1+bx2+cx3)/(a+b+c), (ay1+by2+cy3)/(a+b+c)). a = BC, b = CA, c = AB are the sides opposite A, B, C respectively.
Solution
1. Label the sides a = BC, b = CA, c = AB (each opposite the matching vertex).
2. The bisector from A meets BC at D, dividing it in the ratio AB : AC = c : b.
3. So D = ((bx2+cx3)/(b+c), (by2+cy3)/(b+c)).
4. The incentre I divides AD in the ratio (b+c) : a from A. This ratio comes from the angle-bisector length property.
5. Section formula on A and D gives I = (ax1+bx2+cx3)/(a+b+c) for x, and the matching expression for y.
6. Thus the weights are exactly the opposite side lengths. Replacing a with −a (etc.) yields the three ex-centres.
  D = ((b*x2 + c*x3)/(b+c), (b*y2 + c*y3)/(b+c))
  I divides AD in ratio (b+c) : a
  Ix = (a*x1 + b*x2 + c*x3)/(a + b + c)
  Iy = (a*y1 + b*y2 + c*y3)/(a + b + c)
⚡ JEE Trick Remember “side opposite is the weight”: the vertex gets the length of the side facing it. Because square-root side lengths appear, the incentre is often irrational even when the vertices are nice integers — a useful sanity flag.
Answer: I = ((ax1+bx2+cx3)/(a+b+c), (ay1+by2+cy3)/(a+b+c)).
1 (iii)Fill in the blanks: The orthocentre, centroid and circumcentre of a triangle are collinear, and the centroid G divides the segment joining the orthocentre and the circumcentre in the ratio ______.Euler line ratioProve that Centroid divides Orthocenter and Circumcenter in 2 : 1 ratio🟡 Similar2 : 1
Concept In any triangle the orthocentre H, the centroid G and the circumcentre O are collinear — they lie on the Euler line. The centroid sits between the other two and divides that segment in a fixed ratio that never depends on the shape of the triangle. This single fact ties three otherwise separate centres into one line and is one of the most heavily tested results in co-ordinate geometry.
Formula HG : GO = 2 : 1, equivalently G = (2O + H)/3. The centroid is always twice as far from the orthocentre as from the circumcentre, whatever the triangle.
Solution
1. Place the circumcentre at O; the position vector of H is OH = OA + OB + OC (a standard result).
2. The centroid is G = (OA + OB + OC)/3 = OH/3 relative to O.
3. So OG = (1/3) OH, meaning G lies one-third of the way from O to H.
4. Therefore OG : GH = 1 : 2, i.e. GH : GO = 2 : 1 from the orthocentre’s side. The centroid divides the join of orthocentre and circumcentre in 2 : 1.
5. Rearranging, G = (2O + H)/3, which lets you find any one centre from the other two.
6. Hence the required ratio is 2 : 1. For an equilateral triangle all three merge, so the ratio is still consistent at a single point.
  OH = OA + OB + OC   ->   OG = OH/3
  OG : GH = 1 : 2
  centroid divides ortho-to-circum in 2 : 1
  G = (2*O + H)/3
⚡ JEE Trick Burn in G = (2O + H)/3. Given any two of orthocentre, centroid and circumcentre, the third drops out in one line — a recurring shortcut in Euler-line MCQs that would otherwise need full altitude and perpendicular-bisector work. The same relation also says the orthocentre is the point H = 3G − 2O, which is handy when the circumcentre and centroid are the quantities you are handed.
Answer: 2 : 1.
1 (iv)Fill in the blanks: If ΔABC is an equilateral triangle, then the circumcentre, orthocentre and incentre all ______.Centres of an equilateral triangleProve that in an Equilateral Trianglethe Centroid and the Circumcenter Coincide with each other🟡 Similarcoincide (at one common point)
Concept An equilateral triangle is perfectly symmetric, so every special line — median, perpendicular bisector, angle bisector and altitude — is the same line. Consequently all four classical centres fall on one point.
Formula centroid = incentre = circumcentre = orthocentre. Each centre is the meeting point of a family of lines that here all coincide.
Solution
1. In an equilateral triangle the median from any vertex is also the altitude, the angle bisector and the perpendicular bisector of the opposite side.
2. Medians meet at the centroid; altitudes at the orthocentre; bisectors at the incentre; perpendicular bisectors at the circumcentre.
3. Since the four families are identical lines, their intersection is a single common point.
4. Check with the incentre formula: with a = b = c, I = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3) = G. Equal weights reduce the incentre to the plain average, the centroid.
5. The same symmetry forces O and H to that point too.
6. Hence all centres coincide. The Euler line collapses to a single point, so HG : GO = 2 : 1 holds trivially.
  equilateral: a = b = c
  I = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3) = G
  median = altitude = bisector = perp-bisector
  => centroid = incentre = circumcentre = orthocentre
⚡ JEE Trick If a problem states the centres coincide (or any two of them do), the triangle is equilateral — convert that single fact straight into a = b = c and solve. The converse is just as examinable.
Answer: they all coincide at one common point.

Concept Each classical centre of a triangle is defined by which family of cevians or bisectors passes through it. Matching the name to its defining intersection is a pure recall task but underpins every later calculation.

2. (B) Incentre = the point of intersection of the internal angle bisectors → option (r).
3. (C) Circum centre = the point of intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides → option (q).

1. Given H = (−3, 3) and G = (3, 3).
2. Ox = (3·3 − (−3))/2 = (9 + 3)/2 = 6, so p = 6.

Concept The centroid is the plain average of the three vertices, so each centroid co-ordinate hands you one linear equation in the unknown vertex co-ordinates. With two unknowns and two independent equations the system is solved instantly — no diagram, no section formula, just balancing the sums. This “sum of the x-values equals three times the centroid x” idea reappears across the whole chapter.
Formula G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). Set each co-ordinate equal to the given centroid value and clear the denominator 3.
Solution
1. The three vertices are (p, 4), (2, q) and the third vertex (4, 8); the centroid is given as (4, 6).
2. x-equation: (p + 2 + 4)/3 = 4 ⇒ p + 6 = 12 ⇒ p = 6.
3. y-equation: (4 + q + 8)/3 = 6 ⇒ q + 12 = 18 ⇒ q = 6. The two co-ordinate equations are independent, so p and q decouple cleanly.
4. So the product pq = 6 × 6 = 36. The problem defines r as the sum of the digits of pq.
5. Digit-sum of 36 is 3 + 6 = 9, hence r = 9.
6. Therefore p = 6, q = 6, r = 9, matching the key.
7. Quick check: vertices (6, 4), (2, 6), (4, 8) average to ((6+2+4)/3, (4+6+8)/3) = (4, 6) exactly. Back-substitution confirms both values at once.
  (p + 2 + 4)/3 = 4  -> p = 6
  (4 + q + 8)/3 = 6  -> q = 6
  pq = 36  ->  r = 3 + 6 = 9
⚡ JEE Trick Centroid problems reduce to “sum of the x-values = 3 times the centroid x”. Write that one line and the missing co-ordinate pops out — no diagram, no section formula. Watch the wording of the final step too: “sum of digits of pq” means treat pq as the number 36, not as 6 × 6 kept separate, a small trap that changes the value of r.
Answer: p = 6, q = 6, r = 9.

Concept The centroid formula works on any three points, whether or not they form a genuine triangle. Here the three points turn out to be collinear, yet their average is still a perfectly good point; we compute it and then carry out the stated digit operation on p2+q2. Separating the geometry (the centroid) from the arithmetic puzzle (the digit rule) keeps the work clean.
Formula G = ((x1+x2+x3)/3, (y1+y2+y3)/3). Plain mean of the three given points — collinearity does not block it.
Solution
1. The given points are (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4).
2. Gx = (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 6/3 = 2, so p = 2.
3. Gy = (2 + 3 + 4)/3 = 9/3 = 3, so q = 3.
4. Compute p2 + q2 = 22 + 32 = 4 + 9 = 13. r is the absolute difference of the digits of this number.
5. Digits of 13 are 1 and 3; absolute difference = |1 − 3| = 2, so r = 2.
6. Hence p = 2, q = 3, r = 2, in agreement with the key.
7. Note the three points satisfy y = x + 1, so the “triangle” has zero area — collinear — yet its centroid (2, 3) is still well defined. Equal gaps of (+1, +1) between consecutive points is the giveaway of collinearity.
  Gx = (1+2+3)/3 = 2
  Gy = (2+3+4)/3 = 3
  p^2 + q^2 = 4 + 9 = 13
  r = |1 - 3| = 2
⚡ JEE Trick A centroid never asks whether the points form a genuine triangle — the average exists even for collinear points. Spotting the arithmetic progression (1,2),(2,3),(3,4) tells you they are collinear, a free fact for an area or slope follow-up.
Answer: p = 2, q = 3, r = 2.

1Orthocentre, centroid and circumcentre are: (a) Equal (b) Collinear (c) Opposite (d) Parallel.Euler line (collinearity of centres)Euler Line - Circumcenter, Centroid and Orthocenter🟡 SimilarbCollinear
Concept The orthocentre, centroid and circumcentre of any triangle are never scattered at random: they always lie on a single straight line, the Euler line. The one exception — an equilateral triangle — collapses all three onto the same point, which is still trivially “collinear”.
Formula H, G, O collinear with G = (2O + H)/3, so HG : GO = 2 : 1. A fixed linear relation forces the three points onto one line.
Solution
1. Take the circumcentre O as origin; then the orthocentre is H = OA + OB + OC.
2. The centroid is G = (OA + OB + OC)/3 = H/3.
3. Since G is a scalar multiple of H (both measured from O), the points O, G, H are collinear. Three points with position vectors 0, H/3 and H clearly lie on one line.
4. The spacing gives OG : GH = 1 : 2, i.e. the centroid divides OH in 2 : 1 from the orthocentre.
5. Option (a) “Equal” holds only for an equilateral triangle, not in general; (c) and (d) are never guaranteed.
6. Hence the always-true statement is “Collinear”. This is the defining property of the Euler line.
7. The incentre, by contrast, is generally off this line; only in the equilateral case does it join the other three. So “all four centres collinear” would be false in general — a common distractor.
  O at origin:  H = OA + OB + OC
  G = (OA+OB+OC)/3 = H/3
  0, H/3, H are collinear  ->  O, G, H collinear
⚡ JEE Trick “Collinear” is the safe default whenever a question lists the three centres. Only call them “equal” if you are explicitly told the triangle is equilateral; otherwise the Euler line keeps them distinct but aligned.
Answer: (b) Collinear.

Concept If the vertices have rational co-ordinates, any centre built only from rational arithmetic stays rational. The incentre is the exception because it weights the vertices by side lengths, which are square roots and generally irrational.
Formula centroid, circumcentre, orthocentre use only +, −, ×, ÷ of rationals; incentre needs √(side2). Square roots break rationality.

1. Centroid = average of the vertices — sums and a division by 3, so rational. Option (a) is always rational.
2. Circumcentre solves linear equations PA2 = PB2 = PC2, whose coefficients are rational, so its solution is rational. Option (c) is always rational.
3. Orthocentre is the intersection of altitudes, again lines with rational slopes and intercepts, so rational. Option (d) is always rational.
4. Incentre weights by a = BC, b = CA, c = AB — these are √ of rationals and usually irrational.
5. Hence the incentre need not be rational, so option (b) is excluded.
6. The points that are always rational are the centroid, circumcentre and orthocentre: options (a), (c), (d). All three avoid square roots.
7. Example: an equilateral triangle on rational vertices has an irrational incentre (= centroid here, but in general irrational for scalene rational triangles). Side lengths like √2 ruin rationality.

  centroid     : average -> rational      (a)
  circumcentre : linear eqns -> rational   (c)
  orthocentre  : altitude ints -> rational (d)
  incentre     : sqrt sides -> not rational (b out)
