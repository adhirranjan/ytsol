1What is the rotational equivalent of linear momentum? (a) Angular acceleration (b) Torque (c) Angular displacement (d) Angular momentum — (Level-1)Translation–rotation analogyWhat Is Angular Momentum? — Rotational Motion, Class 11🟡 SimilardAngular momentum
Concept Every translational quantity has a rotational partner produced by one fixed substitution: mass m becomes moment of inertia I, linear velocity v becomes angular velocity ω, and force F becomes torque τ. Linear momentum p = mv measures how much straight-line motion a body carries; its rotational partner must measure how much rotation a body carries about an axis. Making the substitution inside p = mv gives L = Iω. Only one option in the list is built out of I and ω.
Formula p = mv. The linear yardstick we are asked to translate. L = r × p = Iω. Angular momentum is literally the moment of momentum, so it inherits p's role.
Solution
1. Fix the target by units: p = mv carries kg m s−1, so its rotational image must carry (kg m²)(s−1) = kg m² s−1.
2. Apply the substitution rule: p = mv → L = Iω = (kg m²)(rad s−1) = kg m² s−1, exactly the target.Radian is dimensionless, so it never disturbs a unit check.
3. Test (a): angular acceleration α = dω/dt is the image of linear acceleration a = dv/dt, not of p.
4. Test (b): torque τ = r × F is the image of force, because τ = dL/dt mirrors F = dp/dt line for line.Whatever sits on the left of d(…)/dt in one column must sit there in the other.
5. Test (c): angular displacement θ is the image of linear displacement s, one integration below velocity.
6. Only (d) survives: L = r × p = Iω, the quantity conserved when τext = 0 just as p is conserved when Fext = 0.Matching conservation laws is the strongest confirmation the pairing is right.

 s   (m)          ->   theta (rad)
 v   (m/s)        ->   w     (rad/s)
 a   (m/s^2)      ->   alpha (rad/s^2)
 m   (kg)         ->   I     (kg m^2)
 p = m v          ->   L = I w

⚡ JEE Trick The bait is (b) torque, because torque is the heavyweight of rotation and students remember it best. But torque is the partner of force: it is what changes angular momentum, never the momentum itself. Pair the equations, not the vocabulary — F ↔ τ and p ↔ L — and the distractors collapse instantly.
Answer: (d) Angular momentum.
2A uniform equilateral triangular lamina of side a and mass m is rotating with angular velocity ω about an axis passing through its centroid and perpendicular to its plane. Find its angular momentum about the axis of rotation. — (Level-2)L = Iω for a triangular laminaEquilateral triangular lamina of side a, mass m — moment of inertia🟡 SimilarL = ma²ω/12
Concept For pure rotation about a fixed axis the angular momentum is L = Iω, so the whole problem reduces to finding I of an equilateral triangular plate about the axis through its centroid perpendicular to its plane. The plate is planar, so the perpendicular-axis theorem applies: Iz = Ix + Iy where x and y are any two perpendicular in-plane axes through the same point. An equilateral triangle has three-fold symmetry about its centroid, which forces every in-plane centroidal axis to carry the same moment of inertia. That symmetry is what makes the answer come out in one line.
Formula Iz = Ix + Iy. Perpendicular-axis theorem — legal only because the lamina is flat. Iin-plane, centroidal = ma²/24. Standard result for an equilateral plate about a median-parallel centroidal axis. L = Iω. Position vector and momentum are perpendicular for every mass element in pure rotation.
Solution
1. By the three-fold symmetry of the equilateral triangle, all in-plane centroidal axes are equivalent: Ix = Iy = I0.A 120° rotation maps the plate onto itself, so no in-plane direction can be special.
2. Perpendicular-axis theorem then gives Iz = Ix + Iy = 2I0.
3. Integrating a uniform equilateral plate of side a about a centroidal in-plane axis gives I0 = ma²/24.
4. Hence Iz = 2 × ma²/24 = ma²/12 about the centroidal axis perpendicular to the plane.
5. Angular momentum: L = Izω = (ma²/12)ω = ma²ω/12, directed along the rotation axis by the right-hand rule.L is parallel to ω here because the axis is a symmetry axis of the body.
6. Sanity check with a = 0.3 m, m = 2 kg, ω = 5 rad/s: L = 2(0.09)(5)/12 = 0.075 kg m²/s — small, as a thin light plate should be.

⚡ JEE Trick The tempting wrong move is to quote ma²/12 for an in-plane axis (that is the rod result) and then double it. Here ma²/12 is already the perpendicular-axis answer. Remember the pair as ma²/24 in-plane and ma²/12 out-of-plane, and note that the equilateral triangle is the rare shape where you never need to know which in-plane axis was meant.
Answer: L = ma²ω/12, along the axis of rotation.
3A particle is projected at t = 0 from a point P with a speed v0 at an angle of 45° to the horizontal. Find the magnitude and the direction of the angular momentum of the particle about the point P at time t = v0/g. — (Level-3)Angular momentum of a projectile vs timeAngular momentum of a projectile as a function of time about the point of projection🟡 SimilarL = −[mv0³/(2√2 g)] k̂ (into the page)
Concept Angular momentum about a fixed point is L = r × p, so we only need the position and velocity of the projectile at the given instant, both measured from P. Gravity supplies a constant torque τ = r × mg about P, which is why L grows with time instead of staying fixed. Because the motion is entirely in the x–y plane, L points along ±z, and the sign tells us whether the particle sweeps counter-clockwise or clockwise about P. It is faster to build a general-t expression once than to plug numbers into r × p twice.
Formula Lz = m(x vy − y vx). The only surviving component of r × p for planar motion. x = v0cosθ·t, y = v0sinθ·t − ½gt². Standard projectile kinematics with P as origin.
Solution
1. Components at time t: x = v0cosθ t, vx = v0cosθ, y = v0sinθ t − ½gt², vy = v0sinθ − gt.
2. Form x vy = v0²sinθcosθ t − g v0cosθ t² and y vx = v0²sinθcosθ t − ½g v0cosθ t².
3. Subtract: x vy − y vx = −½ g v0 t² cosθ, so Lz = −½ m g v0 t² cosθ.The sinθcosθ terms cancel exactly — only the −½gt² drop survives, which is the whole content of the result.
4. Put θ = 45° so cosθ = 1/√2, and t = v0/g so t² = v0²/g².
5. Lz = −½ m g v0 (v0²/g²)(1/√2) = −m v0³/(2√2 g), i.e. magnitude mv0³/(2√2 g).
6. The minus sign means L is along −k̂, i.e. into the page (clockwise sweep), since the particle drifts to the right while falling below the launch line.Cross-check: τ = r × (−mg ĵ) also points along −k̂ for x > 0, so L must be growing negative.
7. With m = 1 kg, v0 = 10 m/s, g = 10: L = −1000/(2√2 × 10) = −35.4 kg m²/s.t = v0/g = 1 s here, well past the apex time v0sin45°/g = 0.71 s, so the particle is already descending.

4A particle of mass m starts moving from point (0, d) with a constant velocity u î. Find out its angular momentum about the origin at this moment. What will be the answer at a later time? — (Level-4)L of a particle in straight-line motionAngular momentum of a particle about the origin — worked example🟡 SimilarL = −m d u k̂ — unchanged at all later times
Concept Angular momentum about a point is L = r × p, and for a straight-line path it collapses to L = m v r⊥, where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance from the reference point to the line of action of the velocity. A particle moving with constant velocity has no net force on it, so the torque about the origin is zero and L cannot change. Geometrically, the particle slides along a fixed line whose distance from O never changes, so m v r⊥ is frozen. Both arguments must agree, and they do.
Formula Lz = m(x vy − y vx). Planar reduction of r × p. dL/dt = τ = r × F. With F = 0 for constant velocity, L is a constant of the motion.
Solution
1. At the start r = (0, d) and v = (u, 0), so p = (mu, 0).
2. Lz = m(x vy − y vx) = m(0·0 − d·u) = −m d u, so L = −m d u k̂.Negative z means the particle sweeps clockwise as seen from the +z side.
3. At a later time t the particle is at (ut, d) with the same velocity (u, 0).
4. Recompute: Lz = m(ut · 0 − d · u) = −m d u — identical, the x-drift contributes nothing.x multiplies vy, which is zero, so the growing coordinate is invisible to L.
5. Confirm dynamically: constant velocity ⇒ F = 0 ⇒ τ = r × F = 0 ⇒ dL/dt = 0.
6. Confirm geometrically: L = m u r⊥ with r⊥ = d, the fixed distance from O to the line y = d.Same number by three routes — components, torque, and perpendicular distance.
7. Numerically with m = 2 kg, u = 3 m/s, d = 4 m: |L| = 24 kg m²/s at every instant.

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is assuming L must change because r is growing. Only the component of r perpendicular to v counts, and that component is the fixed offset d. The moment you see "constant velocity", jump straight to L = m v r⊥ with r⊥ read off the geometry — and remember it is zero for any reference point lying on the line itself.

5A particle of mass ‘m’ is projected on horizontal ground with an initial velocity of u making an angle θ with the horizontal. Find out the angular momentum of the particle about the point of projection when (i) it just starts its motion, (ii) it is at the highest point of its path, (iii) it just strikes the ground. — (Level-5)Projectile angular momentum at three instantsAngular momentum of a projectile thrown from ground, at the highest point🟡 Similar(i) 0  (ii) mu³sin²θcosθ/(2g)  (iii) 2mu³sin²θcosθ/g
Concept About the launch point O, angular momentum is L = m(r × v), and the fastest route is L = m v r⊥ where r⊥ is the perpendicular distance from O to the line of the velocity at that instant. At launch the velocity line passes through O itself, so L must vanish. Gravity exerts a torque mg·x about O that grows as the particle drifts horizontally, so L builds up steadily and is largest at impact. All three answers come from the same picture with different (v, r⊥) pairs.
Formula L = m v r⊥. r × v reduces to speed times the moment arm of the velocity line. H = u²sin²θ/(2g), R = u²sin2θ/g. Standard projectile apex height and range.
Solution
1. (i) At t = 0 the particle is at O, so r = 0 and L = m(0 × v) = 0.Equivalently, the velocity line passes through the reference point, so r⊥ = 0.
2. (ii) At the apex the velocity is horizontal with magnitude v = u cosθ, and the moment arm is the height H = u²sin²θ/(2g).
3. So L = m(u cosθ)(u²sin²θ/2g) = mu³sin²θcosθ/(2g), directed into the page.A horizontal velocity at height H has moment arm exactly H about a ground-level origin.
4. (iii) At impact the particle is at x = R = u²sin2θ/g on the ground, with velocity u at angle −θ; the vertical component u sinθ is the only one with a moment arm about O.
5. L = m R (u sinθ) = m(u²·2sinθcosθ/g)(u sinθ) = 2mu³sin²θcosθ/g.The horizontal component acts along the ground line through O, so it contributes zero.
6. Ratio check: Limpact/Lapex = 4 exactly, consistent with L ∝ t² from Lz = −½mgu t²cosθ and timpact = 2tapex.A doubling of time must quadruple L — a free correctness test.
7. Numbers (m = 1, u = 20, θ = 60°, g = 10): apex L = 8000(0.75)(0.5)/20 = 150, impact L = 600 kg m²/s.

       L = m u cos t . u^2 sin^2 t /(2g)

⚡ JEE Trick At impact many students use the full speed u with the range R and get twice the right answer. Only the component of v perpendicular to r contributes — here the vertical part u sinθ, because the horizontal part is aimed along OR. Choosing "moment arm of v" or "perpendicular component of v" is a free choice, but you must not mix the two.
Answer: (i) 0, (ii) mu³sin²θcosθ/(2g), (iii) 2mu³sin²θcosθ/g — all directed into the plane of motion.

1A disk of mass 4 kg and radius 1 m is rotating about an axis passing through its centre and perpendicular to the plane of disc with angular velocity 2 rad/s. Find its angular momentum (in kg-m²/s). — (Level-1)L = Iω for a discAngular Momentum of a Rotating Body — Rotational Motion 11, JEE/NEET🟡 Similar4 kg m²/s
Concept A rigid body turning about a fixed axis has every mass element moving in a circle whose plane is perpendicular to that axis, so each element's position vector and momentum are mutually perpendicular. Summing mirivi = miri²ω over the body turns the definition L = Σr × p into the compact L = Iω. All the geometry of the body is packed into I, so the only work left is quoting the right standard moment of inertia. For a uniform disc about its central perpendicular axis that is I = ½MR².
Formula Idisc = ½MR². Mass is spread from 0 to R, so the disc stores half as much inertia as a ring of the same M and R. L = Iω. Valid because the axis here is a symmetry axis, making L parallel to ω.
Solution
1. Read off the data: M = 4 kg, R = 1 m, ω = 2 rad/s, axis = central and perpendicular to the disc.
2. Moment of inertia: I = ½MR² = ½(4)(1)² = 2 kg m².R = 1 m makes R² disappear, so the arithmetic is just half the mass.
3. Angular momentum: L = Iω = (2)(2) = 4 kg m²/s, along the rotation axis.
4. Direction: curl the right hand along the sense of rotation; the thumb gives L, perpendicular to the disc's plane.
5. Unit check: (kg m²)(rad s−1) = kg m² s−1, matching the units asked for.Radian is dimensionless, so it silently drops out of every L = Iω calculation.
6. Cross-check by energy: K = ½Iω² = ½(2)(4) = 4 J, and L = 2K/ω = 8/2 = 4 kg m²/s — same answer.L = 2K/ω is a useful back-door whenever a question mixes energy and momentum.

 L = I w          = (2)(2)       = 4 kg m^2/s

⚡ JEE Trick The single most common slip is using I = MR² (the ring) instead of ½MR², which doubles the answer to 8. A disc is a stack of rings of every radius from 0 to R, so its inertia must be less than the ring's — the factor ½ is not decoration. When R = 1 m the numbers look identical, which is exactly why the setter chose R = 1.
Answer: 4 kg m²/s.
2A particle with position vector r has a linear momentum p. Which of the following statements is true in respect of its angular momentum L about the origin? (a) L acts along p (b) L acts along r (c) L is maximum when p and r are parallel (d) L is maximum when p is perpendicular to r — (Level-1)Direction and magnitude of r × pWhat Is Angular Momentum? — Rotational Motion, Class 11🟡 SimilardL is maximum when p is perpendicular to r
Concept Angular momentum is defined by a cross product, L = r × p, and a cross product has two hard-wired properties. Its direction is perpendicular to both input vectors, so L can never lie along r or along p. Its magnitude is |r||p|sinθ, which is a pure sine curve in the angle between them: zero when they are parallel or antiparallel, largest when they are at 90°. Every option in this question is a test of one of those two properties.
Formula L = r × p. Definition; the right-hand rule fixes the direction. |L| = r p sinθ. Magnitude of a cross product, θ being the angle between r and p.
Solution
1. Direction rule: L = r × p is normal to the plane containing r and p, hence L ⊥ r and L ⊥ p simultaneously.
2. Test (a): if L acted along p then L·p ≠ 0, but L·p = (r × p)·p = 0 always. Rejected.A cross product is orthogonal to each factor — a one-line disproof, no geometry needed.
3. Test (b): the same dot-product argument gives L·r = 0, so L cannot act along r either. Rejected.
4. Test (c): parallel means θ = 0°, so |L| = r p sin0° = 0 — that is the minimum, not the maximum. Rejected.Physically, a particle heading straight at (or away from) the origin sweeps out no area, so it carries no angular momentum about it.
5. Test (d): perpendicular means θ = 90°, giving |L| = r p sin90° = r p, the largest value the product can reach. Accepted.
6. Consistency check with circular motion: there r ⊥ v at every instant, and indeed L = mvr takes its maximal form — matching option (d).Uniform circular motion is the textbook case of the maximum, which is why L = mvr is quoted without a sine.

3The moment of momentum is called (a) Couple (b) Torque (c) Impulse (d) Angular momentum — (Level-1)Naming the moment of a vectorAngular Momentum — Rotational Motion 08, Class 11/NEET/JEE🟡 SimilardAngular momentum
Concept In mechanics the phrase "moment of X about a point" always means the cross product r × X, where r runs from the reference point to the point of application. So "moment of force" is r × F = torque, and "moment of momentum" must be r × p. That cross product is precisely the definition of angular momentum. The question is pure vocabulary, but the vocabulary is systematic once you know the rule.
Formula moment of X = r × X. The universal definition of a moment about a point. L = r × p. Substituting X = p gives angular momentum by name.
Solution
1. Apply the rule to X = p (linear momentum): moment of momentum = r × p.
2. Compare with the chapter's definition L = r × mv = r × p — identical, so the answer is angular momentum.The older textbook name for L is literally "moment of momentum"; the two are the same quantity.
3. Test (b) torque: that is the moment of force, τ = r × F, with units N m, not kg m²/s. Rejected.
4. Test (a) couple: a couple is a pair of equal, opposite, non-collinear forces — a special way of producing torque, again a force concept. Rejected.A couple's moment is the same about every point, which already marks it as a torque-type object.
5. Test (c) impulse: J = ∫F dt = Δp, a momentum change, not a moment of anything. Rejected.The moment of impulse would be angular impulse — a different quantity again, equal to ΔL.
6. Only (d) has units kg m² s−1 = (m)(kg m s−1), exactly length × momentum. Answer (d).

Concept For a mass circling a fixed point, the radius vector r and the momentum p both lie in the plane of the orbit — r along the radius, p along the tangent. Their cross product L = r × p is therefore normal to that plane by the very definition of a cross product. The right-hand rule fixes which of the two normal directions it takes: curl the fingers along the sense of rotation and the thumb points along L. So L is an axial vector standing on the orbit like an axle, not a vector lying in the orbit.
Formula L = r × p. Both factors lie in the plane, so the product is perpendicular to it. |L| = mvr sin90° = mvr. For circular motion r ⊥ v, so the sine is 1.

3. Right-hand rule picks the sign: anticlockwise rotation gives L out of the plane, clockwise gives L into it.
4. Test (c) radius: L·r = (r × p)·r = 0, so L is orthogonal to the radius, never along it. Rejected.
5. Test (d) tangent: likewise L·p = 0, so L is orthogonal to the tangential momentum. Rejected.Options (c) and (d) both fail on the same one-line dot-product argument.

Concept The rod is light, so all the inertia is in the two end balls, and the axis is the perpendicular bisector, so each ball sits at radius d/2 — not d. That halving is the entire difficulty: it enters squared, so each ball contributes m(d/2)² = md²/4 and not md². The system value is then simply twice one ball's value, because moments of inertia about a common axis add. The question deliberately offers the individual and the system answers in the same list so that a factor-of-two slip lands on a plausible-looking option instead of on nothing.
Formula Ii = miri² with r = d/2. Perpendicular bisector ⇒ each end is half the rod length from the axis. L = Iω, Isystem = ΣIi. Rigid structure, one shared ω; inertias about the same axis add.
Solution
1. Geometry: the axis passes through the midpoint of the rod, perpendicular to it, so rA = rB = d/2.Using r = d would quadruple every result — the single most common error in this question.
2. One ball: I1 = m(d/2)² = md²/4, so its angular momentum about the axis is L1 = I1ω = ¼mωd². That makes (a) TRUE and (b) false.
3. System: I = IA + IB = md²/4 + md²/4 = md²/2.
4. Hence Lsystem = Iω = ½mωd², so (c) is TRUE and (d) false.Both balls circulate the same way, so their angular momenta add rather than cancel — unlike the two-particle question in Level-4, where the senses opposed.
5. Consistency check by velocity: each ball moves at v = ωd/2, so its L = mvr = m(ωd/2)(d/2) = mωd²/4 — the same as step 2 by a route that never mentions I.
6. Reduced-mass check: this is the equal-mass case of the two-body rotor, μ = m/2, so I = μd² = md²/2 — agreeing with step 3 and tying this question to Level-5 question 2 of the same lecture.Whenever two formulas from different questions in the same lecture agree on a number, both are almost certainly right.
7. So the correct set is (a) and (c), matching the key's "ac"; (b) and (d) are each exactly twice the true value, the signature of using r = d somewhere.

   |<- d/2 ->|<- d/2 ->|   axis = perp. bisector
 one ball: I1 = m (d/2)^2 = m d^2 / 4
           L1 = I1 w = (1/4) m w d^2      -> (a)
 system  : I  = 2 * m d^2/4 = m d^2 / 2
           L  = I w = (1/2) m w d^2       -> (c)

⚡ JEE Trick Read the axis before writing r: "perpendicular bisector" means d/2 per ball, and because r is squared, mistaking it for d inflates every answer fourfold. Then keep individual and system values separate — the option list contains both ¼ and ½ precisely so that answering the wrong one still "finds" an option. Sanity-check with the reduced mass μ = m/2, which reproduces I = md²/2 in one line.
Answer: (a) and (c).
5Multiple Correct Answer Question. The angular momentum of a disc about the Z-axis in Fig. (i) is ℓ1 and in Fig. (ii) is ℓ2. In Fig. (i) the disc (mass M, radius R) spins about the Z-axis through its own centre; in Fig. (ii) the same disc is carried at a distance d from the Z-axis and revolves about it with the same ω. (a) ℓ1 = (MR²ω/2)k̂ (b) ℓ2 = M(R²/2 + d²)ωk̂ (c) ℓ1 = (MR²ω/4)k̂ (d) ℓ2 = M(R²/2 + d²/2)ωk̂. — (Level-6)Parallel-axis theorem applied to L = IωParallel Axis Theorem & Moment of Inertia – Physics Practice Problems🟡 Similara, bℓ1 = MR²ω/2, ℓ2 = M(R²/2 + d²)ω
Concept Both figures show the same disc turning at the same ω about the same vertical Z-axis; only the location of that axis relative to the disc differs. So the whole question is about which moment of inertia goes into L = Iω. In Fig. (i) the axis is the disc's own symmetry axis and I = MR²/2. In Fig. (ii) the axis is parallel to that one but displaced by d, so the parallel-axis theorem adds Md² — with no factor of a half, because the theorem shifts by the full square of the separation. Both angular momenta point along +k̂, the direction of ω.
Formula Idisc,cm = MR²/2. Uniform disc about the axis through its centre perpendicular to its plane. I = Icm + Md². Parallel-axis theorem — the shift term is Md², never Md²/2. L = Iω. Fixed axis, rigid body, so L is along ω.
Solution
1. Figure (i): the Z-axis is the disc's own central perpendicular axis, so I1 = MR²/2.
2. Hence ℓ1 = I1ω = (MR²ω/2)k̂, which is option (a); option (c) with MR²/4 is the value about a diameter, not about the perpendicular axis.MR²/4 is a real disc formula — for an in-plane diameter — which is exactly why it is a convincing distractor.
3. Figure (ii): the Z-axis is parallel to the disc's own axis but displaced by d, so I2 = Icm + Md² = MR²/2 + Md² = M(R²/2 + d²).
4. Hence ℓ2 = I2ω = M(R²/2 + d²)ωk̂, which is option (b); option (d) halves the d² term and is simply the parallel-axis theorem misremembered.
5. Physical reading of the two terms: MR²ω/2 is the disc's spin about its own centre and Md²ω is the orbital contribution of the centre of mass going round the Z-axis at radius d — the same spin-plus-orbital split used in the rolling problems of Level-5.Here the disc is rigidly attached, so it spins once per revolution and the two contributions share one ω.
6. Limit checks: d → 0 turns Fig. (ii) back into Fig. (i), and ℓ2 → MR²ω/2 correctly; d » R makes the disc a point mass on a long arm and ℓ2 → Md²ω, also correct.
7. So the correct set is (a) and (b), matching the key's "ab".

Equally, keep the disc's two formulas apart: MR²/2 about the perpendicular central axis and MR²/4 about a diameter; option (c) is that second one smuggled into the wrong figure. Test any candidate answer at d = 0 — it must collapse back to ℓ1.
Answer: (a) and (b).
