1A uniform rod of length 2a is placed horizontally on the edge of a table. Initially the centre of mass of the rod is at a distance a/3 from the edge. The rod is released from rest. If the rod slips after it has turned through an angle θ, find the coefficient of friction between the rod and the table. — (Level-7)Rotation about a table edge · slipping conditionUniform rod overhanging a rough table edge starts to rotate — friction🟡 Similarμ = 2 tanθ
Concept Until it slips the rod turns about the table edge, a pivot a fixed distance d = a/3 from the centre of mass. The edge is a line contact, so N is perpendicular to the rod and the friction f runs along it. Slipping starts when the rod needs f > μN, so both reaction components are needed at angle θ; they follow from the centre of mass having a centripetal acceleration ω²d towards the edge and a tangential acceleration αd perpendicular to the rod.
Formula Iedge = ML²/12 + Md², α = Mgd cosθ/I, ω² = 2∫α dθ. α is not constant, so ω² must be built by integrating α over θ, not by a suvat formula.
Solution
1. Geometry: L = 2a, d = a/3, so Iedge = M(2a)²/12 + M(a/3)² = Ma²/3 + Ma²/9 = 4Ma²/9.
2. Torque at angle θ below horizontal: α = Mg(a/3)cosθ ÷ (4Ma²/9) = (3g/4a)cosθ.
3. ω² = 2∫0θ(3g/4a)cosθ dθ = (3g/2a)sinθ. Energy gives the same thing: Mg d sinθ = ½Iω².
4. Along the rod (radial): Mg sinθ − f = −Mω²d, so f = Mg sinθ + M(a/3)(3g/2a)sinθ = (3/2)Mg sinθ.
5. Perpendicular to the rod: Mg cosθ − N = Mαd = M(3g/4a)cosθ(a/3) = ¼Mg cosθ, so N = (3/4)Mg cosθ. N is only 3/4 of the naive mg cosθ because the centre of mass is accelerating away from the edge tangentially.
6. Slipping begins when f = μN: μ = (3/2)Mg sinθ ÷ (3/4)Mg cosθ = 2 tanθ. The mass, the length and g all cancel — the answer depends on the turn angle alone.
 quantity        expression              value at angle th
 --------------  ----------------------  -----------------
 I about edge    4 M a^2 / 9             --
 alpha           (3g/4a) cos th          --
 omega^2         (3g/2a) sin th          --
 f  (along rod)  Mg sin th + M w^2 d     1.5 Mg sin th

⚡ JEE Trick The trap is writing N = Mg cosθ and f = Mg sinθ, which gives the useless static answer μ = tanθ. The centripetal term adds to f while the tangential term subtracts from N — that pair of corrections is exactly why the true μ is twice the static-looking one.
Answer: μ = 2 tanθ.
2The uniform slender rod AC has a mass m and is pinned at B, with AB = L/3 and BC = 2L/3. If it is released from rest when θ = 0° (rod horizontal), determine the magnitude of the reactive force exerted on it by pin B when θ = 90°. — (Level-7)Rod swinging about an off-centre pin · pin reactionFind the hinge force on a rod just after release and later positions🟡 SimilarB = 1.5 mg
Concept The pin fixes one point, so the rod is in pure rotation about B and its centre of mass G moves on a circle of radius d = BG. At θ = 90° the rod is vertical, G hangs directly below B, and the weight has zero moment arm about B — so α = 0 there and the whole acceleration of G is centripetal, pointing straight up towards B. Newton's second law on the centre of mass then gives the pin force in one line, provided ω² is first obtained from energy.
Formula IB = mL²/12 + md², ½IBω² = mgd, B − mg = mω²d. Energy is legitimate because the pin does no work and the rod is rigid.
Solution
1. Locate G relative to B: G is at L/2 from A, B is at L/3 from A, so d = L/2 − L/3 = L/6.
2. IB = mL²/12 + m(L/6)² = mL²/12 + mL²/36 = mL²/9.
3. From horizontal to vertical G falls by d = L/6, so ½(mL²/9)ω² = mg(L/6) ⇒ ω² = 3g/L. The drop is d, not L/2 — the rod pivots about B, not about its own end.
4. At θ = 90° the line BG is vertical, so the moment of mg about B is zero and α = 0; G has no tangential acceleration.
5. Centripetal acceleration of G: an = ω²d = (3g/L)(L/6) = g/2, directed upward along GB. Upward, because the centre of the circular path (the pin) is above G at this instant.
6. Vertical Newton on G: B − mg = m(g/2) ⇒ B = 1.5 mg = (3/2)mg, purely vertical since α = 0 kills any horizontal component. Half the mg excess is the price of turning the corner; at θ = 0 the same rod gives B < mg instead.
 stage                 quantity      value
 --------------------  ------------  --------------

 energy drop           m g d         m g L/6

⚡ JEE Trick Two habits wreck this one: using I = mL²/3 (an end pivot) instead of the parallel-axis value about B, and forgetting that α vanishes at the vertical so the reaction is purely radial. The numbers L and m cancel completely, but the ratio does depend on where B sits: a pin at the very end would give ω²d = 3g/2 and B = 2.5 mg instead.
Answer: B = 1.5 mg, directed vertically upward along the rod.
3A car of mass m travelling at speed v moves on a horizontal track. The centre of mass of the car describes a circle of radius r. If 2a is the separation of the inner and outer wheels and h is the height of the centre of mass above the ground, show that the limiting speed beyond which the car will overturn is given by v² = gra/h. — (Level-7)Overturning of a vehicle · toppling about the outer wheelsOverturning of a vehicle on a curved track — JEE / NEET🟢 Exactv² = gra/h ⚠
Concept On a flat track the centripetal force mv²/r comes entirely from the friction f1 + f2 at the tyres, which acts at ground level while the weight acts at height h. That offset creates a couple that tries to tip the car outward, and the load transfers from the inner wheels to the outer ones as v grows. Overturning is imminent at exactly the speed where the inner normal reaction N1 falls to zero; beyond it the inner wheels leave the road.
Formula ΣFy = 0, ΣFx = mv²/r, Στ about the outer contact = 0. Torques are taken in the non-inertial car frame, where the centrifugal force mv²/r acts outward at the centre of mass.
Solution
1. Vertical: N1 + N2 = mg, with N1 the inner and N2 the outer wheel reaction.
2. Horizontal: f1 + f2 = mv²/r, applied at the ground, a height h below the centre of mass G.
3. Moments about G (half-track a on each side): N1a − N2a + (f1+f2)h = 0 ⇒ N2 − N1 = mv²h/(ra). Load transfer grows as v²: the outer wheel is pressed harder, the inner one relieved.
4. Solving with step 1: N1 = ½mg − mv²h/(2ra) and N2 = ½mg + mv²h/(2ra).
5. Tipping is imminent when N1 = 0: ½mg = mv²h/(2ra) ⇒ mga = mv²h/r. The same line drops out of taking moments directly about the outer contact, where N2 and f2 have no arm.
6. Hence v² = gra/h: restoring moment mg·a balanced by overturning moment (mv²/r)·h. A wide, low car (large a, small h) is hard to roll; a tall narrow one topples early — the physical content of the result.
7. Key check: the printed key gives μ < a/h, which is the condition that skidding (v² = μgr) beats tipping (v² = gra/h), not the required speed. Flagged ⚠.
  N1              N2         G at height h
   |               |         weight mg down
   |<---- a ---->|<---- a ---->|
  f1              f2   ground level, sum = m v^2 / r
  tip condition: N1 = 0
  m g a = (m v^2 / r) h   ->   v^2 = g r a / h
⚡ JEE Trick Overturning is a torque condition, skidding is a force condition — never mix them. Whichever gives the smaller v² happens first: the car skids first if μgr < gra/h, i.e. μ < a/h, and rolls first if μ > a/h. Also note 2a is the full track width, so the arm from G to either wheel is a, not 2a; halving that is the single commonest slip here.
Answer: v² = gra/h (proved). The key prints μ < a/h, which answers a different question. ⚠
4Steel tape is being wound onto a spool which rotates with a constant angular velocity ω0. Denoting by r the radius of the spool and tape at any given time and by b the thickness of the tape, derive an expression for the acceleration of the tape as it approaches the spool. — (Level-7)Winding spool · slowly growing radius——a = bω0²/2π
Concept The incoming tape is a straight, inextensible strip, so its speed equals the rim speed of the roll, v = ω0r. With ω0 held constant, the only way v can change is through r — and r does grow, because every completed turn lays down one more layer of thickness b. So the tape's acceleration is a purely kinematic consequence of the roll fattening, not of any angular acceleration.
Formula v = ω0r, dr/dθ = b/2π, a = dv/dt = ω0(dr/dt). One revolution, Δθ = 2π, raises the radius by exactly one tape thickness b, so dr/dθ is the constant b/2π.
Solution
1. Tape speed: since the tape does not slip on the roll, v = ω0r with ω0 constant.
2. Differentiate: a = dv/dt = ω0 dr/dt, because dω0/dt = 0.
3. Growth law of the radius: r increases by b per turn, so r = r0 + (b/2π)θ and dr/dθ = b/2π. Treating the spiral as a set of concentric layers is exact to first order in b/r, which is all that is asked.
4. Chain rule: dr/dt = (dr/dθ)(dθ/dt) = (b/2π)ω0.
5. Substitute into step 2: a = ω0(b/2π)ω0 = bω0²/2π, directed along the tape, in the direction of its motion.
6. Sanity numbers: b = 0.5 mm, ω0 = 20 rad/s gives a = (5×10−4)(400)/6.283 = 0.032 m/s² — small, steady and constant. Constant, because b and ω0 are both constant; r drops out entirely.
 one turn of the roll:   theta -> theta + 2 pi
 radius grows:           r     -> r + b
 so                      dr/d(theta) = b / (2 pi)
 tape speed              v  = w0 r
 tape acceleration       a  = w0 dr/dt
                            = w0 (b/2pi) w0
                            = b w0^2 / (2 pi)
⚡ JEE Trick Nothing here is rotational dynamics — no torque, no moment of inertia, no mass. The single idea is that a constant ω with a growing radius still means a changing linear speed. Students who write a = αr get zero because α = 0; students who use b per radian instead of b per revolution lose the factor 2π, which is the whole answer.
Answer: a = bω0²/2π, along the tape.
5Two blocks are connected by a string that passes over a pulley of radius R and moment of inertia I. The block of mass m1 slides on a frictionless, horizontal surface; the block of mass m2 is suspended from the string. Find the acceleration 'a' of the blocks and the tensions T1 and T2, assuming that the string does not slip on the pulley. — (Level-7)Atwood system with a massive pulleyTwo blocks over a pulley of radius R and inertia I — acceleration and both tensions🟢 Exacta = m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²)
Concept A pulley with real inertia must be given angular momentum, and the only way to spin it is with unequal string tensions on its two sides. So T1 ≠ T2 here, unlike the ideal massless-pulley case. Three free bodies are needed — the sliding block, the hanging block and the pulley — tied together by the no-slip condition a = αR, which converts the pulley's rotational equation into a linear one.
Formula T1 = m1a, m2g − T2 = m2a, (T2 − T1)R = Iα = Ia/R. The last line is τ = Iα about the pulley axle, with a = αR because the string does not slip.
Solution
1. Horizontal block (frictionless table, only the string pulls it): T1 = m1a.
2. Hanging block: m2g − T2 = m2a, so T2 = m2(g − a).
3. Pulley: (T2 − T1)R = I(a/R) ⇒ T2 − T1 = Ia/R². I/R² behaves exactly like an extra block of mass sitting in the chain — the standard "effective mass" of a pulley.
4. Add the three: m2g = a(m1 + m2 + I/R²) ⇒ a = m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²).
5. Back-substitute: T1 = m1m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²) and T2 = (m1+I/R²)m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²). T2 exceeds T1 by exactly Ia/R², the torque-supplying difference.
6. Limits: I → 0 restores a = m2g/(m1+m2) with T1 = T2; I → ∞ gives a → 0 and T2 → m2g, the pulley locked. Checking both limits costs one line and catches sign or placement errors instantly.
 body        equation                     unknowns
 ----------  ---------------------------  ---------
 m1 (table)  T1 = m1 a                    T1, a
 m2 (hang)   m2 g - T2 = m2 a             T2, a
 pulley      (T2 - T1) R = I a / R        T1,T2,a
 add all ->  m2 g = a (m1 + m2 + I/R^2)
 a  = m2 g / (m1 + m2 + I/R^2)
⚡ JEE Trick Setting T1 = T2 is the one fatal error — with a real I the tensions must differ or the pulley would never spin up. The fast route is to write the "effective mass" m1 + m2 + I/R² straight down and treat the whole loop as one 1-D chain; only afterwards do you unpick the individual tensions.
Answer: a = m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²), T1 = m1m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²), T2 = (m1+I/R²)m2g/(m1+m2+I/R²).
6A circular hole of radius r/2 is cut from a circular disc of radius r. The disc lies in the xy-plane and the hole is cut so that its rim passes through the centre of the disc. Determine the moment of inertia of the remaining body about an axis passing through the centre of the original disc and perpendicular to the plane of the disc, in terms of the mass m of the remaining body. — (Level-7)Negative-mass method · disc with an off-centre holeCircular hole of radius R/2 cut from a disc — moment of inertia of the remainder🟢 ExactI = (13/24)mr²
Concept Moment of inertia is additive, so a body with a hole is a full disc plus a disc of negative mass filling the hole. Both pieces must be referred to the same axis — here the centre O of the original disc — and since the hole is off-centre by r/2, its contribution needs the parallel-axis theorem. The final answer is asked in terms of the mass that actually remains, so a mass bookkeeping step is unavoidable.
Formula Idisc = ½MR², I = Icm + Md², mass ∝ area so Mhole = M(r/2)²/r² = M/4. Uniform thickness and density make the mass scale as the square of the radius, not the radius.
Solution
1. Let the original full disc have mass M and radius r. The hole has radius r/2, hence mass M/4, and its centre sits at d = r/2 from O (its rim passes through O).
2. Full disc about O: Ifull = ½Mr².
3. Hole about O: Ihole = ½(M/4)(r/2)² + (M/4)(r/2)² = Mr²/32 + Mr²/16 = 3Mr²/32. Two terms: its own ½M'R'² plus the shift M'd²; dropping the second is the classic error.
4. Subtract: Irem = ½Mr² − 3Mr²/32 = (16 − 3)Mr²/32 = 13Mr²/32.
5. Convert to the remaining mass m = M − M/4 = 3M/4, so M = 4m/3.
6. Irem = (13/32)(4m/3)r² = 52mr²/96 = (13/24)mr² ≈ 0.542 mr². Sanity: 13/24 sits between ½ (uniform disc) and 1 (ring), which is where a holed disc must land.
 piece       mass own I about cm    shift M d^2  I about O
 ----------- ---- ----------------- ------------ -----------
 full disc   M    (1/2) M r^2       0            16 M r^2/32
 hole (-ve)  M/4  (1/2)(M/4)(r/2)^2 (M/4)(r/2)^2 3 M r^2/32
 remaining   3M/4                                13 M r^2/32
 with M = 4m/3  ->  I = 13 m r^2 / 24
⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit in series. First, the hole's mass is M/4, not M/2 — area goes as radius squared. Second, the question wants the answer per unit of remaining mass, so 13Mr²/32 must be rescaled by M = 4m/3; a student who stops at 13/32 has done all the physics and still gets it wrong. If the axis were instead through the hole's centre, only the parallel-axis distances change, not the method.
Answer: I = (13/24)mr², with m the mass of the remaining body.
7A spherical ball of mass M and radius R is projected along a rough horizontal surface so that initially (t = 0) it slides with a linear speed v0 but does not rotate. As it slides, it begins to spin and eventually rolls without slipping. How long does it take to begin rolling without slipping? — (Level-7)Sliding → rolling transition · kinetic friction[JEE Adv] Find the time elapsed before the sphere starts rolling🟢 Exactt0 = 2v0/(7μkg)
Concept While the ball skids, its contact point genuinely slides forward, so kinetic friction μkMg acts backwards and is constant. That one force does two jobs at once: it decelerates the centre of mass and it torques the ball up to speed. Rolling begins the moment the two curves v(t) and ω(t)R meet; after that the contact point is momentarily at rest, kinetic friction switches off, and the ball rolls on forever at constant speed on a level floor.
Formula v = v0 − μkgt, α = μkMgR/I with I = (2/5)MR², rolling when v = ωR. Friction is kinetic, so its magnitude is fixed at μkN — both equations have constant acceleration.
Solution
1. Translation: friction μkMg backwards gives a = −μkg, so v(t) = v0 − μkgt.
2. Rotation about the centre: α = μkMgR ÷ (2/5)MR² = 5μkg/(2R), so ω(t) = 5μkgt/(2R) starting from ω = 0.
3. Rolling condition at t = t0: v0 − μkgt0 = ωR = 5μkgt0/2. Only the contact point's velocity has to vanish, not the ball's — rolling is a relative-slip condition.
4. Collect: v0 = μkgt0(1 + 5/2) = (7/2)μkgt0 ⇒ t0 = 2v0/(7μkg).
5. Rolling speed then: v = v0 − μkg(2v0/7μkg) = 5v0/7, independent of μk. Angular momentum about a point on the floor is conserved, which forces the 5/7 without any friction detail.
6. Distance covered before rolling: s = (v0² − v²)/(2μkg) = 12v0²/(49μkg); energy lost = (1/10)Mv0². A rough floor with larger μk shortens both the time and the distance, but never changes the final 5v0/7.
 t          v(t)                omega(t) R
 ---------  ------------------  -------------------

⚡ JEE Trick Do not set v = 0; the ball never stops, it just stops slipping. The 2/7 and 5/7 are worth memorising for a solid sphere (a disc gives 1/3 and 2/3, a ring ½ and ½), because the same skid-to-roll question is recycled endlessly. Fastest check of all: L about any floor point is conserved, Mv0R = (7/5)MvR, giving v = 5v0/7 in one line.
Answer: t0 = 2v0/(7μkg), at which point the ball rolls at 5v0/7.
8A gyroscope is spinning at angular velocity ω0. The power is shut off, and the gyroscope begins to slow down with its angular velocity described by ω = ω0e−t/T, with T a constant. (i) How long does it take for the gyroscope to reach half its initial angular velocity? (ii) What is the gyroscope's initial angular acceleration? (iii) What is the gyroscope's average acceleration between the time the power is cut off and the instant it reaches half speed? (iv) Through how many revolutions does the gyroscope turn during this time? — (Level-7)Exponential spin-down · instantaneous vs average αGyroscope spinning down as ω = ω₀e⁻ᵗᵀᴛ — full solution🟢 Exact(i) t = T ln2  (ii) −ω0/T  (iii) −ω0/(2T ln2)  (iv) θ = ω0T/2 rad ⚠
Concept An exponential spin-down is what you get when the retarding torque is proportional to ω itself (viscous bearing drag), so the rotor never formally stops — it only halves, halves again, and so on. Because ω(t) is given explicitly, every part is calculus on one function: differentiate for angular acceleration, integrate for angle turned. The trap is that "initial" acceleration and "average" acceleration are different objects — one is a derivative at a point, the other a chord slope over an interval.
Formula ω = ω0e−t/T, α = dω/dt, αav = Δω/Δt, θ = ∫ω dt. T is the time constant, not a period: it is the time in which ω falls by the factor e, so the half-time T ln2 ≈ 0.693T is shorter than T.
Solution
1. Half speed: ω0/2 = ω0e−t/T ⇒ et/T = 2 ⇒ tf = T ln2 ≈ 0.693 T.
2. Differentiate: α(t) = dω/dt = −(ω0/T)e−t/T, so at t = 0, α0 = −ω0/T (negative = retarding).
3. Average over the interval: αav = (ω0/2 − ω0)/(T ln2) = −ω0/(2T ln2) ≈ −0.72 ω0/T. Only 72% of the initial value, because α itself decays exponentially — the rotor loses speed fastest at the very start.
4. Angle turned: θ = ∫0tfω0e−t/Tdt = ω0T(1 − e−tf/T) = ω0T(1 − ½) = ω0T/2 radians.
5. Convert to turns: N = θ/2π = ω0T/(4π). The key stops at ω0T/2, which is the angle in radians, not the revolution count the question asks for — hence the ⚠. Same number, wrong unit: a factor 2π separates them.
6. Check the total: letting t→∞, θ∞ = ω0T, so the rotor completes exactly half of all the turning it will ever do by the time it has lost half its speed. A neat consequence of θ and ω sharing the same exponential.

 ------------------  --------------------  --------------

⚡ JEE Trick Two traps sit side by side. First, αav is not the mean of the endpoint accelerations, and it is not α0; it is the chord slope Δω/Δt, which here comes out 0.72ω0/T. Second, "how many revolutions" always means divide the integrated angle by 2π — examiners load this question precisely because candidates hand in radians. Remember e−t/T integrates to T(1 − e−t/T), never to −T e−t/T alone.
Answer: (i) T ln2, (ii) −ω0/T, (iii) −ω0/(2T ln2), (iv) ω0T/2 rad per the key — my working gives ω0T/(4π) revolutions. ⚠
9A square frame is made of four uniform rods, the mass of each rod being m and its length l. Match the axis of rotation in Column-I with the moment of inertia of the frame about that axis in Column-II. Column-I: (A) frame tilted 45°, axis in the plane through the top vertex; (B) axis in the plane along one side; (C) axis in the plane along a diagonal; (D) axis perpendicular to the plane through the mid-point of one side. Column-II: (P) (2/3)ml², (Q) (5/3)ml², (R) (7/3)ml², (S) (8/3)ml². Options: (a) (A)-(S), (B)-(Q), (C)-(P), (D)-(R)   (b) (A)-(P), (B)-(Q), (C)-(R), (D)-(S)   (c) (A)-(S), (B)-(P), (C)-(Q), (D)-(R)   (d) (A)-(R), (B)-(S), (C)-(P), (D)-(Q) — (Level-7)Square frame · parallel- and perpendicular-axis bookkeepingMoment of Inertia of a Square wire for IIT-JEE and NEET Physics🟡 Similara(A)-(S), (B)-(Q), (C)-(P), (D)-(R)
Concept Build two master values first and derive everything else by shifting axes. For the frame (total mass 4m, side l) only two central moments are needed: the in-plane one and the perpendicular one. Every axis in the picture is one of these two moved sideways, so the parallel-axis theorem with the whole 4m does the rest. The perpendicular-axis theorem then shows the diagonal axis is not a new case at all — symmetry forces it to equal the side-parallel one.
Formula rod about its centre ml²/12, I = Icm + Md², Iz = Ix + Iy (planar). The frame is a plane lamina, so the perpendicular-axis theorem applies and links the in-plane axes to the out-of-plane one.
Solution
1. Central perpendicular axis: each rod gives ml²/12 + m(l/2)² = ml²/3, so I⊥,cm = 4 × ml²/3 = 4ml²/3.
2. Central in-plane axis parallel to two sides: the two parallel rods give 2 × m(l/2)² = ml²/2, the two perpendicular rods give 2 × ml²/12 = ml²/6; total I‖,cm = 2ml²/3. Rods lying along the axis contribute only their own ml²/12; rods parallel to it contribute only md².
3. Diagonal axis (C): by the perpendicular-axis theorem the two in-plane diagonals must sum to 4ml²/3, and symmetry makes them equal, so each is 2ml²/3 = (P).

Concept The table is smooth, so during the collision nothing external acts horizontally: linear momentum and angular momentum are both conserved, but kinetic energy is not, because the particle sticks. Solution

2. Linear momentum: mv = 3m vcm ⇒ vcm = v/3, in the direction of the blow.
3. Moment of inertia about the new centre: I = (2m)l²/12 + 2m(l/6)² + m(l/3)² = ml²/6 + ml²/18 + ml²/9 = ml²/3. Three pieces: the rod's own, the rod shifted by l/6, and the stuck particle at l/3.

1A solid cylinder is placed in "ends on" position on an inclined plane. It is found that at an angle θ the cylinder starts to slide. Concept The two experiments probe the same μ in two different ways. Standing on its flat end, the cylinder cannot rotate about a horizontal axis, so it is simply a block: it slips when gravity's pull along the slope beats limiting friction, which happens at tanθ = μ. Lying on its side it can roll, and rolling needs only a fraction of that friction — the rest of the gravity component goes into linear acceleration. So a rolling body tolerates a much steeper slope before its contact starts to slip, and the multiplier is fixed entirely by the moment of inertia.
Formula sliding: μ = tanθ. Rolling: required friction f = mg sinφ/(1 + mR²/I), and slipping begins when f = μmg cosφ. For a solid cylinder I = ½mR², so mR²/I = 2 and the required friction is only one third of mg sinφ.
Solution
1. End-on test: the cylinder is a block, so it starts sliding when mg sinθ = μmg cosθ ⇒ μ = tanθ. Standing on its end there is no torque axis available, so no part of the gravity load can be converted into spin.
2. Rolling test: for pure rolling, ma = mg sinφ − f and fR = I(a/R) with I = ½mR², so f = ½ma.
3. Eliminate a: mg sinφ = ma + ½ma = (3/2)ma ⇒ a = (2/3)g sinφ, hence f = mg sinφ/3.
4. Steepest rolling angle: friction cannot exceed μmg cosφ, so mg sinφ/3 ≤ μmg cosφ ⇒ tanφ = 3μ at the limit.
5. Divide: tanφ/tanθ = 3μ/μ = 3, option (c). The ratio is independent of μ itself — it is a pure moment-of-inertia number, 1 + mR²/I = 3.
6. Generalise as a check: the same argument gives 1 + mR²/I — 2 for a ring, 3 for a disc or cylinder, 3.5 for a solid sphere. The distractors 1/3 and 1/2 are simply this ratio inverted or the disc factor confused with the sphere's. The ratio must exceed 1: rolling is always the gentler demand on friction, so φ > θ always.

The magnitude of angular momentum of the projectile about the point of projection when the particle is at its maximum height h is: (a) mu³/(√2 g) (b) zero (c) (√3/2)(mu²/g) (d) (√3/16)(mu³/g)  (JEE Main 2024 Online, 30th January Morning Shift) — (Level-5)Angular momentum of a projectileAngular Momentum of a Projectile thrown from the ground, at the highest point🟡 Similard(√3/16)(mu³/g)
Concept At the top of its flight a projectile has no vertical velocity, so its momentum is purely horizontal with magnitude mu cosθ. Angular momentum about the launch point is then the product of that momentum and the perpendicular distance from the launch point to the (horizontal) line of motion — which is exactly the maximum height h. Nothing about the range is needed, because the horizontal offset is parallel to the velocity and contributes nothing to r⃗ × p⃗ — which turns a vector problem into a two-factor multiplication once h is written in terms of u.
Formula L = m v h at the apex, where v = u cosθ is horizontal. Only the component of r⃗ perpendicular to p⃗ counts, and at the apex that is the height. h = u²sin²θ/(2g). Standard maximum height, obtained from the vertical motion alone.
Solution
1. At the apex the velocity is horizontal: v = u cos30° = (√3/2)u.
2. Maximum height: h = u²sin²30°/(2g) = u²(1/4)/(2g) = u²/(8g).
3. The perpendicular distance from the point of projection to the apex velocity's line of action is h. The horizontal displacement R/2 is along the velocity, so it drops out of the cross product entirely.
4. L = m v h = m × (√3/2)u × u²/(8g) = √3 mu³/(16g) → option (d).
5. Dimension check: mu³/g is kg m²/s, correct for angular momentum, which kills option (c) whose mu²/g has the wrong dimensions. One glance removes a quarter of the option list.
6. Option (b) "zero" traps those who see the velocity is horizontal and conclude r⃗ and p⃗ are parallel — they are not, since the apex is a height h up.
7. Cross-check: integrating τ = mg × (horizontal distance) from launch to apex reproduces the same √3mu³/16g.

The hollow sphere's axis is an ordinary diameter, giving (2/3)MR². 41A thin circular disc of mass M and radius R is rotating in a horizontal plane about an axis passing through its centre and perpendicular to its plane with angular velocity ω. If another disc of same dimensions but of mass M/2 is placed gently on the first disc co-axially, then the new angular velocity of the system is : (a) (4/5)ω (b) (5/4)ω (c) (3/2)ω (d) (2/3)ω  (JEE Main 2024 Online, 8th April Evening Shift) — (Level-4)Angular momentum conservationA circular disc rotating about its axis — another disc placed on it, new angular speed🟡 Similard(2/3)ω
Concept Dropping the second disc on gently means no external torque acts about the vertical axis — the friction that brings the two discs to a common spin is internal to the system, and gravity and the normal force are both parallel to the axis and exert no torque about it. Angular momentum about that axis is therefore conserved even though kinetic energy is not — the rotational analogue of a perfectly inelastic collision — so the added inertia forces the angular speed down in exactly inverse proportion.
Formula I1ω = (I1 + I2)ω'. No external torque about the axis, so L is unchanged through the messy frictional interlude. I = ½mR² for each disc about the common axis. Same dimensions and same axis, so only the masses differ.
Solution
1. First disc: I1 = ½MR².
2. Second disc, same radius but mass M/2: I2 = ½(M/2)R² = ¼MR². Half the mass at identical radii means exactly half the moment of inertia.
3. Combined: I1 + I2 = ½MR² + ¼MR² = (3/4)MR².
4. Conservation: (½MR²)ω = ((3/4)MR²)ω', so ω' = ω(1/2)/(3/4) = (2/3)ω → option (d).
5. Shortcut: ω'/ω = M/(M + M/2) = 2/3, since ½R² is common and cancels. With identical radii the answer depends on the masses alone.
6. Energy check: KE falls to ½(3I1/2)(2ω/3)² = (2/3)½I1ω², so a third is lost to friction, as expected for an inelastic process.
7. Options (b) and (c) exceed ω, impossible when inertia is added with no torque; (a) would need the added mass to be M/4.
